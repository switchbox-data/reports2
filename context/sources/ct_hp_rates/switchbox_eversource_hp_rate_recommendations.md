# Switchbox Recommendations for Eversource Heat Pump Rate

June 2026

This document contains recommendations from Switchbox to Eversource for the design of a heat pump rate in its Connecticut service territory.

## Recommendations

1. **Delivery cost of service:** measure the true transmission and distribution cost of service for heat pump customers (for an example of this analysis, see Switchbox's NY report)
2. **Cost allocation:** determine a lower **target revenue** to collect from heat pump customers, based on their cost of service, for all delivery bill line items currently funded by volumetric charge, including distribution, transmission and public policy programs.
   a. While public policy programs don't have a cost of service standard to compare to, asking heat pump customers to fund these programs in proportion to their post-heat pump usage increase still creates a cross-subsidy. To avoid a cost-shift, the goal should be to adjust rates such that their payments into these programs are approximately the same before and after they install heat pumps.
   b. In other words, the goal should be for revenue neutrality within the residential class as a whole. Since the goal is to unwind an unfair cross-subsidy that heat pump customers are currently paying for, aiming for revenue neutrality within the heat pump customer subclass would entirely defeat the purpose of a heat pump rate.

[PDF p. 2 | printed p. 2]

3. **Rate design:** design a rate that achieves fairness by collecting the target revenue. The precise rate design can vary, though we recommend two principles: 1. simplicity (so customers understand) and 2. **all participating ratepayers' bills are lower than they would be under the default rate, holding usage constant** (to enable auto-enrollment).
4. **Auto-enrollment:** following Eversource's approach in Massachusetts, auto-enroll all known existing heat pump customers into the new rate, as well as customers that install heat pumps going forward through Energize CT, the New England Heat Pump Accelerator, etc.
5. **Minimize adoption friction** by allowing enrollment through attestation rather than requiring meter upgrades or other costly verification infrastructure, not requiring separate metering, and allowing geothermal heat pump customers and heat pump customers with gas backups to enroll.
6. **LMI discounts:** stack on existing LMI discounts rather than building income-graduation into the rate itself, which would add design complexity and risk delaying implementation when LMI discount programs already exist, and would layer well onto heat pump rates.
7. **Frame the rate as a cross-subsidy correction, not a subsidy or incentive.** In regulatory filings, the cost-of-service analysis substantiates this framing; in customer-facing materials, the emphasis should be on bill savings.

[PDF p. 3 | printed p. 3]

## Discussion

### GOAL OF HP RATE: FAIRNESS

The point of a dedicated heat pump (HP) rate is **fairness**: stop overcharging heat pump customers for electrical delivery.

The reason heat pump customers (and electric heating customers as a whole) are very likely being overcharged by Eversource is due to the utility's reliance on volumetric rates to recover fixed delivery costs (largely for existing transmission and distribution investments).

To illustrate why this happens: when a household gets air-source heat pumps, its annual electricity usage can increase by 50-150%. Its volumetric delivery payments (for distribution, transmission, and public policy) increase by the same proportion. But since these costs are fixed in the short term, and since the customer's cost of service for transmission and distribution does not increase by 50-150% after installing heat pumps (if it increases at all), this household ends up being overcharged (relative to their cost of service), which creates a cross-subsidy (or cost shift) from customers that heat with electricity to those that heat with fossil fuels.

### FIRST STEP: COST ALLOCATION

The foundation of a dedicated HP rate is therefore cost allocation: measuring the delivery cost of service of heat pump customers as a distinct group, which tells us how much revenue Eversource _should_ be collecting from them, versus how much revenue the company happens to be collecting from them under default rates.

Methodologically, this requires an **hourly cost of service study** that assigns demand-related costs to particular hours in the year—applying traditional class NCP allocators to residential subclasses will not produce an accurate picture of when costs are currently caused, which is overwhelmingly in the summer. It also requires thinking carefully about to what extent the cost causation of a customer who has just installed a heat pump reflects their _historical_ cost-causation. (Specifically, since most demand-related costs are caused during the summer, and many customers in CT gain more cooling capability after installing heat pumps, looking at their post-HP cost causation will overstate their historical cost of service, and potentially lead to overestimating their embedded cost-of-service.)

The goal of cost allocation is to determine a **revenue target**: how much revenue the utility _should_ be collecting from its existing heat pump customers (outside of the customer service charge, which would stay the same), based on their _true_ cost of service.

In terms of scope, this revenue target should include not just demand-related distribution costs (currently paid for by the distribution charge, ESI charge, and so on), but **transmission costs** as well, because heat pump customers are also being overcharged for those, relative to their cost of service.

In fact, the revenue target should include all volumetric delivery charges that collect revenue for **public policy programs** and other programs with fixed budgets. The reason is that these volumetric charges also produce a **cross-subsidy** between heat pump customers and customers that heat with fossil fuels.

To illustrate: if a customer installs a heat pump and doubles their annual electricity usage, they will also double their contribution to these programs. Under fixed budgets, if a customer pays more, others can pay less. (This cost-shifting is a mechanical consequence of volumetric cost recovery; it occurs whether or not a customer's payment into these programs can be compared to a cost-of-service standard.)

### SECOND STEP: RATE DESIGN

Any rate that collects the revenue target (no more and no less) eliminates overpayments from heat pump customers, on average. Even though rate design gets all the attention in the discussion of heat pump rates, **cost allocation is what actually achieves the fairness goal**: the simplest fair HP rate is simply to lower the existing flat volumetric delivery charges on the default residential tariff by the extent necessary to bring the annual revenues collected from heat pump customers in line with the revenue target.

The purpose of rate design, then, is to achieve **other goals**: a seasonal design with lower winter rates will lower winter bills, thereby advancing **bill stability**. A time-of-use design that reflects marginal supply and delivery costs incentivizes demand shifting, thereby advancing **economic efficiency**. And so on.

Conversely, simply adding seasonal or daily price variation to default volumetric charges, without lowering them, does not reduce overpayment from heat pump customers. When volumetric rates dominate cost recovery, making rates time-varying is not sufficient to achieve fairness. Volumetric rates must be sufficiently lowered, and/or the fixed charge sufficiently increased—which may be more viable under a tech-specific rate.

Whatever rate design is chosen, as long as the rate collects delivery revenue in line with cost-of-service, the impact on energy bills for customers switching from natural gas to heat pump should be significant.

### THIRD STEP: ENROLLMENT

For a heat pump rate to eliminate the cross-subsidy between heat pump customers and other customers, it must be adopted by the vast majority of these customers.

Opt-in rates consistently underperform, even with extensive customer marketing. Auto-enrolling all eligible customers sidesteps the adoption gap. Following the approach Eversource already took in Massachusetts, the company should work with Energize CT and any other programs that have provided heat pump installation incentives.

To avoid recreating the cross-subsidy, auto-enrollment should be paired with heat pump installation under existing incentive programs, including the New England Heat Pump Accelerator. This will also ensure that the next wave of heat pump installations lead largely to bill savings, rather than the reverse, which would fuel market transformation through positive word-of-mouth, rather than backlash through its opposite.

Finally, Eversource should avoid any rate designs, eligibility criteria, or implementation approaches that preclude or complicate auto-enrollment:

- Designs that simply lower volumetric rates guarantee bill saving for all participating ratepayers, whereas designs that create structural losers may make auto-enrollment unviable.
- Requiring separate metering or disallowing enrollment through attestation would make the perfect the enemy of the good, likewise making auto-enrollment infeasible.
- Similarly, excluding geothermal heat pump customers, or heat pump customers with gas backup, would add complexity and reduce beneficiaries for little benefit, given the small number of such customers.

[PDF p. 8 | printed p. 8]

### FOURTH STEP: EVALUATION

Since the transmission and distribution components of the heat pump rate should be based on cost-of-service, rather than an arbitrary lowering of delivery charges, they should also be reevaluated periodically based on updated cost-of-service studies that reflect existing headroom on the transmission and distribution network, as well as evolving marginal capacity costs of expanding this network.

It's important to note that, at the transmission and distribution levels, the grid won't become winter-peaking all at once: each feeder and substation will become winter-constrained at separate times, largely as a function of local EV and heat pump adoption. Therefore, there won't be a "tipping point" where the heat pump rate will suddenly come out of alignment with cost of service. Rather, it should be gradually adjusted to follow the marginal delivery costs associated with heat pump usage.

We recommend that cost-of-service studies be updated as part of each general rate case, with adjustments to the heat pump rate reflecting changes in grid headroom and marginal capacity costs. This approach avoids the need for a hard sunset date and ensures the rate remains grounded in cost-of-service as system conditions evolve.
