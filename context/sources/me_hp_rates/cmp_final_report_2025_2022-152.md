### **v. Lessons Learned and Stakeholder Feedback**

As indicated in the analyses of the Seasonal Heat Pump Rate and Electric Technology Rate, conducted by Demand Side Analytics and attached hereto, neither alternative rate is meaningfully influencing customer usage. Stakeholders discussed whether enrollment in the alternative rates should necessarily result in a change in usage behavior, or whether customers whose usage naturally aligns with the incentives provided by the rates should enjoy those incentives regardless of a measurable change in behavior. Stakeholders had mixed opinions, with some suggesting that while behavioral shifts may be the motivation for adopting alternative rates,

16

pre-existing behavior that aligns with the ultimate objective of the rate should not be penalized or otherwise suggest that the rate doesn’t provide any value. Others suggested that if those enrolled in the rate are merely taking advantage of a rate structure that aligns with their behavior, they should not receive a benefit when the purpose of the alternative rate is to shift behavior.

As discussed above, stakeholders also discussed a potential pilot peak time rebate program, where customers would be alerted of a peak event in advance of the event, and would receive some kind of monetary reward or rebate for reducing usage during that peak event. The stakeholder group considered questions such as how to determine a customer’s baseline usage (whether to look to the same week in the previous year, the previous week, a previous day or days during the same peak period), what criteria should establish when a peak demand period should be called, whether the reward should be limited to a certain amount or percentage reduction only for discrete events or from a shift from usage during broader peak periods during the rolling previous 12 months, what communications channels could be effective in alerting customers to peak periods, and how an incentive or rebate should be calculated and awarded or applied. Stakeholders also discussed whether a pilot of such a program would provide value, since other jurisdictions have adopted similar programs and could serve as reliable test cases for Maine. Finally, stakeholders discussed the scale of potential behavioral change associated with a peak time rebate program: if a behavior shift depends upon individual decision-making with fairly short notice, and depends on a shift from prior usage that may or may not be well understood by the customer, then would the behavioral shift occur or, if so, would it produce a meaningful result. Stakeholders differed on these issues. CMP is willing to continue discussions on this topic, but at this time consensus has not been reached about whether a peak time rebate program has broad support.

### **IV. Commitment Regarding Further Discussions of the Non-Firm Customer Rate Option**

The Rate Case Stipulation provided that the Collaborative Rate Design sessions may also consider a Non-Firm Customer Rate Option for commercial customers. This was first proposed

18

by the GEO as a tool to allow customers with flexible loads to potentially enable deferral of transmission and distribution upgrades. The Company agreed to collaborate with the GEO, EMT and stakeholders to investigate whether to implement non-firm service terms or related load control strategy in front of or behind the customers’ meters to potentially reduce grid upgrade costs for major new electric vehicle supply equipment installations.

With the objective of reducing grid upgrade costs for major new EV charging equipment installations in mind, CMP and the GEO met on five occasions in 2024. In addition, stakeholders discussed the concept at the January 31, 2024 meeting and the May 23, 2024 meeting. At these January and May meetings, stakeholders offering input generally expressed support for reducing grid upgrade costs for new flexible load such as EV charging stations – especially such as EV chargers at a car dealership – however, those stakeholders also felt that this objective could best be achieved through the customer’s interconnection agreement, which would be individualized to establish parameters for usage and the physical equipment or technology that could be deployed to ensure those parameters would be observed. Stakeholders offering input generally felt that tariff design, which seeks to equitably allocate costs among classes of customers, would be challenging for customers whose interconnection needs vary based both on expected usage and circuit capacity.

Nevertheless, CMP and the GEO continued discussions about options for reducing interconnection costs through interconnection agreements and physical equipment, technology, and combinations of both to allow uninterrupted service until grid conditions require curtailment for that customer. The customer would benefit from an expedited and lower cost interconnection (assuming there were upgrade costs associated with the interconnection of the customer’s new

19

load), and the system would benefit by ensuring safe, reliable, operation without the need for significant investment.

Three options emerged as most feasible. First, a centralized active network management (“ANM”) technology would enable grid operators in CMP’s energy control center (“ECC”) to monitor grid conditions and individual contributors to potential overloading, and allow CMP to send signals to participating customers to reduce usage to maintain grid stability. Second, a decentralized ANM system would work similarly, but instead of being deployed grid-wide, it would be deployed only on certain circuits. Finally, a SCADA-controlled switch could be installed at the customer’s point of interconnection, allowing CMP to open the switch and cut off power when necessary. This SCADA-controlled switch option would be the lowest cost and fastest to implement for an individual customer, but switching the customer completely off would likely be unwelcome by many customers, and there would be no economies of scale with this option, making it the most difficult to implement across the system.

# **<mark>1 INTRODUCTION</mark>**

Central Maine Power (CMP) contracted with Demand Side Analytics (DSA) to estimate the impact of rate transitions on household energy consumption for homes that enrolled in one of two new delivery rate options: the Electric Technology (ET) rate and the Seasonal Heat Pump (SHP) rate. The ET rate is an optional delivery rate that offers CMP’s residential customers a reduced volumetric charge ($/kWh) paired with a larger monthly service charge. Likewise, the SHP rate is an optional delivery rate that offers CMP’s residential customers a significantly reduced volumetric charge during the heating season (November 1<sup>st</sup> through April 31<sup>st</sup> ) paired with a larger monthly service charge and a larger volumetric charge outside of the heating season. In Section 2, we provide additional details about the rate structures and enrollment history.

The guiding research questions for this evaluation were:

**Seasonal Heat Pump Rate:** Does a seasonal change in volumetric delivery charges induce additional electric consumption during the heating season, and/or reduced electric consumption during the cooling season?

To estimate the impact of rate transition on consumption, DSA compared post-enrollment and preenrollment consumption data for enrolled accounts with consumption data for a matched comparison group consisting of homes that are on the standard residential rate (Rate A) and had the same technology as the participants. In order to isolate the effect of the rate transition on energy consumption, premises with major load modifier installations (heat pumps, heat pump water heaters, EV chargers, etc.) within a year of rate transition were removed from the analysis. Likewise, control group candidates with major load modifier installations during the analysis window were removed. The goal with this approach was to avoid confounding the impact of the rate transition with the impact of the technology adoption. After developing the matched comparison group, we used the difference-indifferences technique to estimate energy and demand impacts. Estimates were then weathernormalized using typical meteorological year (TMY3) weather conditions. We verified the robustness of the difference-in-differences estimates using fixed-effects and time-effects panel regression models.

For each analysis, Figure 1 shows the energy impacts by time of year. The orange bars represent the change in average daily kWh (positive implies an increase, negative implies a decrease). The black reference lines in the right panel separate the SHP heating and cooling seasons. The figure echoes the takeaways from Table 1 – energy impacts were negligible for ET accounts and small for SHP accounts. Although our methodological approach was to remove homes where load modifiers were installed within a year of rate transition, it’s possible that the pre/post differences in Figure 1 could be explained by load modifiers that we are not aware of, if they affected the participant and matched control group differently (e.g., adding another supplemental heat pump or a second electric vehicle).

# **<u><mark>3</mark></u>** **<mark>DATA SOURCES</mark>**

There were three primary data sets used for this analysis:

- Efficiency Maine Trust (EMT) rebate data

- Historical hourly interval data for homes enrolled on the ET or SHP rates and for a control pool of homes on Rate A

# **<u><mark>4</mark></u>** **<mark>METHODS</mark>**

The primary challenge of the impact evaluation was to accurately detect changes in energy consumption attributable to the SHP or ET rate transition while systematically eliminating other plausible explanations for the changes. Figure 10 provides an overview of our evaluation approach. Once a customer enrolls on one of the new rates, the rate is in place every day, and it is no longer possible to observe their behavior absent the new rate. Moreover, the adoption of one of these rates often occurs around the same time as the adoption of a new electric technology (heat pump, EV, etc.) that undoubtedly affects energy consumption. Thus, estimating energy and demand impacts associated with rate transition requires a control group of customers with the technologies who are not on the rate and, ideally, a year of pre-treatment and post-treatment data for both the participants and the control group. If the new rates lead to changes in energy use, we should observe a change in load patterns for customers who went on the new rates but no similar change for the control group.

Our load impacts for each rate were estimated by comparing outcomes for customers on the new rates with a matched control group using difference-in-differences. In order of importance, we outline the key evaluation challenges in the bullets below and discuss how our methodology addresses them. Additional details are provided in subsequent sections.

- **Confounding factors.** The adoption of heat pumps, heat pump water heaters, and electric vehicles may occur around the same time as enrollment in the SHP and ET rates, making it difficult to disentangle the rate impacts from the technology impacts. Our approach was to identify participants that had the qualifying technology a year before (and after) enrolling on the new rate, so the only change in the analysis period is enrollment onto the rate.

- **Identifying an appropriate control pool.** A good control has nearly identical characteristics and energy usage patterns before the intervention in question (the new rate) goes into effect. Ideally, the control group customers have the same technology (e.g., EVs, heat pumps, heat pump water heaters), are in the same geographic area, and have similar usage patterns as participants before the participant enrolled on the new rate. Our approach emphasizes identifying a high-quality, matched control group and estimating load impacts using difference-in-differences.

- **Comparable periods and balanced datasets.** While difference-in-differences is a powerful technique, it can err when the data is imbalanced (i.e., due to rolling enrollments) and when energy use patterns diverge over time (i.e., the parallel trends assumption). In our analysis, we ensured that every participant's post-intervention period has a similar pre-intervention period, and that the matched control also has the corresponding time periods. In addition, we carefully checked that the treatment and control group energy use trends do not diverge prior to the intervention.

The difference-in-differences approach mentioned above can be used to produce accurate estimates of weather-normalized load impacts associated with enrollment in the SHP or ET rates.

#### **4.3 DEVELOPING THE MATCHED COMPARISON GROUP**

Our analysis methodology relies on the use of a matched comparison group of homes that are on the standard residential rate (Rate A) but have electric technologies like heat pumps, heat pump water heaters, or electric vehicles. The comparison group helps account for exogenous factors that could affect energy consumption, like variation in supply rates and return-to-office mandates. If enrolling in the ET or SHP rate leads to changes in energy use, we should observe a change in load patterns for customers who went on these rates but no similar change for the matched comparison group.

To develop the matched comparison group, CMP provided DSA with approximately three years of interval data for a control pool of approximately 12,000 accounts. From there, our goal was to match each ET and SHP account with one control pool account based on variables like:

- Weather-normalized annual consumption (pre-enrollment) or consumption bins

- Weather sensitivity of load or sensitivity bins

- Overnight demand (to identify homes with EVs)

- Average demand during ISO-NE summer and winter peak periods

- Geographic location

- Income group (low-income or not)

#### **4.4 MODELING METHODS**

Using a difference-in-differences approach, we estimated energy impacts of the ET and SHP rate transition for each day of the year. To do this, we calculate:

(𝐸𝑛𝑟𝑜𝑙𝑙𝑒𝑑 𝐶𝑢𝑠𝑡. 𝑘𝑊ℎ𝑃𝑜𝑠𝑡 −𝐸𝑛𝑟𝑜𝑙𝑙𝑒𝑑 𝐶𝑢𝑠𝑡. 𝑘𝑊ℎ𝑃𝑟𝑒) − (𝐶𝑜𝑛𝑡𝑟𝑜𝑙 𝑘𝑊ℎ𝑃𝑜𝑠𝑡 −𝐶𝑜𝑛𝑡𝑟𝑜𝑙 𝑘𝑊ℎ𝑃𝑟𝑒)

where:

- The first difference is the pre/post change in usage for customers enrolled in the ET or SHP rate

- The second difference is the pre/post change in usage for customers in the comparison group

- Taking the difference of the two yields the daily energy impact, net of any changes observed for control group customers.

For robustness, will also developed difference-in-differences impact estimates from a time and fixed effects difference-in-differences regression model. The model specification is as follows:

𝑘𝑊ℎ𝑖,𝑡 = 𝛼𝑖 + 𝛽1𝑃𝑜𝑠𝑡𝑖,𝑡 + 𝛽2𝑇𝑟𝑒𝑎𝑡𝑚𝑒𝑛𝑡𝑖 + 𝛽3𝑇𝑟𝑒𝑎𝑡𝑚𝑒𝑛𝑡 𝑖 ∗𝑃𝑜𝑠𝑡𝑡 + 𝜀𝑖 + 𝑣𝑖 + 𝑤𝑡

where:

- 𝑖 represents individual accounts

- 𝑡 represents each time period

- 𝛽1 represents the pre/post change in usage common to both groups

- 𝛽2 is the pre-existing difference between the treatment and control group. In practice, this parameter is omitted when individual fixed effects are included.

- 𝛽3 is the difference-in-differences estimate. It is the net impact after accounting for preexisting differences between treatment and control group customers and for common differences between the pre and post periods.

- 𝑣𝑖 are individual fixed effects. These control for time-invariant unobserved customer characteristics.

- 𝑊𝑡 are time effects. These control for unobserved time effects that are common across individual customers.

Other explanatory variables, such as weather, can also be included in the model specification to reduce noise in the estimates, but the control group's usage and the use of time-effects should largely account for weather already.

#### **4.5 WEATHER-NORMALIZING THE RESULTS**

Our process to estimate weather-normalized impacts is straightforward:

1. Using the difference-in-differences approach outlined in Section 4.4, estimate daily impacts for each day of the year.

2. Develop a predictive model to estimate impacts as function of weather, day of year, and other relevant characteristics.

3. Use the model to estimate daily impacts under TMY3 conditions. The results can then be aggregated to the month, season, or year level.

**5.1.5 BILL SAVINGS**

In addition to estimating the energy and demand impacts of rate transition, we also estimated bill impacts to understand the financial impact of rate transition. We used two approaches for the bill impact calculation:

- **Shadow bill approach.** Under the shadow bill approach, we answer the question: relative to what they would have paid on rate A for the same amount of energy (delivery only), how much more/less did customers pay during their first year on the ET rate? This approach only examines

<!-- Start of picture text -->

(°°, Demand Side Analytics<br><!-- End of picture text -->

Page | 27

Internal Use

Docket No. 2022-152 Attachment 6 Page 28 of 41

the post-transition period and ignores changes in consumption patterns across the pre and post periods.

- **Difference-in-difference approach.** This approach mimics the energy and demand impact calculations. We isolate the year before and after the rate transition, perform the rate algebra, then we find (1) the pre/post difference in bills for the ET rate group, (2) the pre/post difference in bills for the matched comparison group, and (3) the difference of these two differences. Unlike the shadow bill approach, this approach does not ignore changes in consumption patterns.

The benefit to the DID approach is that it accounts for pre/post changes in consumption patterns. The benefit to the shadow bill approach is that more accounts can be included (since we do not have to remove accounts with confounding load modifiers). Results are shown in Table 9 and additional details are provided in subsequent sections. At a high level, the two approaches produced directionally similar conclusions: accounts that switched to the ET rate saved upwards of $200, on average, during their first year on the rate.

Note that the results presented in this section use the versions of the ET rate and Rate A that were in place at the time (see Table 2). The service and volumetric charges for both Rate A and the ET rate have since increased.

Table 11 shows difference-in-difference results, weather-normalized impact estimates, percent impacts, and 95% confidence intervals for the weather-normalized impacts. One possible explanation for the relatively small response to the rate is that many of these homes may have _supplemental_ heat pumps that were already being maxed out prior to rate transition rather than _whole-home_ heat pumps that were being underutilized. In other words, the rate response is partially a function of heat pump utilization prior to rate transition.

Although our analysis approach was to remove premises with major load modifier installations within one year of rate transition, it is possible that the pre/post change in electric consumption could be explained by load modifiers we are unaware of (e.g., an additional supplementary heat pump, converting domestic hot water from nonelectric to electric) rather than behavioral changes among the SHP premises.

##### **5.2.5 BILL SAVINGS**

In addition to estimating the energy and demand impacts of rate transition, we also estimated bill impacts to understand the financial impact of rate transition. We used two approaches for the bill impact calculation:

- **Shadow bill approach.** Under the shadow bill approach, we answer the question: relative to what they would have paid on rate A for the same amount of energy (delivery only), how much more/less did customers pay during their first year on the SHP rate? This approach only examines the post-transition period and ignores changes in consumption patterns across the pre and post periods.

- **Difference-in-difference approach.** This approach mimics the energy and demand impact calculations. We isolate the year before and after the rate transition, perform the rate algebra, then we find (1) the pre/post difference in bills for the SHP rate group, (2) the pre/post difference in bills for the matched comparison group, and (3) the difference of these two differences. Unlike the shadow bill approach, this approach does not ignore changes in consumption patterns.

The benefit to the DID approach is that it accounts for pre/post changes in consumption patterns. The benefit to the shadow bill approach is that more accounts can be included (since we do not have to remove accounts with confounding load modifiers). Results are shown in Table 13 and additional details are provided in subsequent sections. At a high level, the two approaches produced directionally similar conclusions: accounts that switched to the SHP rate spent more money, on average, than they would have spent in a baseline scenario where they did not changed rates.

Note that the results presented in this section use the versions of the SHP rate and Rate A that were in place at the time (see Table 3). The service and volumetric charges for both Rate A and the SHP rate have since changed (see Table 14). The volumetric charge for Rate A increased, but the seasonal volumetric charges for the SHP rate decreased. The service charge for Rate A increased by about $3 per cycle, and the service charge for the SHP rate increased by less than one dollar per cycle. Using the current rate structures for this analysis paints a different picture. In this alternative scenario, the shadow bill approach results in estimated first-year savings (as opposed to an increase) of $251 and the DID approach results in estimated first-year savings of $184. The gulf between these numbers seems large, but the calculations are performed on different sets of customers (the DID approach isolates customers without confounding load modifiers).

##### **5.2.5.2 Difference-in-Differences**

# **<mark>6 CONCLUSION</mark>**

Key findings from the analysis include:

- We did not find a statistically significant increase or decrease in average annual consumption or seasonal consumption following enrollment on the ET. For the SHP rate, we found relatively small but significant increases in post-enrollment annual electric consumption (kWh = 158, percent impact = 1.3%, p-value = 0.01) and post-enrollment heating season electric consumption (kWh = 114, percent impact = 1.4%, p-value = 0.01).

  - Figure 38 shows average daily post-enrollment participant consumption (blue), baseline consumption (gray), and daily impacts (orange) for the ET analysis. Post-enrollment consumption mirrored pre-enrollment consumption.

  - Figure 39 shows the same metrics for the SHP analysis; the black reference lines in the figure separate the rate’s heating and cooling seasons.

  - Although our analysis approach was to remove premises with major load modifier installations within one year of rate transition, it is possible that the pre/post change in electric consumption for the SHP group could be explained by load modifiers we are unaware of (e.g., an additional supplementary heat pump, converting domestic hot water from non-electric to electric) rather than behavioral responses to the rate.

- We did not find statistically significant increases or decreases in average demand during the summer peak period or the winter peak period (as defined by ISO-NE) following ET or SHP rate transition.

- Accounts that moved from Rate A to the ET saved money on their energy bills (relative to a baseline scenario where the ET accounts did not change rates), while accounts that moved from Rate A to the SHP saw bill increases (relative to a baseline scenario where the SHP accounts did not change rates). The bill impact calculations were performed using the rates that were in effect during the analysis timeframe, but all three rates (Rate A, ET, and SHP) have since changed. For SHP accounts in particular, the current rate structure is more favorable to enrollees than it was when this research began (see Table 14).
