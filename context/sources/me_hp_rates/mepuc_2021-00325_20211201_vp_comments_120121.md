Versant Power is filing in this docket comprehensive proposals for EV rates, energy storage end uses rates, and heat pump rates, including rates for residential EV charging as well as for public chargers, energy-storage related end uses for home as well a commercial and industrial uses, and heat pump, space and water heating end uses. The Company takes this approach to updating its customer pricing here and will do so in other dockets, including in Docket No. 2021-00198 related to T&D Rate Design for EVs in which Versant Power has filed similar and consistent proposals.

The Company is taking a broader view of updating its rate designs in the interim-short term and longer term than the specific rate mandates of each docket. Moreover, Versant Power wishes to make its thinking, proposals, and approach transparent to the Commission, its customers and stakeholders given the import of various dockets – and customer feedback – to Maine’s ability to meet the State’s greenhouse gas, clean energy, and related energy goals. Versant Power views moving forward with our customers to design rates that both work for our customers and are built on solid rate design principals to be the most efficient and likely most

successful path for long-term customer and Company transition together to meeting Maine’s Greenhouse Gas and clean energy goals.

The enactment of multiple Legislative mandates for the Commission and Maine’s T&D utilities precipitated an ongoing Company examination of its rate design for multiple categories of rate customer classes and uses. These mandates coincide with Versant Power’s investments in advanced metering infrastructure (“AMI”) and meter data management system (“MDMS”). Because of the Legislative mandates and Versant Power’s upcoming improved capabilities, the Company is considering how it can offer rates which are more dynamic and responsive to emerging customer uses and needs.[^1] Enabling customer adoption of advanced end-use technologies, whether to save customers money or to use energy as they choose, is a set of capabilities that Versant Power is committed to building within its systems. The transition to enabling higher levels of grid-scale and distributed renewables on Versant’s system is a substantial one and Versant views rate design as one of the critical tools to enabling these transitions in a cost-effective manner for our customers and efficient manner for the Company.

The capabilities of AMI and related MDMS offer the Company the opportunity to provide customers with new rate options. Versant Power proposes making time of use (TOU) residential rates available to its Maine Public District (MPD) customers for the first time based on the 2022-2023 deployment of AMI to the MPD customers. New rates for residential and small business customers for EVs and energy storage, including small business customers, will be possible with the deployment of the AMI and upgraded MDMS changes in 2022 and into 2023 for northern Maine.

For customer understandability and communications, Versant Power will endeavor to provide similar and consistent, if not identical rate designs and structures, for its MPD and Bangor Hydro District (BHD) customers. Because these new rates—like new technologies from EV to electric storage batteries, and other distributed energy resources—are new to customers, the Company proposes maintaining similar and consistent rate designs across both of its service territories and across multiple Commission dockets. Accordingly, Versant Power anticipates offering rates on a similar and consistent basis in other Commission dockets, adjusting them based on feedback from the Commission, customers, and stakeholders.

Versant Power is, of course, proposing to follow appropriate rate design principles. While the Commission has not articulated advanced rate design principles, the Company notes that the New Hampshire Public Utilities Commission (New Hampshire Commission) has provided its electric distribution companies (EDCs) with guidance and a similar order asking for filing of EV rates for commercial and residential charging. The New Hampshire Commission set forth that its utilities should apply the following principles for EV rates:

a) Rates should be based directly on cost causation.

b) Rates should incorporate time-varying energy supply, transmission, and distribution components.

c) Rates should have three periods (e.g., off-peak, mid-peak, and peak).

d) Rates should be seasonally differentiated (e.g., summer and winter).

e) Rates should have an average price differential between off-peak and peak of no less than 3:1 in an annual yearly average (not necessarily for each season).

f) Rates should have a peak period of no longer than five hours in duration.[^2] Versant Power is therefore aware of these NH EV rate design principles.

Versant Power also puts for the following additional rate design principles:

g) consistency with established rate designs,

[^2]: New Hampshire Commission, August 2020 Order 26,394, web reference: https://www.puc.nh.gov/Regulatory/Docketbk/2020/20-004/ORDERS/20-004_2020-08-18_ORDER_26394.PDF

<!-- PDF page 6 (printed page 6) -->

h) offering rates that allow customers to make informed decisions in selecting among other electricity and energy pricing options, therefore rates that customers can understand,

i) rates that offer customers meaningful control of their energy usage and costs, and,

j) rates that align customer pricing signals to grid costs for different elements of electricity service. The last principle is important for a modern grid: aligning customer pricing signals to the Company’s actual grid cost, as well as standard offer supply service, for each element of electricity service. The marginal cost of distribution, transmission, capacity, and supply may tend to move together around times of peak summer and winter usage but will also be distinct. Properly assigning costs to periods when customers will see savings from shifting electricity usage to the same times—when marginal grid service costs are low—will save all Versant Power customers money. Flexible end-use technologies such as EV charging, energy storage and even programmable heat pumps offer customer new flexibility to shift and modify usage patterns previously only available through programmable thermostats.

The carbon footprint of electricity in Maine is undoubtedly smaller than any other source of heating and transportation fuel. By providing pricing options which align grid customer with customer costs for customer wishing to adopt new end use electricity technologies, Versant Power anticipates reducing the cost of Maine’s transition to a net zero carbon emissions economy while facilitating Maine’s transition to beneficial electrification so as to be cost-advantageous for its customers, both those who adopt EVs, energy-storage, and heat-pumps and those who do not.

### C. Equity in Versant Power’s Existing Pricing and Current Residential Electric Thermal (ET) Rates

Versant Power’s current ET rate structure incorporates equity and minimal service considerations for low-and moderate-income customers in three regards.

First, a minimal amount of electricity, 100 kWh is included in the minimal monthly distribution charge. That amount while not much, is enough to keep a furnace operating, a refrigerator going and some lights or medical equipment operating on electricity. It is truly minimal. It is not sufficient to ET uses or any advanced end-uses it does allow service to stay involving minimal usage at a minimal monthly price.

Second, the minimum service charge of $7.56 for the Company’s BHD and $9.85 for the Company’s MPD are relatively low. These charges are lower than other IOUs minimum charges in Maine and among the lowest minimum service charges in the region. Maintaining a low minimum monthly charge allows customers to maintain service at a low minimal bill representing the minimal costs of maintaining a metering, billing, and customer services. If a customer is able to heat with wood for example, this minimum charge and usage allows for some lights and a refrigerator or other equipment to stay on, water to be pumped from a well and other minimal usage.

Third, the residential heating rates described above allow the same discount for electrical thermal resistance heating, an admittedly older and less efficient form of ET. While electrical resistance space heating is far less than heat pump space heating technologies, it can be the

<!-- PDF page 25 (printed page 25) -->

current heating in moderate income housing. Hopefully, residences with electrical resistance heating will have more efficient heating systems such as heat-pump technology replace older resistance heating systems. The economics suggest a payback to make system replacement economic for many residential customers. However, the capital investments for new heat-pump technologies are an impediment for many moderate income customers to convert despite assistance from the Efficiency Maine Trust (EMT). In the meantime, these ET rates allow low-and moderate-income (LMI) customers a rate discount for maintaining electrical heating when usage exceeds 600 kWhs in the Company’s MPD and 700 kWhs in the Company’s BHD. As Maine transitions to more efficient heating systems and provides help for LMI customers to convert to more efficient ET space heating, the amount of electrical resistance heating decreases. Nonetheless, this rate provides an equal measure of equity to provide the same level discount to customers with less efficient ET systems as to customers who can convert more readily while Maine undertakes this transition.

While none of these residential rate discounts is capped, Versant Power raises a question of whether the discount should be capped as a reasonably high level of electricity usage for a Maine household. A monthly cap for example based on usage data for heating relying on ET technologies could be set in the range of a reasonable upper limit for heating a reasonably sized Maine residence in the Maine winter with advanced ET technologies? Further, upon a customer showing that it has older electrical resistance heating and qualifies for LMI assistance, a higher monthly cap based on electric resistance heating systems usage to heat a reasonably sized Maine residence in a Maine winter could be put in place for specific customer accounts?

1. Rate Design: Rate design is often and traditionally undertaken in rate cases. For this reason, Versant Power respectfully suggests the Commission consider establishing or incorporating another customer-focused process to focus on challenges and opportunities to adjust rate design for EVs.

2. Focused Adjudicatory Docket: The Commission can establish a focused adjudicatory docket, as here, to home in on development of advanced rates for different purposes. For adoption of marginal changes to existing rates and short-term modification, this process can be effective. For these reasons, other Commissions have opened their process to broader stakeholder and public processes through non-adjudicatory mechanisms.

3. Staff-Facilitated Non-Adjudicatory Docket: The Commission may initiate a non-adjudicatory docket to convene stakeholders. Commission Staff can act as a facilitator or convenor of such discussions, particularly Staff with training to moderate and facilitate stakeholder discussions. If Commission Staff have the training, facilitation expertise, and hours to allow them to facilitate such a process, Versant Power submits that this would be preferable to a formal adjudicatory process for consideration of new rate offerings that will influence the efficiency and speed with which Maine will be able to achieve its transportation related Greenhouse Gas reduction mandates. **Comments**

**1. The capability of the systems (and related systems, e.g., for wholesale market load** **settlement) to reliably offer time‐differentiated rates for delivery and supply of energy.**

Versant Power’s plan is to implement more sophisticated time-differentiated rates based on Commission direction, customer feedback and the improving capability of the Company’s metering, data management, and billing systems. Today, Versant Power’s systems have limited capability to provide time-differentiated rates. The Company is investing in modern, flexible, and robust metering

<!-- PDF page 65 (no printed page number) -->

and data management technology integration to be able to offer customers services and products requiring more granular data management.

With our current systems, the company’s billing systems can be programmed for time of use (TOU) rates, and Versant Power offers customers TOU delivery rates based on set peak, shoulder, and peak pricing. Suppliers do not offer TOU energy rates in our service territories to the best of our knowledge.[^9]

For those few customers who have selected a TOU delivery rate in the Company’s Bangor Hydro District (BHD), a meter with specific configurations is required. The meter must have the capability to be programmed with pre-defined TOU periods so that the meter can pass that energy values back as peak reads (for both demand and energy) so that the billing system can “see” this information and apply the associated rate. For example, the BHD TOU periods are defined as Peak, Shoulder, and Off-Peak.[^10] Versant Power must program the meter with these TOU registers. The read data for each of the three time periods is then communicated back to the Company’s billing system. The billing system performs the calculation using that meter’s data multiplied by the unique rates for each of the time periods. The nuance here is that the customer billing system is not interpreting each hourly read, rather that is done through meter programming. Therefore, any customer who wishes to switch to a TOU rate needs to have the Company to replace the existing meter with one specifically configured to accommodate TOU rates.

**3. The capability of the systems to accommodate a smart grid and provide smart grid** **function as defined in Title 35‐A, section 3143.**

The scope and breadth of the smart grid functions as defined in Title 35-A, §3143 is significant, and there will need to be a series of investments, upgrades and design decisions made over the next few years and beyond to fully accommodate the entire functionalities envisioned. A few of the notable smart grid components outlined in the legislations are: • The ability for customers to “manage and adjust their electricity consumption or to generate and store electricity” • “using modern technology to increase reliability and reduce costs” • “Increased use of digital information and control technology to improve the reliability, security and efficiency of the electric system” • “Deployment and integration into the electric system of renewable capacity resources” • “Deployment and integration ... of demand response technologies, demand-side resources and energy-efficient resources” • “Deployment of smart grid technologies, including real-time, automated, interactive technologies that optimize the physical operation of energy consuming appliances and devices, for purposes of metering, communications concerning grid operation and status and distribution system operations” • “Deployment and integration into the electric system of advanced electric storage and peak-reduction technologies, including plug-in electric and hybrid electric vehicles” • “Provision to consumers of timely energy consumption information and control options” • “Consumer education – if a transmission and distribution utility ... implements smart grid functions, the utility ... shall, to the extent the commission determines appropriate, provide information to customers about the purpose and goals of smart grid functions, the ways in which smart grid functions, including but not limited to time-of-use pricing, may involve customer interaction and how the implementation of smart grid functions can benefit customers.” Versant Power’s strategy in this area is emerging, however there is a series of technical infrastructure investments that will form the foundation for advanced metering and advanced rate designs. AMI, including the smart meters and the back-end systems to collect and manage the data, is the foundation. The Company believes that building in flexibility will be key. Flexibility can be achieved by collecting and storing the detailed interval data information.
