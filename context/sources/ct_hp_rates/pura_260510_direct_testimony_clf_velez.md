## {8} II. SUMMARY AND RECOMMENDATIONS

{9} **Q. Please summarize your findings to the Authority.**

{10} **A.** I demonstrate that the volumetric rates associated with the Company’s default residential
{11} tariff (Rate 1) are overcharging heat pump customers relative to costs they cause when adopting
{12} a heat pump. This creates a substantial cross-subsidy _within_ the residential customer class, which
{13} flows from electric customers that heat with heat pumps to those that heat with fossil fuels.
{14} To establish this, I provide evidence that CL&P’s electric customers with heat pumps are
{15} overcharged by an average of \$1,040 per year on distribution and transmission — far in excess
{16} of any marginal infrastructure costs their heat pumps actually cause.

{17} Charging heat pump customers far in excess of their cost-causation has two serious
{18} consequences: first, it violates intra-class equity, one of the key principles of cost-of-service
{19} ratemaking. Second, it undermines the state’s ambitious building policy goals: as a direct result
{20} of being overcharged, only 57% of CL&P’s electricity customers that heat with fossil fuels can
{21} currently lower their energy bills by switching to heat pumps.

{22} To stop overcharging heat pump customers, I propose that the Company create a new rate
{23} schedule for heat pump customers, Rate HP, with lower volumetric rates for distribution and

[PDF p. 8 | printed p. 6]

{1} transmission during the winter months. The purpose of lowering these rates is to decrease the
{2} total revenue collected from heat pump customers for distribution and transmission, in order to
{3} bring the incremental revenue collected from customers installing heat pumps in line with the
{4} incremental marginal costs they cause.

{5} By better aligning rates for heat pump customers with their actual cost-causation, this
{6} proposal would eliminate the significant cross-subsidy that exists between heat pump and fossil-
{7} heating electric residential customers, thus making the Company’s rates more fair. As our
{8} analysis shows, this proposal would also make it cheaper to heat with heat pumps than fossil
{9} fuels for 83% of Connecticut households, thus eliminating a key obstacle to the state’s building
{10} electrification goals.

{11} **Q. What is the principle of cost causation?**

{12} **A.** Cost causation is a foundational principle of sound rate design: a utility’s costs should be
{13} borne by the customers on whose behalf the costs are incurred.[^7] In other words, if a customer’s
{14} usage causes a cost, that customer should be charged for it — and if it does not, they should not
{15} be charged as though it does.

{17} **consistent with the cost-causation principle?**

{18} **A.** Yes. When fossil fuel-heated residential customers install heat pumps, their annual electricity
{19} consumption increases by an average of 6,432 kWh. Every one of those new kWh requires the

{7} **Q. Is the bill increase that customers pay for distribution and transmission after installing**
{8} **heat pumps consistent with the cost-causation principle?**

{9} **A.** No. The Company is currently overcharging heat pump customers for distribution and
{10} transmission costs. I will demonstrate this in the following questions.

{11} **Q. You testified that every kWh of electricity consumption causes generation costs. Does**
{12} **every kWh also cause transmission and distribution (T&D) costs?**

{13} **A.** No.

{14} For generation, every kWh a customer consumes causes a generation cost: someone has
{15} to generate the electricity that customer is consuming, and the Company has to pay for it. This is
{16} true at every hour of the year — a kWh consumed at 3 a.m. in February costs money to generate,
{17} as does a kWh consumed at 5 p.m. in August.

{18} In terms of cost causation, transmission and distribution are fundamentally different from
{19} generation: very few of the kWh a customer consumes causes a new (or marginal) T&D cost.
{20} Distribution and transmission are networks of existing infrastructure that deliver electricity to
{21} customers. When a customer uses electricity, it flows over this existing infrastructure. The vast
{22} majority of the time, this flow _causes no costs_: the infrastructure is already there, and it has the
{23} capacity to deliver the load, without the need for capacity upgrades. (And while this existing

[PDF p. 16 | printed p. 14]

{1} infrastructure does need to be paid for, those are _sunk_ costs tied to old investments, not _marginal_
{2} costs, or costs caused by customer usage in the present.)

{3} So when does customer usage actually cause new distribution and transmission costs?
{4} When the grid is **constrained**: during the small number of peak hours that a customer’s demand
{5} pushes their local distribution feeder/substation, or the upstream bulk transmission system, near
{6} its capacity limits. This triggers the need for capacity upgrades — larger transformers,
{7} reconductored feeders, etc. — whose costs, according to the cost-causation principle, should be
{8} borne by the customers who caused them.

{9} Bottom line: unlike generation, only usage during **peak hours** causes marginal T&D
{10} costs to be incurred by the Company. Usage outside of peak hours simply flows over existing
{11} infrastructure, without triggering any new investment.

{9} **Q. What is a cross-subsidy, and how do volumetric rates create one within the residential**
{10} **customer class?**

{11} **A.** A cross-subsidy within a rate class arises when one identifiable group of customers
{12} systematically pays more than their cost of service, and that excess revenue allows the customers
{13} of another identifiable group to pay less than their cost of service.

{14} The mechanism is straightforward: when fixed costs — like the embedded costs of the
{15} transmission and distribution system — are predominantly recovered through volumetric
{16} charges, customers with higher-than-average usage pay a greater share of those fixed costs,
{17} effectively subsidizing the customers with lower-than-average usage.[^23]

Bonbright explains that a uniform per-kWh rate, “in treating the total cost of the business as if it varied directly with changes in the kilowatt-hour output of energy — a grossly false assumption — violates the most widely accepted canon of fair pricing, the principle of service at cost,” because many costs (capacity and customer costs) are not a function of kWh output. James C. Bonbright, _Principles of Public Utility Rates_ (Columbia Univ. Press, 1961), at 304–305, 345–346 (Ch. XVI and XVIII). The NARUC cost allocation manual establishes the foundational framework, classifying transmission and distribution costs as primarily **demand-related** (varying with kW peak demand, not kWh consumption), so that recovering them through volumetric energy charges violates cost-causation principles. NARUC, _Electric Utility Cost Allocation Manual_ (1992), Ch. 2 §V, Ch. 6. Most directly, the RAP manual identifies this as “essentially a question of a potential intraclass cross-subsidy between types of residential customers” — specifically between electric-heating and non-heating customers — and notes that “seasonally differentiated rates, if based appropriately on cost causation, can achieve the same distributional impact as separate rate classes.” Jim [PDF p. 30 | printed p. 28] Lazar, Paul Chernick & William Marcus, _Electric Cost Allocation for a New Era: A Manual_ (RAP, 2020), §5.2; _see also_ §6.1.2, Ch. 28.

    {2} recovery creates cross-subsidies, noting that current rate structure produces “existing cross-
    {3} subsidies within the class (higher usage customers disproportionally paying more than cost of
    {4} service)” and recommending that the Company “reduce the over-reliance of fixed distribution
    {5} cost recovery through usage charges” in order to “increase equity across customers within the
    {6} class.”[^24] (We address the relationship between intra-class cross-subsidies and intra-class equity

## {11} VIII. RATE 6’S BLOCK STRUCTURE IS REGRESSIVE

{12} **Q. Besides Rate 6’s discount being too low, what problems does the rate’s block structure**
{13} **create?**

{14} **A.** Targeting lower rates directly to incremental heat pump load is a laudable goal, and a block
{15} rate structure would appear to be a natural way to do it. But the design rests on an implicit
{16} assumption: that every customer’s pre-heat-pump electricity use is approximately 700 kWh per
{17} month, so that kWh above the threshold can serve as a proxy for incremental heat pump load. If
{18} that assumption held, the design would work — the threshold would neatly separate base load
{19} from heat pump load for everyone.

{20} But in reality, the Company’s fossil fuel-heated customers vary far too widely in their
{21} pre-heat-pump electricity use for a single cutoff to work. When a customer’s base load deviates
{22} from the threshold, the discount is misallocated in one of two ways:

[PDF p. 70 | printed p. 68]

1. {1} **Discount leakage**: when a customer’s base load is _below_ the threshold, some of their
   {2} incremental heat pump electricity lands in Block 1 at the full rate, and they receive less
   {3} discount than intended.

2. {4} **Windfall discount**: when a customer’s base load is _above_ the threshold, some of their
   {5} pre-existing consumption — unrelated to the heat pump — gets the Block 2 discount as
   {6} an unintended windfall.
   {7} These **misallocations** are not specific to the 700 kWh threshold — they are inherent to

{8} any block design with a fixed cutoff applied to a heterogeneous population.

{9} And as I will show, these misallocations produce two concrete **harms**: 1. the block
{10} design is regressive, because lower-income customers have lower base loads; and 2. it is
{11} systematically biased against natural gas customers, who also have lower base loads. (The
{12} seasonal rate I propose in Section X avoids these two harms entirely, and ultimately does a better
{13} job of delivering a discount that is proportional to each customer’s incremental heat pump load.)

{14} I will address each of these harms in turn, after providing examples of the misallocations.

{15} **Q. Can you provide examples to illustrate discount leakage and windfall discount, the two**
{16} **types of discount misallocations introduced above?**

{17} **A.** Consider two delivered fuel homes, both of which switch to a heat pump and add the same
{18} 500 kWh per month of incremental winter electricity.

{19} **Discount leakage.** Home A uses 400 kWh per month before the heat pump — well
{20} below the threshold. After the retrofit, it uses 900 kWh. Under Rate 6, the first 700 kWh is billed
{21} at the Block 1 rate (13.6 ¢/kWh) and only the last 200 kWh gets the Block 2 rate (11.7 ¢/kWh).
{22} Of the home’s 500 kWh of heat pump load, only 200 kWh — 40% — lands in Block 2. The

[PDF p. 71 | printed p. 69]

{1} other 300 kWh is billed at the full Block 1 rate. The discount _leaks_ — it fails to reach most of the
{2} incremental heat pump load it was designed for.

{3} **Windfall discount.** Home B uses 900 kWh before the heat pump — well above the
{4} threshold. After the retrofit, it uses 1,400 kWh. Under Rate 6, the first 700 kWh is again Block 1,
{5} and the remaining 700 kWh gets Block 2. All 500 kWh of heat pump load lands in Block 2, but
{6} Home B also gets the Block 2 discount on 200 kWh of its pre-existing base load. That is a
{7} _windfall_ — a discount on electricity that has nothing to do with the heat pump.

{8} The threshold treats both homes the same, but the economic outcomes are very different:
{9} Home A receives 1.9 ¢/kWh of discount on only 40% of its heat pump load, while Home B
{10} receives that discount on 100% of its heat pump load _plus_ an additional windfall on existing
{11} consumption.

{3} **Q. Does Rate 6’s block design have a regressive impact?**

{4} **A.** Yes. A rate design feature is regressive when it delivers a larger benefit to higher-income
{5} customers than to lower-income customers. In this case, the benefit is a **proportional bill**
{6} **discount**: Rate 6 aims to give all delivered fuel customers a 14.1% discount on their heat pump
{7} bill, because it lowers the all-in Block 2 rate by 14.1%. But Rate 6 does not deliver proportional
{8} bill discounts to all customers. Instead, the percent reduction in incremental heat pump bills
{9} increases with income — 8.0% for the lowest-income third of delivered fuel homes, 13.8% for
{10} the middle third, and 15.9% for the highest-income third — against a design target of 14.1% for
{11} all.

{12} Ultimately, the reason Rate 6’s block design is regressive is straightforward: higher-
{13} income homes have higher base loads, which places them above the 700 kWh threshold; lower-
{14} income homes have lower base loads, which keeps them below it. The threshold converts the
{15} correlation between income and base load into a correlation between income and savings.

{16} **Q. Could Rate 6’s regressive impact be avoided by choosing a different threshold?**

{17} **A.** No. Regressivity is an inherent problem of _any_ block rate with a one-size-fits-all threshold,
{18} not just 700 kWh.

{19} Moving the threshold lower would reduce leakage but increase windfall; moving it higher
{20} would do the reverse. In fact, 700 kWh is close to the threshold that minimizes total
{21} misallocation.[^68]

[^68]: This is because 700 kWh is near the mean base load of delivered fuel homes, and at that point leakage and windfall are roughly equal.

[PDF p. 76 | printed p. 74]

{1} But even when misallocations are minimized, they are far from eliminated, because base
{2} loads vary so widely across homes. And the fact that this variation in base load is correlated with
{3} income ensures that lower-income people will tend to get discount leaks, and higher-income
{4} people will tend to get windfalls — even if the “best” threshold is chosen.

{5} **Q. In Section VII, you showed that Rate 6’s discount is far smaller than it could be. If the**
{6} **discount were set at the marginal cost floor (as in modified Rate 6), would the regressive**
{7} **impact of the block design go away — since all customers would be getting a much larger**
{8} **benefit?**

{9} **A.** No — it gets worse. As I showed above, the threshold systematically gives low-income
{10} homes only 57% of their intended savings, while high-income homes get 112%.[^69] These
{11} _percentages_ arise from the threshold, and do not change with the discount size. However, a
{12} bigger discount applied through the same percentages produces a bigger gap in what customers
{13} actually receive.[^70]

{14} Under Rate 6 as proposed, the Block 2 discount is 1.9 ¢/kWh, or 14% of the Block 1 rate.
{15} As I showed above, low-income homes see an effective bill reduction of only 8%, while high-
{16} income homes see 16% — a 8 percentage-point gap, driven entirely by the threshold’s inability
{17} to consistently separate base load from heat pump load.

[^69]: A customer’s “intended savings” is what they would receive if the threshold perfectly separated their base load from their heat pump load — that is, if every kWh of incremental HP consumption landed in Block 2 and none of their base load did. The ratio shown (e.g. 57% for low-income homes) is each customers’ actual savings divided by their intended savings, averaged across the customers in an income group. A home with mostly leakage might capture only 20%; a home with a large windfall might capture 150%. The group average tells you whether the threshold delivers the right amount of savings to that population on balance — and for low-income homes, even after including homes that receive windfalls, the answer is barely more than half.

[^70]: The arithmetic: low-income homes capture 57% of their intended savings, so their effective bill reduction is 57% × 72% = 41%. High-income homes capture 112%, so theirs is 112% × 72% = 81%. The same ratios that produced an 8 percentage-point gap under a 14% discount produce a 40 percentage-point gap under a 72% discount.

[PDF p. 77 | printed p. 75]

{1} Under the modified Rate 6 I presented in Section VII — with Block 2 rates for upstream
{2} distribution, local distribution, and transmission all set at marginal cost — the discount grows to
{3} 13.4 ¢/kWh, or 72% of the Block 1 rate. If the threshold worked perfectly, every customer’s
{4} incremental heat pump bill would fall by 72%. But the same threshold imprecision applies: low-
{5} income homes would see only a 41% reduction, while high-income homes would see 81% — a
{6} 40 percentage-point gap.[^71]

{7} In other words, fixing Problem 1 (the discount being too small) without fixing Problem 2
{8} (the block structure misallocating the discount) actually makes the regressivity _five times worse_
{9} in absolute terms.

{13} **Q. Is it possible to design a block rate that avoids these biases?**

{14} **A.** Yes, by moving to per-customer thresholds, rather than a single residential class-wide
{15} number. There are at least three ways to implement per-customer thresholds.
{16} The most accurate would be **separately metering** the heat pump. In addition to the cost, this
{17} approach would add significant friction to customer enrollment, and rule out auto-enrollment
{18} entirely, of both existing and new heat pump customers. (As I will discuss in Section XII, auto-
{19} enrollment is essential to the success of the rate.)

{20} Another option would be **circuit-level energy monitors** such as Sense or Emporia Vue
{21} (often called non-invasive submeters, in regulatory contexts). These would be lower cost, and
{22} may be compatible with auto-enrollment of new heat pump customers: contractors installing heat
{23} pumps under EnergizeCT already interact with each home, and opt-out energy monitor

{4} integrate into the Company’s billing software. Savings would need to be delivered as credits
{5} administered by a third party, rather than lower rates on the customer’s bill.[^72]

{6} The cheapest and most frictionless option would be a **software-based solution**. The
{7} Company could estimate per-customer thresholds from their meter data: for instance, by
{8} averaging each calendar month’s consumption over the past 3–5 years (the average of January
{9} 2024, January 2025, and January 2026 becomes the January threshold, and so on). The Company
{10} would then subtract the threshold from each month’s post-installation consumption to estimate
{11} incremental heat pump load, apply the Block 2 rate to that load, and generate the bill. This could
{12} be done within the billing system itself — Rate 6 already uses a block threshold as a billing
{13} determinant, and making that determinant customer-specific rather than class-wide is standard
{14} functionality in utility billing platforms.[^73] Alternatively, a third party could perform the baseline
{15} estimation and load separation, and deliver the resulting savings as a credit outside the bill.

{16} All three approaches add complexity — whether in hardware, software, or administration.
{17} The first two make auto-enrollment difficult to impossible, which would lead to single-digit rate
{18} enrollment and leave the cross-subsidy largely in place.

{7} winter, without relying on a block threshold at all. I present exactly such a rate in the next
{8} section.

## {9} X. PROPOSED RATE DESIGN: RATE HP

{10} **Q. What do you propose to address the overcharging of heat pump customers?**

{11} **A.** I recommend that the Company create a new rate schedule, Rate HP, for residential customers
{12} with heat pumps. Rather than using a block design, Rate HP would lower the volumetric rates for
{13} distribution and transmission during the winter months (November through March), when the
{14} incremental heat pump load occurs. Rate 1’s summer rates, generation rates, and the fixed
{15} customer charge would be **unchanged**. All customers with heat pumps would be eligible to
{16} enroll in Rate HP, regardless of whatever heating system they had before.

{17} **Q. How is Rate HP intended to affect revenue collection from heat pump customers?**

{18} **A.** The purpose of lowering winter rates is to bring the incremental revenue the Company
{19} collects from customers installing heat pumps in line with the incremental marginal costs those
{20} heat pumps actually cause (while preserving the revenue previously collected from their base
{21} load). As I showed in Section III, heat pump customers are overcharged because volumetric rates
{22} charge far more per incremental kWh than the incremental marginal T&D cost those kWh cause.
{23} Rate HP corrects this by reducing volumetric delivery rates in winter, the season when

[PDF p. 84 | printed p. 82]

{1} incremental heat pump load is concentrated, in order to achieve bills consistent with cost-
{2} causation: that is, bills that get as close possible to each customer’s pre-heat pump T&D bill, plus
{3} their incremental marginal T&D cost caused by the heat pump.

{4} **Q. What is the ultimate objective of Rate HP?**

{5} **A.** By removing the overcharge for heat pump customers (on average), the ultimate objective of
{6} Rate HP is to fix the intra-class equity and building electrification policy problems that Rate 1
{7} creates (as documented in Section III and IV), and which Rate 6 fails to satisfactorily address (as
{8} documented in Sections V through IX).

## {12} XII. ENROLLMENT: HOW CUSTOMERS GET ON TO RATE HP

{13} **Q. How would customers be enrolled in the proposed Rate HP?**

{14} **A.** I recommend two enrollment channels, both automatic.

[^81]: Same methodology as Figure 37; delivered fuel bills use EIA residential heating oil and propane prices.

[PDF p. 95 | printed p. 93]

{1} **Every new customer that installs a heat pump** should be auto-enrolled in Rate HP at
{2} the time of installation. To achieve this, every customer receiving a heat pump incentive through
{3} Energize CT or the New England Heat Pump Accelerator should be placed on Rate HP
{4} automatically. For installations that happen outside of Energize CT — for example, self-funded
{5} purchases — the Company should provide an attestation and certification pathway: a simple
{6} form the installer or customer submits to confirm a qualifying heat pump was installed.

{7} **For existing heat pump customers**, the Company should auto-enroll all known electric
{8} customers who have previously installed heat pumps. These customers are being overcharged
{9} right now. Since the day they installed their heat pump, they have been paying far more than the
{10} T&D costs caused by their new load. And a portion of these customers are readily identifiable by
{11} the Company, those that previously received a heat pump incentive through EnergizeCT or any
{12} other relevant state or utility program. There is no reason to delay correcting this.
{13} After a customer is auto-enrolled, Rate HP would be opt-out: the customer could return to Rate 1
{14} (or any other optional rate) if they choose.

{15} **Q. Why do you recommend automatic enrollment rather than an opt-in rate?**

{16} **A.** Because opt-in rates fail. They require customers to know the rate exists, understand that it
{17} would save them money, and take affirmative action to enroll. Most customers never do.
{18} This is not a theoretical concern. Maine’s Central Maine Power offered a seasonal heat pump
{19} rate on an opt-in basis. Enrollment never exceeded 2% of eligible customers.[^82]

{20} York’s experience is even more striking. Con Edison has facilitated more than 43,000
{21} heat pump installations through its Clean Heat program, yet as of December 2025, only about

Even with active marketing, only about 2,100 enrolled.

{1} 500 households — barely 1% — had enrolled in the utility’s opt-in Steady Use Rate, which is

{4} know the rate existed or found the enrollment process confusing.

{5} The goal of offering heat pump rates by creating Rate HP is to eliminate the existing
{6} cross-subsidy between heat pump customers and fossil-heating customers, and to prevent
{7} customers newly installing heat pumps from overpaying in the first place. **But creating a new**
{8} **rate schedule does not automatically solve the problem — the rate needs to be adopted at**
{9} **scale. And if a heat pump rate isn’t paired with auto-enrollment, it won’t be.**

{10} Massachusetts avoided the opt-in trap entirely by building auto-enrollment into its heat

{15} installation. For customers who installed heat pumps before the program existed, Massachusetts

{17} We know that opt-in rates get low enrollment; the Company can and must design a better

{19} Pump Accelerator. Every heat pump customer who is _not_ enrolled continues to be overcharged
{20} by the full amount every year.

{1} **Q. Could automatic enrollment cause some heat pump customers to see higher bills?**

{2} **A.** No, because Rate HP is specifically designed so that _every heat pump customer pays less than_
{3} _they would under Rate 1_. By lowering Rate 1’s existing winter rates, no heat pump customer
{4} would be made worse off by being placed on Rate HP.

{5} This is the key link between rate design and enrollment. A rate that only _lowers_ charges
{6} can be safely applied to all heat pump customers without risk of adverse bill impacts. If the rate
{7} raised some charges and lowered others — for example, a higher fixed charge paired with a
{8} lower volumetric rate — some customers could see higher bills in certain months. The seasonal
{9} winter reduction avoids this entirely: summer rates are unchanged, and winter rates are only
{10} lower. There are no structural losers.

{11} A rate design that only lowers charges unlocks auto-enrollment, but it necessarily
{12} constrains eligibility: it must be a tech-specific rate, rather than one available to all residential
{13} customers. A lower rate is justified for heat pump customers. As covered in section III, they are
{14} structural “high-usage” customers who would otherwise overpay. A lower rate available to all
{15} customers would simply lower the bills of anyone who enrolled, which would create a revenue
{16} shortfall under heavy enrollment.

{17} I believe this is the right trade-off. A rate that can be safely auto-enrolled to every eligible
{18} customer, even if restricted to a defined population, will reach far more people and thus have a
{19} fundamentally bigger impact than an optional rate with a revenue-neutral design (like a high
{20} fixed charge) that is available to all customers but which will achieve negligible enrollment,
{21} because it requires customers to discover it, evaluate it, and opt in.

{22} **Q. Why is it important to auto-enroll existing heat pump customers, not just new ones?**

[PDF p. 98 | printed p. 96]

{1} **A.** Because enrolling only new customers stops the cross-subsidy from getting bigger, but it does
{2} not fix it for the customers who are already bearing it.

{3} If the Authority only enrolls new heat pump customers going forward, every customer
{4} who has _already_ installed a heat pump continues to be overcharged by the full amount,
{5} indefinitely — unless they opt in on their own, which as I have discussed, many of them will not.
{6} The per-household stakes are substantial: \$1,040 per year in combined T&D overcharges. These
{7} are real dollars being transferred from heat pump customers to fossil-heating customers every
{8} year, and they have been accumulating since the day each customer installed their heat pump.

{9} The Company and Energize CT have records of past heat pump incentive recipients. For
{10} customers who installed heat pumps outside of incentive programs, an attestation process — as
{11} Massachusetts uses — can identify them. Auto-enrollment of existing heat pump customers is
{12} what makes this proposal a genuine fix rather than a half-measure.

## {13} XIII. DISCUSSION OF ALTERNATIVE RATE DESIGNS

{14} **Q. The heat pump rate you propose in Rate HP is a seasonal design, lowering winter**
{15} **transmission and distribution rates. Are there other rate designs that can eliminate the**
{16} **cross-subsidy?**

{17} **A.** Yes, and I considered several before arriving at this recommendation. But any viable solution
{18} must solve two problems simultaneously: the **overpayment problem** — by stopping the
{19} overcollection of transmission and distribution revenue from heat pump customers — and the
{20} **enrollment problem** — ensure that the customers who are actually being overcharged are
{21} actually on the rate that fixes it. These two constraints significantly narrow the design space.

{22} There are two broad categories of solutions: **default rate redesign**, which changes Rate 1
{23} for all residential customers, and **tech-specific rates**, which create a new schedule for heat pump

[PDF p. 99 | printed p. 97]

{1} customers only. Each approach has different strengths and weaknesses on the overpayment and
{2} enrollment dimensions. I will discuss each in turn.

{3} **Q. Could the cross-subsidy be eliminated by redesigning Rate 1?**

{4} **A.** The main virtue of the default rate redesign approach is that it solves the enrollment problem
{5} directly: if you managed to eliminate the overpayment by redesigning Rate 1, the vast majority
{6} of residential customers are already on this rate, so you would fix the problem at scale without
{7} requiring anyone to change anything.

{8} The difficulty is that the main lever for reducing the overpayment within Rate 1 is raising
{9} the fixed charge. Raising the customer charge shifts revenue recovery away from volumetric
{10} rates, which reduces the per-kWh overcollection on high-usage customers.

{11} The problem is magnitude. To eliminate the heat pump overpayment by redesigning Rate
{12} 1, the monthly fixed charge would then have to rise from \$12.36 to \$121.49 — an increase of
{13} \$109.13 per month.[^85]

{14} This is a massive change that would affect every residential customer, not just heat pump
{15} customers, and would be regressive to low-income households unless paired with an income-
{16} graduated structure.

{17} To put that in perspective, the Company’s own MCOS supports a marginal customer cost
{18} of \$28.98 per month for Rate 1.[^86] Yet for Rate 1, the Company proposes moving the customer
{19} charge from \$9.62 to just \$12.36 — a \$2.74 step toward a \$16.62 gap.[^87] At that pace, the fixed

[^85]: The derivation of this figure — the revenue-neutral, cost-reflective redesign of Rate 1 — is described in Exhibit JPV-2, Section 6.

[^86]: Exhibit CLP-MCOS-1 (Eversource 2026c), Table 1: Rate 1 marginal customer cost = \$28.98/month.

[^87]: Joint Direct Testimony of Edward Davis and Kimberly R. Parsons, Exhibit CLP-RATES-1 (Eversource 2026e), Section IV.B. Current Rate 1 customer charge of \$9.62 proposed to increase to \$12.36 per the MRCC calculation in Exhibit CLP-RATES-2.13.

[PDF p. 100 | printed p. 98]

{1} charge would not approach the level needed to eliminate the heat pump cross-subsidy (\$121.49)
{2} for many decades to come.

{3} **Q. What about the tech-specific approach? What design levers does it offer, and why?**

{4} **A.** Within the default rate, where revenue sufficiency must be maintained, the only way to reduce
{5} overcollection from heat pump customers is to raise the fixed charge on everyone, shifting
{6} revenue recovery away from per-kWh rates.

{7} And the Company could choose to create a tech-specific rate exclusively for heat pump
{8} customers with a high fixed-charge design. Indeed, Rate 6 takes a step in this direction, by
{9} raising the fixed charge from \$12.36 to \$30.95.

{10} But a tech-specific rate opens an additional lever for reducing overcollection that the
{11} default rate (or any rate that must be revenue neutral with respect to the residential class as a
{12} whole) does not have: keep the fixed charge the same, and simply lower volumetric charges.
{13} If the overcollection problem arises from the application of volumetric rates _designed for the_
{14} _average electric customer_ to a subclass of residential customers with structurally _above-average_
{15} usage, then simply lowering the volumetric rates for that population reduces overcollection from
{16} that population. Put another way, instead of fundamentally changing the rate design for Rate 1,
{17} you can simply recalibrate it, by designing the volumetric rate component for the average heat
{18} pump customer, rather than the average residential customer.

{19} **Q. How do each of these two kinds of tech-specific rates — raising the fixed charge vs. just**
{20} **lowering the volumetric rates — affect the enrollment problem?**

{21} **A.** Unlike redesigning Rate 1, a tech-specific rate does not solve enrollment automatically —
{22} customers must enroll onto it. And as I showed in Section XII, auto-enrollment is the only
{23} realistic solution to reaching meaningful participation with an optional rate.

[PDF p. 101 | printed p. 99]

{1} I am working under the assumption that the Authority is more likely to approve auto-
{2} enrollment if the rate design guarantees that no enrolled customer is made worse off.

{3} A version of Rate HP with a very high fixed charge could cause a minority of heat pump
{4} customers — particularly lower-usage ones — to see higher bills in certain months. Lower
{5} volumetric rates guarantee the opposite: every enrolled customer either saves or pays the same in
{6} a given month, depending on the design, but no customer pays more in any month.

{7} In other words, tech-specific heat pump rates with lower volumetric charges would allow
{8} the Authority to achieve the benefits of auto-enrollment without paying the cost of creating
{9} structural losers.

{10} **Q. Are there different ways to structure the lower volumetric charges under a tech-specific**
{11} **rate?**

{12} **A.** Yes. There are three main designs, each of which can eliminate the overpayment, if
{13} volumetric rates are lowered to the right level:

1. {14} **Flat** — lower all non-generation volumetric rates by whatever percentage is needed to
   {15} eliminate the annual overpayment. The rates would still be flat, so they would be the
   {16} same for every hour of the year. This is the simplest option.

2. {17} **Seasonal** — leave the summer rate unchanged and lower only the winter rate. **This is**
   {18} **what I propose.** This is also how Massachusetts has designed their heat pump rates.

3. {19} **Time-of-use** — create a heat-pump-specific version of a TOU rate that would have lower
   {20} on-peak and off-peak rates compared with a TOU rate designed to be revenue-neutral for
   {21} the residential class as a whole (such as Rate 7), layering TOU price signals onto the
   {22} tech-specific reduction.

[PDF p. 102 | printed p. 100]

{1} All three solve the overpayment problem as long as the resulting per-kWh rates are set
{2} low enough to stop overcollecting from the average heat pump customer.

{3} I’ll discuss the costs and benefits of each design in turn.

{4} **Q. Given these options, why do you not propose a flat tech-specific rate?**

{5} **A.** A tech-specific rate with a flat volumetric charge that is lower year-round, paired with auto-
{6} enrollment, solves both the overpayment and enrollment problems, and it does so with the
{7} simplest available design. It eliminates the overcharge, guarantees savings for every enrolled
{8} customer, and is simple enough that customers, the Authority, and the Company can all verify
{9} that it works. While this would remove the annual overpayment paid by heat pump customers on
{10} average, it would do so by reducing the T&D bills of heat pump customers by a uniform
{11} percentage in every month, including those in summer. I share the Company’s objective of
{12} pricing incremental heat pump loads at (closer to) marginal cost, in order to satisfy cost-
{13} causation principles. Doing this would “right-size” post-heat-pump T&D bills during the heating
{14} season, since that is where 97% of incremental heat pump load occurs for the average customer
{15} switching from fossil fuels (October through April).

{16} **Q. Why do you advocate for a seasonal tech-specific rate?**

{17} **A.** In addition to the benefits of a flat tech-specific rate — solving the overpayment and
{18} enrollment problems — a seasonal rate has two main benefits:

1. {19} Seasonal rates are targeted: as we saw in Section III, most of the overcollection occurs
   {20} during winter. Lowering rates in winter reduces overcollection from the season where
   {21} most of it occurs, rather than year-round.

2. {22} Seasonal rates promote bill stability: winter is when new heat pump customers see their
   {23} electric bills rise most sharply — the heat pump replaces a gas furnace, so electricity

[PDF p. 103 | printed p. 101]

{1} consumption increases significantly during heating months. A seasonal rate concentrates

{2} the bill relief in exactly those months, smoothing out the seasonal bill shock that might

{3} otherwise discourage adoption or cause customers to question whether the heat pump was

{4} a good investment (even if they are saving money by switching overall).

{5} **Q. How could the Company design a seasonal tech-specific heat pump rate correctly?**

{6} **A.** Designing a seasonal heat pump rate correctly requires satisfying three conditions:

1. {7} The seasonal rate must _lower_ what heat pump customers pay annually. Some seasonal
   {8} designs try to maintain revenue-neutrality for heat pump customers by lowering the
   {9} winter rate but raising the summer rate, which merely shifts the overpayment between
   {10} seasons, defeating the entire purpose of a heat pump rate.

2. {11} The seasonal rate must lower what heat pump customers pay annually _by a sufficient_
   {12} _amount to eliminate the overpayment_. Reducing the winter rate by 1 c/kWh, for example,
   {13} would be a half-measure.

3. {14} While rates in both seasons can be lowered — the reduced revenue collection doesn’t all
   {15} have to concentrate in winter — neither season’s rates should be set higher than those in
   {16} Rate 1, as that could produce structural losers, thus complicating auto-enrollment — even
   {17} if the rate satisfies the first two conditions.

{18} **Q. Why do you not recommend the other approaches you discussed?**

{19} **A.** Each of the alternatives I have discussed in this section could eliminate the overpayment
{20} problem in theory. In practice, they either complicate auto-enrollment, or require significant
{21} disruption to all residential customers in order to make a difference. In other words: given the
{22} downsides of these alternatives, they would likely leave the cross-subsidy largely intact.

[PDF p. 104 | printed p. 102]

{1} A **redesigned Rate 1** would require raising the fixed charge by an amount I do not
{2} believe the Authority is prepared to approve in this case, particularly without introducing
{3} income-graduation to avoid regressive impacts. If the Authority took this approach, the most
{4} likely outcome would be a marginal increase in the fixed charge that would look like progress
{5} but do essentially nothing to remove the overpayment and fix heat pump operating costs.

{6} A **tech-specific rate with a high fixed charge** could strike customers as unfamiliar and
{7} would create structural losers among lower-usage heat pump customers, complicating auto-
{8} enrollment. And without auto-enrollment, creating an optional heat pump rate (that almost no
{9} customers opt into) risks allowing stakeholders to claim victory without having any meaningful
{10} impact on the problem.

{11} A **tech-specific TOU rate** does not solve the overcollection problem on its own; it would
{12} need significantly lower volumetric rates than a TOU rate design for the residential class as a
{13} whole (such as Rate 7) to actually eliminate the overcharge. (The reason is simple: the
{14} overpayment problem arises from an overreliance on volumetric rates applied to structurally
{15} high-usage customers. Making a volumetric rate time-varying does not change this — it still has
{16} to be significantly lowered.) While a properly designed TOU rate for heat pump customers
{17} would create incentives for load shifting as well as removing the overpayment, it would also add
{18} complexity and may create structural losers, unlike a seasonal tech-specific rate. Unless the
{19} Authority is prepared to create a properly designed tech-specific TOU rate with rates low enough
{20} to eliminate the overcharge, auto-enroll all heat pump customers onto it, and do so in a timely
{21} fashion, this route risks allowing the perfect to become the enemy of the good.
