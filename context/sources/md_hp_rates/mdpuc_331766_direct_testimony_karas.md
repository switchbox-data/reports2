## {1} III. RATE DESIGN PRINCIPLES

{2} **Q. WHAT ARE THE BASIC PRINCIPLES BEHIND EFFECTIVE RATE**
{3} **DESIGN?**
{4} A. An effective rate design incorporates the principles of cost causation,
{5} intergenerational equity, price signaling, reasonableness, gradualism, and both inter-
{6} class and intra-class equity. These are documented by experts within the area of
{7} utility ratemaking and are principles employed by this Commission in prior base rate
{8} case proceedings as well as by numerous other commissions around the country.[^3]
{9} **Q. DOES THE COMPANY’S CURRENT RATE DESIGN REFLECT THESE**
{10} **PRINCIPLES?**
{11} A. Generally, yes, although there are certain changes to the current rate design that, if
{12} made, would demonstrate improved adherence to these principles. For example,
{13} according to the principle of cost causation, costs should be borne by the customers
{14} on whose behalf the costs are incurred. The Company’s proposed electric base
{15} distribution revenue allocation methodologies, a two-step approach, is an example of
{16} how cost causation can be addressed by using the results of the ECOSS to move
{17} customer class returns closer to the system average return. As a result, costs will be
{18} borne by the appropriate customers. However, BGE’s proposed methodology also
{19} incorporates the principle of gradualism, whereby rates are moved by incremental
{20} steps rather than by drastic changes.[^4]

[^3]: Principles of Public Utility Rates, Second Edition 1988, by James Bonbright, Albert Danielsen and David Kamerschen, is a well-known reference for the rate design professional.

[^4]: The definition of the word gradualism per Merriam-Webster reads “the policy of approaching a desired end by gradual stages” http://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/gradualism.

[PDF p. 8 | printed p. 5]

{1} **Q. IN WHAT OTHER WAYS DOES COST CAUSATION AFFECT THE**
{2} **COMPANY’S RATE DESIGN?**
{3} A. The Company’s rate design should be consistent with the nature of the costs incurred
{4} in providing service to customers. In other words, fixed and demand-related costs
{5} (costs that do not vary with the total amount of electricity delivered) should be
{6} recovered through fixed monthly rates and rates that reflect a customer’s demand on
{7} the system, respectively, while variable costs (costs that increase or decrease as the
{8} total amount of electricity delivered changes) should be recovered through rates that
{9} vary based on the amount of electricity delivered to a customer. Adherence to this
{10} aspect of rate design is also considered along with other policy objectives such as the
{11} desire to support conservation goals.[^5] However, incorporation of the principle of cost
{12} causation into rate design and the desire to support energy efficiency goals need not
{13} be mutually exclusive.
{14} As I discuss further in my testimony below, the results of the ECOSS support
{15} increasing certain Customer Charges in this proceeding to ensure greater conformity
{16} between the rate design and the nature and level of the costs incurred to serve those
{17} customers.
{18} **Q. WOULD YOU PLEASE EXPLAIN THE COMPANY’S CURRENT RATE**
{19} **STRUCTURE?**
{20} A. BGE’s distribution rate structures primarily include the use of a Customer Charge, a
{21} Demand Charge and a Delivery Service Charge.[^6] The Customer Charge is the fixed,

{1} **Q. WHAT ARE THE EFFECTS OF A RATE STRUCTURE THAT DOES NOT**
{2} **FOLLOW COST CAUSATION AS YOU DESCRIBED?**
{3} A. Rates that do not follow cost causation create intra-class inequities and send incorrect
{4} price signals to customers. For example, customers receive strong price signals from
{5} retail electric supply prices because these prices are based upon the actual commodity
{6} costs borne by suppliers on a per-unit basis. When they reduce consumption,
{7} customers save money as energy providers supply less energy and costs are reduced
{8} on a per-unit basis. Alternatively, the costs to construct and maintain the distribution
{9} system are generally fixed or a function of customer demand levels and not a function
{10} of the total amount of energy delivered to customers. This follows from the certainty
{11} that customers need distribution service to deliver energy to their premises whether
{12} they use a lower-than-average amount of energy, an average amount of energy, or an
{13} above-average amount of energy over a certain period. In fact, customers’ ability to
{14} use energy depends on the continuous safe and reliable operation of the electric
{15} distribution system.
{16} Furthermore, the distribution system must be designed to meet the
{17} requirements of customer peak demand placed on the system. The Company’s cost
{18} to serve a customer is influenced more by a customer’s peak demand than the total
{19} amount of energy used by that customer over a period of time. This is primarily
{20} because substations and other distribution equipment must be sized to meet the
{21} demand of customer peak load to maintain reliability. For example, if an electric
{22} customer’s peak hourly usage is 60 kilowatts of electricity, the electric distribution
{23} system must be designed and built to transport 60 kilowatts of electricity per hour
{24} regardless of the amount of electricity the customer uses the rest of the day.

[PDF p. 11 | printed p. 8]

{1} The rate schedules for all customer classes (except PL) also include a
{2} volumetric component which currently recovers a significant amount of the
{3} distribution portion of the customer bill (approximately 84% for electric residential
{4} customers) – a greater percentage than what the ECOSS supports being recovered
{5} through volumetric rates.[^8] In other words, when customers reduce their consumption
{6} they may save money through reduced delivery service charges, but the overall costs
{7} to support the distribution system are not correspondingly reduced (as these costs are
{8} primarily customer-related or demand-related). Recovery of fixed (customer-related)
{9} and demand-related costs through volumetric rates creates intra-class inequities.
{10} **Q. PLEASE FURTHER EXPLAIN HOW INTRA-CLASS INEQUITIES ARE**
{11} **CREATED WHEN FIXED COSTS ARE NOT RECOVERED THROUGH**
{12} **FIXED CHARGES?**
{13} A. Intra-class inequities are created or allowed to persist when fixed costs are not
{14} recovered through fixed charges because different residential customers, like all
{15} customers, use varying levels of energy. When fixed costs (or costs that do not vary
{16} with the total amount of energy delivered) are recovered through fixed charges,
{17} customers pay for the portion of fixed costs they cause the Company to incur,
{18} regardless of their energy usage. However, when fixed costs are instead recovered
{19} through variable charges, customers with higher usage are paying for a higher portion
{20} of fixed costs than customers with lower usage. This means that customers with
{21} higher-than-average usage are subsidizing the fixed costs of those customers with
{22} lower-than-average usage even though many costs to serve those customers are the

{2} recovered through the variable charges on a customer’s bill, which does not follow
{3} the principle of cost causation. Effective rate design should follow cost causation,
{4} with consideration for gradualism.
{5} **Q. IS THE COMPANY PROPOSING TO MAKE CHANGES TO THE**
