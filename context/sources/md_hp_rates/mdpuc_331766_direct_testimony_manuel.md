## II. Overview of Cost of Service Study Theory

**Q. Please provide a high-level overview of a cost of service study.**

A. The primary objective of an embedded cost of service study is to present a reasonable representation of the cost allocation of the Company's costs during the study period and revenue responsibility among its customer classes, based upon principles of cost causation and revenue responsibility. The purpose of conducting this analysis is to ascertain whether each customer class's revenues are covering their fair share of costs.

**Q. Please explain how the ECOSS will be utilized for rate design purposes.**

A. The ECOSS results included in my Direct Testimony provide a depiction of the rates of return for each of the electric customer classes relative to the system average. These RROR provide guidance for the distribution of the necessary revenue changes among the customer classes. When revenue changes do not take relative rates of return into consideration, under-earning customer classes may continue to under-earn and over-earning classes may continue to over-earn, perpetuating interclass subsidies among customers.

Cost of service studies provide a guide for proper rate design. When rate design is not in line with the classification of cost components, there can be a disconnect between the price signals given to customers and the cost to provide service to those customers, which can result in intraclass subsidies. Company Witness Karas will provide in her Direct Testimony further detail on the use of cost of service study results in proper ratemaking.

**Q. Please summarize how a cost of service study is developed.**

A. There are generally three basic steps to measure customer class responsibility for rate base and expenses:

1. functionalization;
2. classification; and
3. allocation.

**Functionalization** is the process of dividing rate base and expense components of the cost of service study into specified utility functions based on the characteristics of those components. BGE functionalizes its electric delivery service assets and related expenses as transmission or distribution operations. Electric transmission costs, which are subject to the exclusive jurisdiction of FERC, are not included in the ECOSS for the purpose of distribution service ratemaking before the Commission. Electric supply costs recovered through BGE's Rider 1 – Standard Offer Service procurement are also not included in the ECOSS analysis for the purpose of distribution service ratemaking before the Commission.

**Classification** is the process of separating the electric functionalized rate base and expenses into categories that relate to how costs are caused. Distribution costs are primarily classified between demand and customer-related components. Demand-related costs are generally driven by customer class non-coincident peak and/or coincident peak demand levels (discussed in more detail later in my testimony), while customer-related costs are driven by the number and cost of customers connecting to electric transformers and the necessary requirements for the utility to service those customers (e.g., metering, meter reading, account processing, and billing systems). There are some instances in which distribution costs (though minor in relative cost significance) are variable with customer class consumption; in those instances, expenses are classified as energy related.

**Allocation** is the process through which rate base and expenses in each of the classified cost categories are assigned to customer classes according to customer load impositions on the distribution system and/or customer connection requirements. Company costs are directly assigned to the specific customer classes whenever the costs are known to be related to investments or expenses that serve only a particular customer or group of customers. When the costs are not directly assignable to customer classes (e.g., electric substations), they are allocated using an appropriate methodology that best aligns with the cost causation of those elements. That methodology varies depending on the nature of the item being allocated and the data available at the time of the analysis.

## III. Company Recommended ECOSS

**Q. Please summarize the Company's customer-class electric cost of service model.**

A. The ECOSS that I am sponsoring was developed to assign and allocate each element of rate base, revenues and expenses to the Company's electric customer classes. The costs embedded in the 12 months ending December 31, 2024, are identified using the FERC Uniform System of Accounts. Allocations of these costs are made in a manner that follows principles of cost causation. These costs are broken down into categories, such as plant in service, depreciation expense, and operation and maintenance ("O&M") expenses. The allocation of these categories flows into many of the other allocations in the ECOSS. See Company Exhibit JMBM-1 for a comparison of customer class RROR and Company Exhibit JMBM-2 for a summary of the ECOSS results.

**Q. Please describe the allocation of costs to customer classes in the electric cost of service model.**

A. As noted previously, the final step in the ECOSS process is allocation. During this step, rate base and expenses in each of the classified cost categories are assigned to customer classes according to demand on the distribution system, customer-based requirements, usage, and/or revenues. When allocating distribution costs in the ECOSS, it is important to understand the concepts of Non-Coincident Peak demand and Coincident Peak demand. For that reason, I devote a separate section of my testimony below to further explain these demand concepts and their impacts to the ECOSS.

Customer-related distribution electric plant in service FERC accounts (Plant Accounts 369 through 371) includes services, meters, and installations on customers' premises, with these assets allocated to the customer classes according to the number of customers/meters and the level of investment related to size/type of equipment.

Another major allocator used to allocate plant in service is the **LABOR allocator**. The LABOR allocator is an internally developed allocator that is a combination of labor-related expenses included in certain O&M expenses as well as administrative and general expenses. This combination of expenses provides a comprehensive allocator that can be used to allocate a variety of labor-related general plant expenses that are used to serve all customer classes in proportion with the expenses outlined above.

The remaining items such as depreciation, amortization and other O&M expenses are primarily allocated based on the corresponding plant accounts associated with the depreciation, amortization or O&M expenses. For example, operation expenses recorded in FERC Account 582 – Station Operation Expenses and maintenance expenses recorded in FERC Account 592 – Maintenance of Station Equipment are allocated based on the allocated balances of FERC Plant Account 362 – Station Equipment.

## IV. Electric Demand Allocators

**Q. Please explain the concepts of Coincident Peak and Non-Coincident Peak demand.**

A. **Coincident Peak ("CP") demand** is the demand of individual customer classes that coincides (in time) with the peak demand of the whole system. **Non-Coincident Peak ("NCP") demand** represents the actual individual peak demands of each customer class although the individual class peak demands do not necessarily coincide with the time the system peak happens. For example, in 2024 the CP demand of 6,662 MW occurred on July 16, 2024, at the hour ending at 6:00 p.m. Although the system peak occurred in July 2024, the demand for Schedule T peaked in January 2024, which was not coincident with the system peak.

**Q. Please describe the allocation of costs to customer classes and the use of demand allocators in the ECOSS.**

A. The CP and NCP demand are important allocators in the ECOSS. In BGE's ECOSS, distribution 34 kV, 13 kV, and secondary voltage level plant and associated O&M components are classified as demand-related and allocated to the customer classes based on each class's contribution to the total NCP kW, at their respective voltage levels. As a primary allocator of demand components, NCP is used in the ECOSS to reflect how substations and distribution feeders are planned and sized. Distribution feeders are planned and sized based primarily on substation load center peak demands — as opposed to total system peak demand, which drives network transmission facilities.

Service and meter plant and associated O&M expenses are classified as customer-related and allocated to the classes based on weighted customer investment or the number of customers or meters in each rate class. Costs associated with billing functions are classified as customer-related and are generally driven by the number of customers in each class.

**Q. Why is the NCP used to allocate demand-related distribution investment in the ECOSS?**

A. Use of the NCP in the allocation of demand-related distribution investment is the generally accepted methodology in ECOSS development. For example, a cost allocation manual issued by the National Association of Regulatory Utility Commissioners ("NARUC") notes that area loads are major factors in sizing distribution equipment and that the customer class NCP demand allocation method is normally used to allocate distribution substations and primary feeders.[^2]

[^2]: NARUC Electric Utility Cost Allocation Manual at 97 (Jan. 1992).

Distribution substations and distribution feeder connections, down to the secondary transformer, are planned such that sufficient capacity is available to meet customer loads at localized voltage service levels — as compared to network transmission facilities constructed to meet system-wide capacity requirements (where the use of a system coincident peak allocator may be more appropriate).

In general, electric distribution facilities do not function as a single integrated system in meeting system peak demand. Substation area peak loads are the major factor in sizing electric distribution equipment, and customer class NCPs are the generally accepted approach for allocating the demand-related component of distribution plant. For example, a substation load center might have a large concentration of residential air-conditioning end-use customers. As a result, distribution substations and feeders in this load center are typically going to be sized according to the late afternoon or early evening high demand summer peaks when air-conditioning use is most prevalent for residential customers. Alternatively, certain distribution substations and feeders may have a large concentration of electric resistance heating end-use customers that drive a winter peak. As a result, distribution substations and feeders in this load center are typically going to be sized according to the high-demand winter peaks when electric heating use is prevalent.

Other substation load centers may be dominated by peak loads that are mixed with industrial processing or other business profiles. These peak loads should be less weather sensitive and more of a function of economic activity when compared to residential peaks. Substations and feeders serving these loads are likely sized to accommodate cyclical production requirements as opposed to serving seasonal weather sensitive peaks.

The use of an NCP allocator does not consider when the total system peak is recorded but instead reflects more closely the diversity in customer group load patterns.

**Q. Please explain more specifically how the NCP allocator is used in the ECOSS.**

A. Demand-related costs are allocated to customer rate classes based on the corresponding NCP demand, adjusted for losses at each predominant voltage level. The NCP allocator is based on each customer class's highest hourly kW demand. For each month, the maximum hourly demand observed for every customer class — regardless of the hour or the day — is determined. Each class's contribution to the NCP is calculated by dividing that class's maximum hourly demand by the sum of every class's maximum hourly demand. This process develops the system allocators used to assign distribution demand-related costs to the various customer classes. As explained later, the ECOSS I am sponsoring uses demand allocators based upon an average of demands observed for each customer class over the four years from 2021–2024.

In the ECOSS, the Company measures residential customer peak kW demand (Schedules R and RL) in aggregate on an hourly basis. The Schedules R and RL individual peaks are then determined at the time of the total residential peak (i.e., coincident peak with the total residential peak).

Similar to residential peak demand, the Company measures small commercial customer peak demand (Schedules G and GS) in aggregate on an hourly basis. The Schedules G and GS individual peaks are determined at the time of the total small commercial peak.

All residential customers are treated as one demand group, and all small commercial customers are treated as another single-demand group in the NCP allocator due to each group having similar distribution service requirements. This aligns with cost causation principles, which refers to the determination of what, or who, is causing costs to be incurred by the utility. The Company plans and builds distribution substations and distribution feeders to ensure that sufficient capacity is available to meet peak customer loads whenever they may occur. The Company's engineers do not look at the split of customers between Schedules R and RL or Schedules G and GS when designing the distribution system. Although slight variations may exist in on-premises meter sizes or service connections, Schedules RL and GS were established to allow for time-of-use commodity pricing. Schedule RL customers may choose to change to Schedule R service and vice versa at any time, and Schedule GS customers may choose to change to Schedule G service and vice versa at any time.

**Q. Does BGE's ECOSS recognize the use of various delivery service voltage levels in allocating distribution investment among customer classes?**

A. Yes. The ECOSS recognizes that the distribution system consists of several voltage level grids and identifies customer class cost responsibility at each of the grid levels. The distribution voltage level grids are segmented by:

1. sub-transmission voltages at 34 kV;
2. primary voltages at 13 kV; and
3. secondary voltages.

Recognizing this distinction in service voltages ensures that only the loads of those customers who benefit from the facilities are included in the corresponding NCP allocators.

**Q. Does NCP demand used in the Company's ECOSS include system losses?**

A. Yes. System energy losses occur in the transformation of power from higher to lower voltage levels of service and in the distribution of power to load centers in general. System losses are included in the development of the NCP allocator. The Company's 34 kV, 13 kV, and secondary voltage NCP demand allocators are adjusted to account for appropriate line losses as well as to reflect the removal of demands not associated with the corresponding voltage level allocator. The NCP allocators used in the Company's ECOSS are net of system losses.

**Q. What demand allocators are you proposing to use in the ECOSS?**

A. Consistent with the ECOSS approach approved by the Commission in Case Nos. 9645 and 9692, I am proposing four-year average demand allocators be used for all metered classes on a per-customer basis. Consistent with the ECOSS approach accepted by the Commission in Case Nos. 9645 and 9692, as well as Commission precedent, I am proposing single-year actual demand allocators be used for non-metered classes. My demand allocator proposals are consistent with the Company's proposal in Case No. 9692, as accepted by the Commission in Order No. 90948 in that case.[^3]

See further discussion of the Company's compliance with this directive in Section VI of my testimony.

## V. Changes Made to the ECOSS Presented in This Case

**Q. Have you made any changes to allocation methodologies used in the ECOSS since the Company's last base rate proceeding?**

A. Yes, there have been a few changes to the allocation methodologies since Case No. 9692. First, the Company is utilizing a new **PLANTLAB allocator**, as proposed by Staff in Case No. 9692, for accounts 303, 389, and 398 in the current rate case. As noted in the section below, this was directed by the Commission. In addition, rows have been added for new regulatory assets related to major storms and supplier consolidated billing along with a new row for the accumulated deferred income tax balance associated with the new major storm regulatory asset. The new rows all leverage existing allocators utilized for rate base and expense items that have similar cost-causation characteristics.

**Q. Please describe the allocators related to the new regulatory assets.**

A. BGE proposes to use the existing **PLTDOHLN allocator** for the major storm regulatory asset and associated accumulated deferred income tax balance. This allocator is used to allocate O&M expenses included in FERC Account 583 – Overhead Line Operation Expenses and FERC Account 593 – Overhead Line Maintenance Expenses. I believe this is a reasonable allocator to use as the vast majority of expenses deferred into the major storm regulatory asset relate to overhead line O&M expenses.

BGE proposes to use the existing **LABOR allocator** for the electric supplier consolidated billing software regulatory asset. As noted previously in my testimony, this allocator is used in the COSS to allocate costs primarily driven by labor. I believe this to be a reasonable allocator as it is consistent with the use of the LABOR allocator to allocate costs associated with FERC Account 303 – Miscellaneous Intangible Plant for Common Non-AMI Assets, which includes software.
