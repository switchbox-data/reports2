## II. DISTRIBUTION REVENUE ALLOCATION

### A. Introduction

**Q.** What is the distribution revenue allocation process?

**A.** The distribution revenue allocation begins with the Rate Year 1 distribution revenue requirement presented by the Revenue Requirement Panel. The distribution revenue requirement consists of base delivery revenues (customer, demand, distribution kilowatt hour ("kWh") and kilovar ("kVar") revenues). The Panel allocated the proposed total revenue increase to each service classification and designed rates for each class. CMP summarizes its development of base distribution revenues by service classification in Exhibit RD-1.

<!-- PDF page 8 (printed RD-6) -->

**Q.** Please describe the Company's revenue allocation methodology.

**A.** As an initial step in the revenue allocation process, the Company reviewed the results of both the ECOS and MCOS studies and compared the ratios of the revenue to revenue requirement for each class. Using the ECOS study, current revenues were compared to the revenues required from each service class to achieve a rate of return equal to the overall system rate of return. A similar analysis was conducted using the MCOS study results. The Company compared the forecast of revenues at current rates to the revenues for each class priced at marginal costs, which were adjusted using an equi-proportional ratio to reflect the overall marginal cost revenues to forecast revenues at existing rates. The results of this analysis are presented in Exhibit RD-2. The comparison of revenue to revenue requirement ratios indicates which classes warrant an increase higher than an overall average revenue increase because they are under-contributing to revenue requirement recovery and which classes should receive no increase as they are over-contributing.

Recognizing that some judgments and approximations are part of any cost analysis, the Company applied a tolerance band to the results of the cost studies to account for potential variation in results. That is, if the revenue to revenue requirement ratios of both the ECOS and MCOS studies for any of the service classes fall outside of the +/-15% tolerance band in the same direction, the contributions for those classes would change by a percentage other than an overall average revenue increase.

<!-- PDF page 9 (printed RD-7) -->

### B. Findings and Proposed Adjustments

**Q.** What findings resulted from the Company's revenue allocation process?

**A.** As can be seen in Exhibit RD-2, the LGS-ST-TOU service class is the only class[^2] that shows an over-contribution for both the embedded and marginal cost analysis. CMP adjusted the revenues allocated to the LGS-ST-TOU service class such that the revised allocation was within the tolerance bands. CMP then adjusted the Rate A and SGS classes to collect the revenues removed from the LGS-ST-TOU class. The Rate A and SGS classes showed the most symmetry between the embedded and marginal revenue to revenue requirement ratios and allocating the small amount of revenues removed from the LGS-ST-TOU class did not affect rate design for the Rate A and SGS classes. The Rate A and SGS service class revenue allocation remain within the tolerance bands after the adjustment.

**Q.** Has the Company's revenue allocation methodology changed from the methodology presented in previous CMP distribution rate cases?

**A.** No. The proposed revenue allocation is consistent with the revenue allocation process approved by the Commission in Docket No. 2013-00168 and makes movement toward an appropriate allocation of cost responsibilities among the various service classes, as indicated by the results of the ECOS and MCOS studies.

## III. OVERVIEW OF RATE DESIGN

**Q.** What are the Company's guiding principles regarding revenue allocation and rate design?

**A.** The Company's primary revenue allocation and rate design goals are adequacy, efficiency, and fairness. Adequacy ensures that the rates are designed to recover the necessary revenue requirement set forth by the Revenue Requirement Panel. Efficiency means designing rates to recover costs from customers in a way that reflects, as closely as possible, how the Company incurs these costs. Fairness calls for allocating the total revenue cost of providing service to each class. Also important is the goal of rate stability, which recognizes that rates based solely on the goals mentioned above must often be tempered because of impacts to customer total bills.[^3]

**Q.** How has the Company addressed the adequacy goal?

**A.** The Company has addressed adequacy by designing rates to recover the distribution revenue requirement proposed by the Revenue Requirement Panel from the respective service classes. In developing the proposed revenue allocation, the Company used the

<!-- PDF page 11 (printed RD-9) -->

results of the ECOS and MCOS studies as guidance for allocating the revenue requirement among the service classes.

**Q.** How has the Company addressed the efficiency goal?

**A.** Collecting costs on the same basis as they are incurred to achieve economically efficient pricing is consistent with 35-A M.R.S. § 3152, which "require[s] the Commission to relate transmission and distribution rates more closely to the costs of providing transmission and distribution service."[^4] Economic theory states that, with efficiency being the goal, the pricing of services should be based on the marginal costs of providing those services. In addition, 35-A M.R.S. § 3153-A(1)(B) expressly requires rate design proposals to "reflect marginal costs of services at different voltages, times of day or seasons of the year, including long-run marginal costs associated with the construction of new transmission and distribution facilities."[^5] The Commission's obligation to incorporate economic efficiency in rate design was strengthened by the Omnibus Energy Act of 2013.[^6]

**Q.** How has the Company addressed the fairness goal?

**A.** The Company is using the results of the MCOS study conducted by Ms. Nieto to guide its rate design proposals. The MCOS study measures the marginal cost of CMP's electric distribution system over three components. The first component is customer-related costs, which are the costs associated with meters, services and the associated operation and maintenance expenses, as well as other customer-related costs such as customer accounts, customer service and billing. The second component is local distribution

<!-- PDF page 12 (printed RD-10) -->

facilities costs, which are the costs associated with connecting a customer to the grid, consisting generally of line transformers, secondary lines and local primary lines. Local distribution facilities are sized based on the maximum expected loads, or design demand, of customers using them over the life of the equipment. The expectation is that local distribution facilities will not be expanded in response to month-to-month or year-to-year variations in actual customer usage. The third component is upstream distribution costs, which consist of upstream distribution stations, distribution substations and trunkline feeders. These facilities are expanded to accommodate growth in distribution peak load and maintain reliability. Ms. Nieto discusses the MCOS study results in her testimony. As she explained, an efficient rate design would mirror the structure of CMP's marginal costs. The filed MCOS study presents the details that guide the rate design proposed in this case. The MCOS study supports recovery of customer-related costs and local distribution facilities costs through a $/month per-customer service charge. Upstream distribution costs would be recovered through time-differentiated per kWh charges or per kW demand charges. The proposed rates do not precisely equal the marginal costs as presented by the MCOS study, as the Company must develop its rates to recover the class distribution revenue requirement.

**Q.** How has the Company addressed the rate stability goal?

**A.** In fashioning its rate design proposal, CMP also considered rate stability. Moving strictly to a cost-of-service based rate design could cause changes that result in more significant bill impacts on certain customers. Consequently, the Company considered customer bill impacts during the rate design process. The Company describes the specifics of these constraints in the respective sections below.

<!-- PDF page 13 (printed RD-11) -->

**Q.** What other considerations factored into the Company's rate design proposals?

**A.** CMP also seeks to enable State policy and stakeholder consensus objectives. The State of Maine has established aggressive greenhouse gas ("GHG") emission reduction targets, requiring emissions to be reduced 45% below 1990 levels by January 1, 2030, and 80% below 1990 levels by January 1, 2050.[^7] Per the Maine Department of Environmental Protection ("DEP") Eighth Biennial Report on Progress toward Greenhouse Gas Reduction Goals,[^8] 54% of Maine's emissions from fossil fuel combustion arise from the transportation sector. Another 30% of emissions arise from the residential and commercial building sector. Maine established its first Climate Action Plan, _Maine Won't Wait_ ("Maine Climate Action Plan"),[^9] to begin scoping analyses and strategies to achieve GHG emission reductions. The first Maine Climate Action Plan strategy, Strategy A, is to embrace the future of transportation in Maine, with the first objective to accelerate Maine's transition to EVs. The Nature Conservancy sponsored a stakeholder engagement process managed by the Great Plains Institute, dubbed the Maine Utility/Regulatory Reform and Decarbonization Initiative ("MURRDI"), that culminated in an April 2021 report ("MURRDI Report") containing a set of stakeholder recommendations.[^10] The MURRDI Report included a recommendation to have load flexibility enabled by dynamic rate designs. As such, CMP seeks to provide rates to

<!-- PDF page 14 (printed RD-12) -->

customers that while adhering to the principles of cost-based rate design, can also serve to advance the State and stakeholder decarbonization and load flexibility priorities.

### B. Time of Use Periods

**Q.** Why is the Company proposing changes to its time of use periods?

**A.** CMP's TOU periods need to be updated. The Company is concerned about CMP's growing regional load ratio share[^12] and resulting increase in Regional Network Service ("RNS") expense responsibility to CMP customers (_i.e._, regional transmission expense that flows to all Maine customers). CMP estimates this additional load ratio share change from 2018 to 2020 is costing CMP customers over $5 million annually. Misaligned TOU peak periods send improper price signals to customers and do not motivate consumption away from actual system peaks, hindering alleviation of CMP's growing load ratio share and increasing transmission expense overall for Maine customers. Further, load and

<!-- PDF page 16 (printed RD-14) -->

distributed generation on CMP's system is evolving and anticipated to continue to change due to policy efforts to advance distributed energy resources and beneficial electrification technologies. For these reasons, and based upon the further analysis provided in PA's MCOS testimony, the Company believes that updating TOU periods is necessary.

**Q.** How will TOU hours change based on the Company's proposal?

**A.** CMP proposes to redefine its TOU periods for all TOU classes in accordance with the MCOS study results.[^13] As described in PA's MCOS testimony, the Company considered several TOU options and ultimately decided an option which consists of three seasonal periods – Summer (May – August), Winter (December – February), and Shoulder (March – April, September – November) - and offers an on- peak/shoulder/off-peak TOU structure as set forth in Figure 1 below.

**Q.** When would the Company be able to implement its TOU proposal, if approved?

**A.** As discussed in Section IX of this testimony, CMP anticipates the Company will need approximately 15 months to implement the system changes necessary to implement the new TOU structure once the Commission approves it.

**Q.** What are the benefits of the proposed TOU hour change?

**A.** The proposed TOU option reduces the number of peak hours and designates the same consecutive blocks of weekday hours as peak/shoulder times across all seasons. The TOU hours for residential and non-residential customers will be the same under CMP's proposal. As stated in the MCOS testimony, this option balances the goal of reflecting the variation in marginal costs during the day with minimizing complexity for customers.

**Q.** How will the Company inform customers of the proposed TOU period and hour change?

**A.** CMP realizes that the proposed TOU structure may present initial challenges to some customers to understand the new time periods and adjust usage patterns. During the period that CMP is configuring its systems to support the new TOU periods, CMP plans to develop and share educational materials for customers to help with the transition to the new TOU periods.

### C. Time of Use Rate Design for Residential Customers Effective October 1, 2024

**Q.** Is the Panel proposing other changes that affect TOU customers?

**A.** As noted above, for the residential class, the Company combined the revenue requirements for Rate A, A-TOU, and Super Saver[^14] to develop new rates reflecting the proposed TOU periods. With the redesigned TOU Option, CMP proposes to eliminate

<!-- PDF page 18 (printed RD-16) -->

the current A-TOU and Super Saver classes. CMP's redesigned residential TOU Option collects a significant portion of the distribution revenue not allocated to the service charge through the on-peak per-kWh price to provide differentiation between on-peak, shoulder, and off-peak pricing, as indicated by the MCOS study. This differential will provide an opportunity for customers to shift their usage to the off-peak period. CMP still intends to apply non-distribution revenues (_e.g._, stranded cost and Efficiency Maine Trust ("EMT") assessment) equally to the on-peak, shoulder, and off-peak per-kWh price. CMP is proposing an option to apply transmission charges to the on-peak kWh only for non-demand TOU classes.

## VI. OTHER RATE DESIGN PROPOSALS

### A. New Optional Residential and SGS Rates

**Q.** Is the Panel proposing optional beneficial electrification rates for the Company's residential and SGS customers?

**A.** Yes. CMP proposes new optional rates for the residential class and SGS class targeted at advancing beneficial electrification technologies, such as EV charging and heat pumps. The two rates would be available to be taken either as a whole-house rate or, on a separate meter, for just EV charger(s) or other dedicated load. The customer would not be required to have an EV charger or heat pump if taking the rate as a whole-house rate. The rates consist of a fixed monthly charge for distribution revenue requirement recovery and a peak demand charge for transmission revenue requirement recovery. The peak periods over which the demand charge is applied correspond to the peak periods shown in Figure 1. Other costs such as stranded costs, low income, and the EMT assessment, are recovered via volumetric kWh charges as is done in existing rates.

<!-- PDF page 19 (printed RD-17) -->

**Q.** What benefits are associated with these optional EV or heat pump rates?

**A.** The customer and societal benefits of these rates for EV or whole-house loads include:

1. minimization of cross-subsidies between customers because the rate design is consistent with marginal costs;
2. customer understandability due to its relative simplicity and predictability while preserving cost-causation;
3. providing rate options to customers, which customers value per the JD Power and Associates' Customer Satisfaction Survey;[^15]
4. providing opportunity for customers to lower their delivery charges by avoiding heavy use of the system during peak times; and
5. responding to stakeholder and State policy interest in enabling EVs while also utilizing transmission and distribution infrastructure efficiently.

**Q.** How do economic considerations and rate design goals factor into the design of the optional EV or heat pump rates?

**A.** This delivery rate option has been designed with both marginal cost economics and modern behavioral economics in mind. As evident in the submitted MCOS study, most of the marginal distribution delivery costs are fixed. These new optional rates have a fixed charge that comes closer to the marginal fixed costs than do the old existing standard Rate A or SGS rates. As such, these new optional rates are more consistent with the economic efficiency espoused in the economics of marginal costs and with Maine law.[^16] At the same time, the rates are customer-focused, and provide a level of simplicity and predictability that enables customer satisfaction via easy budgeting and management

<!-- PDF page 20 (printed RD-18) -->

for cost savings. Per behavioral economics, the relative understandability of these optional rates responds to human cognitive biases to seek out simple solutions in the face of complexity and cognitive overload.[^17]

**Q.** Did the Company consider outcomes from previous regulatory proceedings when developing these optional EV or heat pump rates?

**A.** Yes. It is worth noting these new optional rates also try to satisfy – in an alternative form than what was proposed, but not ultimately accepted in the 2018 rate case – the Commission's desire for CMP to introduce a demand rate option for residential and SGS classes. In CMP's 2013 distribution rate proceeding in Docket No. 2013-00168, the Commission stated in its October 14, 2014 Order on Rate Design Issues (Part II) that optional demand rates should be developed for the residential and SGS customer classes, subject to a review of billing system costs. As such, while focused on enabling EVs and heat pumps, customers may desire to take delivery under this rate for other purposes that result in more efficient utilization of the grid, such as for the installation of distributed storage technology that would shift load outside of the peak demand window, reducing demand and thus infrastructure investments on the grid and saving the customer on delivery costs.

**Q.** Please describe the savings potential a customer could receive by taking service under an optional EV or heat pump rate.

### B. Expansion of Dynamic Demand Delivery Rate Option

**Q.** Is the Company proposing optional rates for customers taking service under rates with demand charges?

**A.** Yes. The Company anticipates expanding eligibility for the B-DCFC rate design to all demand-bill customers in their respective classes, thus offering a dynamic delivery rate for customers, meeting recommendation #3 of the MURRDI stakeholders on Load Flexibility Enabled by Dynamic Rate Designs.[^18] Note that this new optional rate, B-CPT, has been proposed in Docket No. 2021-00325.

The two-part demand charge design of this rate accounts for differences in (a) distribution costs that are largely driven by customer maximum demands and (b) transmission costs that are largely driven by the temporal coincidence of those demands with system peaks. Specifically, the two-part demand rate contains two separate measures of customer demand: (a) the customer's non-coincident peak ("NCP") demand, which is the customer's maximum demand during each billing period; and (b) the customer's coincident peak ("CP") demand, which is a customer's maximum demand during the monthly system peak.

<!-- PDF page 23 (printed RD-21) -->

The NCP demand charge reflects a customer's contribution to costs associated with distribution facilities. The NCP demand charge recovers local delivery costs, which are generally: (a) any portion of the rate class customer-related costs that is not recovered in the customer charge; (b) secondary distribution demand-related costs; and (c) any portion of primary distribution costs not included in local transmission costs. The NCP demand charge is calculated the same way as CMP's traditional demand charges.

The CP demand charge is designed to align price signals received by customers with their responsibility for those costs incurred in serving CMP's system peak load. The CP demand charge recovers upstream delivery costs, which are generally: (a) local transmission costs (_e.g._, non-pooled transmission facilities); and (b) regional transmission costs (_e.g._, pooled transmission facilities). A customer's CP billing demand is calculated monthly, in the same manner as an existing sub-transmission and transmission ("ST&T") customer's CP demand is calculated.

**Q.** What are the benefits of the B-DCFC/B-CPT optional rates described above?

**A.** This two-part demand rate aligns with the costs incurred by CMP, thereby better reflecting cost causation. Existing participants on the B-DCFC rate have realized over 40% savings on delivery costs,[^19] and future participants are expected to achieve about 45% savings,[^20] given the charging load characteristics experienced at light-duty vehicle Level 3 fast charging stations.

<!-- PDF page 24 (printed RD-22) -->

The Company believes other customers, such as those seeking to deploy distributed storage solutions, should have the opportunity to achieve cost savings that might be realized under this cost-based two-part demand rate. For certain customers, this dynamic delivery rate, where the CP demand is incurred in a short window, may result in savings without any change in behavior. For others, this dynamic peak pricing delivery rate – which has a shorter peak window than the existing fixed and NCP measured TOU demand rate – may help enable behavioral or technological solutions, such as distributed storage, that can save customers costs while at the same time shifting load and reducing stress on the system at peak times.

Such a rate helps enable more efficient utilization of CMP's delivery network. This two-part demand rate avoids transmission peak investments via a more direct price signal; the same signal already utilized by ST&T customers. Benefits include a time variant CP that is inherently adaptable over time to changes in system peaks.

**Q.** How will the Company promote these optional rates to customers?

**A.** This new rate option for demand-billed customers will be included in the customer education and awareness campaign for CMP's rate changes and new options.

### F. Demand Charge for MGS, IGS, and LGS Distribution Customers

**Q.** What are the proposed changes to demand charges for the MGS, IGS, and LGS rate classes?

## VII. RESIDENTIAL SERVICE CHARGES AND LOW-INCOME CUSTOMERS

**Q.** Will CMP's proposed changes to residential service charges have a negative effect on low-income customers?

<!-- PDF page 30 (printed RD-28) -->

customers.[^25] These findings are consistent with CMP's analysis of the usage patterns of its ELP customers.

**Q.** Does the Company have any analysis that supports that its low-usage customers are not necessarily low-income customers?

**A.** As described in CMP's Rebuttal Testimony in Docket No. 2013-00168[^26] and as updated using customer information for 2021, a significant portion of the Company's low-use customers are not low-income. An analysis of CMP customer data showed conservatively that about 30 - 35% of CMP's low-use customers – _i.e._, those using under 400 kWh per month – are seasonal or vacation homes.[^27] By any measure, the ability to own a second property is not indicative of a low-income customer.

CMP's analysis of customers with usage equal to or below 400 kWh in 2021 shows that approximately 14% of CMP's total customer base falls into this usage group. Of these customers, approximately 3.3% received HEAP benefits.

**Q.** Has the Panel drawn any conclusions regarding low-income customers and increased service charges?

**A.** Yes. CMP continues to assert that the analyses showing the impact of increased service charges to ELP customers are relevant. As the Company has demonstrated, these lowest income customers tend to use significantly more electricity than the average and, as a whole, would benefit from a rate structure with an increased service charge. CMP also

<!-- PDF page 31 (printed RD-29) -->

notes that outside of knowing which customers participate in HEAP and ELP programs, CMP collects no other income-related information on its customers.

## IX. PROPOSED CHANGES TO METERING SYSTEMS

### A. Approach and Methodology

**Q.** Why is CMP proposing a change to its metering systems?

**A.** CMP needs to make changes to its metering systems to accommodate the Company's TOU proposal, particularly if adopted at scale.

**Q.** What is CMP's current methodology to calculate a customer's energy consumption for billing?

**A.** Currently, CMP's SAP Customer Relationship Management and Billing ("CRM&B") system calculates electric energy consumption based on meter register reads (_i.e._, point in time values of the kW and/or kWh units measured by the meter) obtained from the Itron Enterprise Edition Meter Data Management System ("IEE MDMS"), MV-90 or FCS.

**Q.** Is CMP proposing any changes?

**A.** CMP has determined that using interval-based, not register-based, billing by leveraging Rate Modeler, a component of the IEE MDMS, is an efficient systems-based approach to accommodate changes to TOU schedules and rates particularly if adopted at-scale. Physical meter exchanges or reprogramming are not required with this approach. Although the meter's display will not show a change to the TOU periods originally programmed into the meter, customers will be able to see their hourly interval consumption on the Energy Manager portal and total consumption per updated TOU period on their electric bill.

<!-- PDF page 33 (printed RD-31) -->

**Q.** Will the Company's Rate Modeler tool facilitate the implementation of new TOU and demand-based rates?

**A.** Yes. For customers opting for a TOU rate, CMP would use Rate Modeler to configure the TOU schedules. When SAP CRM&B requests it, Rate Modeler would perform a time series calculation, which means summing each meter's hourly intervals within each TOU period during the billing period to produce one consumption value in kWh per TOU period. Rate Modeler would then transmit these consumption values to SAP CRM&B in what is called a time of use bucket. For residential or SGS customers opting for the demand rate, Rate Modeler would send a demand kW value based on the hourly interval with the highest consumption during the billing period as well as a total consumption value for the billing period. For customers opting for both TOU and demand rates, Rate Modeler would send a set of demand kW values based on the highest hourly interval within each TOU period during the billing period in addition to a set of total consumption values for each TOU period.

### B. Summary of Expected System Changes

**Q.** Please describe the changes to IEE MDMS.

**A.** This system contains the register and interval data for AMI meters. Rate Modeler is already available in IEE MDMS, but new rates and TOU schedules will need to be setup.

**Q.** Please describe the changes to the Company's MV-90 system.

**A.** The MV-90 system contains the meter data transmitted via dial-up or IP communications from customers with complex metering and/or daily ISO-NE reporting requirements. There are approximately 470 MV-90 meters as of this filing, of which approximately 340 are used specifically for billing purposes via a register generated by a custom module and

<!-- PDF page 34 (printed RD-32) -->

the remaining 130 are for measuring non-billing generation and tie lines. Since this system already leverages hourly interval data, only a change to the TOU schedule in the application settings is required.

**Q.** Please describe the changes to the Company's Field Collection System ("FCS").

**A.** The FCS system receives manually read meter data uploaded from handheld devices. As of this filing, approximately 53 TOU customers participate in CMP's Smart Meter Opt-Out program, including 49 residential TOU, 3 residential TOU Super Saver, and one SGS. Since consumption will be calculated in Rate Modeler, a custom interface to support the flow of meter interval data from FCS to MDMS will need to be developed.

**Q.** Please describe the changes to SAP CRM&B.

**A.** The SAP CRM&B system is used for customer service business functions including customer billing, which currently uses register values from IEE MDMS, MV-90, and FCS as billing determinants. Billing and meter configuration would need to be modified to use the consumption values provided by Rate Modeler as billing determinants to produce the bill.
