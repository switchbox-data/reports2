**By the Commission:**

## I. INTRODUCTION

On May 23, 2024, Commonwealth Edison Company (“ComEd”) filed with the Illinois Commerce Commission (“Commission”) a Petition pursuant to Section 16-105.5(a) and (c) of the Public Utilities Act (“Act”), 220 ILCS 5/16-105.5 (a) and (c), to approve its proposed revenue-neutral tariff changes and its proposed delivery time of use rate. The tariff filing included ComEd’s direct testimony and attached exhibits.

The purpose of this rate design investigation (“RDI”) proceeding is to consider “revenue-neutral tariff changes…which shall present the Commission with an opportunity to suspend the tariffs and consider revenue-neutral tariff changes related to rate design.” 220 ILCS 5/16-105.5(c)(1). Pursuant to Sections 16-105.5(a) and (c) of the Act, ComEd is required to either propose revenue-neutral tariff changes or refile existing tariffs without changes every three years. Section 16-105.5 also authorizes a utility that files a Multi-Year Rate Plan (“MYRP”) pursuant to Section 16-108.18, as ComEd did, to file a revenue neutral RDI separate from the MYRP. 220 ILCS 5/16-105.5(a) and (c). Section 5/16-105.5 (c)(1) states: “the electric utility must submit its separate revenue-neutral rate design filing no later than 3 years after the date on which the Commission’s most recent final rate design order was entered for the electric utility.” ComEd’s most recent rate design order was entered on May 27, 2021, in Docket No. 20-0832. Commonwealth Edison Co., Docket No. 20-0832, Order (May 27, 2021).

## III. SCOPE OF THE PROCEEDING

### A. ComEd’s Position

ComEd states that this proceeding is the rate design portion of ComEd’s MYRP. _See generally_ Commonwealth Edison Co., Docket Nos. 22-0486/23-0055/24-0181 (Consol.) (“MYRP Docket”). By statute, the scope of RDIs is limited to revenue-neutral tariff changes to the allocation of delivery service costs among ComEd’s rate classes and changes to the rate design of Rate MRPP – Multi-Year Rate Plan Pricing (“Rate MRPP”). _See_ 220 ILCS 5/16-105.5(a), (c) (referencing the “revenue-neutral rate design component” of an electric utility’s MYRP for delivery service rates). Specific issues raised by REACT, ICEA/RESA, CUB/EDF, CTA, Metra, and the City are out of scope for an RDI because they are unrelated to the allocation of ComEd’s Commission-approved delivery service revenue requirement, propose that the Commission exceed its authority, and/or are not revenue neutral.

According to ComEd, the Commission has only the authority granted to it by the legislature through statutes, and an agency’s statutory power to act defines its jurisdiction. _See_ Bus. & Pro. People v. Ill. Com. Comm’n, 136 Ill. 2d 192, 243 (1989); Ill. Power Co. v. Ill. Com. Comm’n, 111 Ill. 2d 505, 510 (1986). In every ComEd RDI brought under Section 16-108.5(e), the Commission has concluded that the scope of the proceeding was “limited to considering revenue neutral tariff changes to allocation of delivery service costs among ComEd’s rate classes and possible changes to the rate design formula of Rate DSPP – Delivery Service Pricing and Performance (“Rate DSPP”) in accordance with provisions of subsections 16-108.5(e) of the Act.” Commonwealth Edison Co., Docket No. 13-0387, Order at 3 (Dec. 18, 2013) (“2013 RDI Order”); _see also_ Commonwealth Edison Co., Docket No. 17-0049, Order at 2 (Jul. 26, 2017) (“2017 RDI Order”); _see also_ Commonwealth Edison Co., Docket No. 20-0832, Order at 2 (May 27, 2021) (“2020 RDI Order”). The language of Section 16-105.5 mirrors its predecessor statute, Section 16-108.5(e), so these rulings remain instructive in the current proceeding. In addition, the Commission has consistently concluded that ComEd’s RDIs are “not about determining, or adjusting, ComEd’s Commission-approved delivery service revenue requirement.” 2017 RDI Order at 3.

In direct contravention of the scope as defined above, ComEd states that ICEA/RESA seek to move a substantial amount – $500 million – which is nearly 25% of ComEd’s Commission-approved delivery service revenue requirement to supply service. _See generally_ ICEA/RESA Ex. 1.0; ICEA/RESA Ex. 2.0. The Commission must reject this position because it is, by definition, not a revenue neutral proposal and is in contravention of the statutory parameters of Section 16-105.5. The Commission has previously decided that this exact issue is outside the scope of a revenue neutral rate design investigation and is instead appropriately decided in a rate case. _See_ 2013 RDI Order at 57-58 (addressing customer care costs and directing that the merits of these costs be addressed in a rate case). ICEA/RESA witness Giacchino even admitted that under his narrow view of his proposal, at the very least, a portion of the proposed shift would not be revenue neutral. ICEA/RESA Ex. 2.0 at 13. That is, in Giacchino’s view, he admits his proposal would reduce the total revenue that ComEd collects, i.e., the return on the capital assets that he shifts to supply. _See id._ The Commission should make that same determination again here and reject ICEA/RESA’s proposal to alter the apportionment of any costs between distribution and supply as out of scope.

Regarding modifying Rider NS – Nonstandard Services and Facilities (“Rider NS”), as a threshold matter, changes to other tariffs – the revenue requirements of which are not established in ComEd’s MYRP – fall outside the delivery service rate design component of this filing. 220 ILCS 5/16-102 (defining delivery service). ComEd states that the City, Metra, and CTA seek to modify ComEd’s Rider NS in ways that run contrary to fundamental RDI principles. For example, revenues collected under Rider NS are not part of ComEd’s delivery service revenue requirement, but rather are collected directly from the customer requiring the service. Rider NS, ILL. C. C. No. 10, 1st Revised Sheet No. 277 (noting ComEd will provide nonstandard services or facilities “provided [that ComEd] is allowed to recover from the retail customer the costs of furnishing, installing, owning, operating, replacing, and maintaining such services or facilities”). Furthermore, the City seeks to amend Rider NS in a manner that would ultimately shift costs from the City to all other ComEd customers. _See_ COC Ex. 1.0 at 18-19; ComEd Ex. 10.0 at 8-9; ComEd Ex. 9.0 REV. at 5. The Commission should reject such proposals here in favor of workshops where potential modifications to Rider NS can be evaluated more fully.

ComEd states that CTA and Metra propose changes to ComEd’s Rider DE – Distribution System Extensions (“Rider DE”) that are similarly flawed. _See_ CTA/Metra Ex. 1.0 at 17 (requesting wholesale exclusion from Rider DE for public transit agencies); ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 69 (noting the Commission rejected this same proposal in the BE Plan 1 Order (citing Commonwealth Edison Co., Docket Nos. 22-0432/22-0442 (Consol.), Order at 251 (Mar. 23, 2023) (“BE Plan 1 Order”). ComEd argues that the Commission should reject these proposals and find that the proposed changes to ComEd’s Rider DE are outside the scope of an RDI and better addressed in other forums like ComEd’s rate cases.

ComEd explains that REACT proposes changes to ComEd’s Rider CFRA – Carbon-Free Resource Adjustment (“Rider CFRA”), recommending that ComEd increase the frequency of its updates to the Carbon-Free Resource (“CFR”) Adjustment. While the REACT proposal is not contested and should be adopted, the Commission should also determine generally that issues pertaining to ComEd’s Rider CFRA are not related to the allocation of ComEd’s delivery service revenue requirement.

ComEd argues that REACT’s proposal to modify ComEd’s Rider SPWS REBATE – Solar Paired with Storage Rebate (“Rider SPWS”) is both a collateral attack on the SPWS RH Order, and out of scope for this RDI. _See_ Commonwealth Edison Co., Docket No. 21-0812, Order on Rehearing at 24 (Aug. 11, 2022) (“SPWS RH Order”). The proposal should be rejected. ComEd explains that REACT’s proposed changes to ComEd’s Rider SPWS would alter the Commission-approved way ComEd calculates Incremental Interconnection Costs, which are a subset of the costs rebated under Rider SPWS. _See_ REACT Ex. 3.0 at 10, 12. The Commission already explicitly evaluated and rejected REACT’s proposal in the SPWS RH Order. _See generally_ SPWS RH Order. Indeed, the same witness (REACT witness Marhoefer) who offered the testimony at issue in this proceeding offered testimony on behalf of Magid Glove (a member of REACT) in the Rider SPWS proceeding. REACT Ex. 3.0 at 4. Furthermore, costs rebated under Rider SPWS are not part of ComEd’s distribution revenue requirements and the mechanics are not related to ComEd’s delivery service rate design. ComEd Ex. 4.0 at 6. Therefore, ComEd asserts that the Commission should find that REACT’s proposal also falls outside the scope of this delivery service rate design investigation.

ComEd states that CUB/EDF made two proposals that violate the statutory scope of this proceeding and must be rejected. First, CUB/EDF propose that ComEd deduct from its distribution revenue requirement projected deferred distribution system investments that could result from adoption of ComEd’s proposed distribution time of use (“TOU”) (“DTOU”) rate. CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 20-21. According to ComEd, the Commission should reject this proposal because it is not revenue neutral and is therefore outside the statutory scope of Section 16-105.5. _See_ ComEd Ex. 4.0 at 16-17.

Second, CUB/EDF propose that ComEd be ordered to offer a supply TOU (“STOU”) rate. CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 24. CUB/EDF’s proposal asks the Commission to exceed its authority under Section 16-103(e) of the Act, which states that “[t]he Commission shall not require an electric utility to offer any tariffed service other than the services required by this Section.” 220 ILCS 5/16-103(e). Because CUB/EDF’s proposals for a supply TOU cannot be granted by the Commission, and because they are not relevant to the cost allocation of ComEd’s delivery service revenue requirement, the proposals must be rejected as out of scope for this proceeding.

All of these intervenor proposals are out of scope because they are not revenue neutral, are unrelated to the allocation of ComEd’s Commission-approved MYRP delivery service revenue requirement, and/or propose that the Commission exceed its authority. Inclusion of these issues in this proceeding has turned this statutorily focused revenue neutral delivery service allocation proceeding into something much more cumbersome and difficult for all parties to litigate. In its final Order, the Commission should reaffirm the limited scope of this statutory RDI. This proceeding is not a catch-all for any and every concern with a filed rate or rider; it is a proceeding to address the revenue-neutral cost allocation of ComEd’s Commission-approved delivery service revenue requirement.

### B. Staff’s Position

Staff states that pursuant to Section 16-105.5(a) and (c) of the Act, ComEd is required to either propose revenue-neutral tariff changes or refile existing tariffs without changes. In other words, any changes made must be revenue neutral, ensuring that no additional costs are transferred between customer groups.

Staff further states that Section 16-108.18 authorized ComEd to file an MYRP. Specifically, Section 16-108.18(d)(1) provides that, if an electric utility had a performance-based formula rate in effect under Section 16-108.5 as of December 31, 2020, the utility may file a petition proposing tariffs implementing a 4-year MYRP for delivery service rates to be effective for January 1, 2024, through December 31, 2027. Section 16-105.5, pursuant to which this docket was initiated, authorizes a utility to file the RDI petition separate from an MYRP to establish the rate design for the rates established in the MYRP. Thus, this proceeding is limited to evaluating the design of delivery service rates.

### C. City’s Position

The City’s proposals to address ComEd’s unjust and unreasonable administration of Rider NS fall squarely within the confines of this proceeding. The City refutes ComEd’s baseless assertions otherwise, noting that ComEd’s position contravenes the unambiguous language of the Act, prior Commission findings on Rider NS, and ComEd’s position in other proceedings.

The City explains that the Act expressly authorizes the Commission to consider revenue neutral tariff changes related to rate design in this proceeding. 220 ILCS 5/16-105.5(c)(1). Here, the City has persuasively demonstrated that ComEd’s administration of Rider NS is unjust and unreasonable and therefore in need of revision in this case. Thus, the City states that pursuant to its authority under the Act the Commission must act to address this deficiency in this proceeding.

The City argues that ComEd’s unduly narrow read of the scope of this case cannot be squared with the legislature’s directives set forth in P.A. 102-0662, which directs the Commission to holistically consider the impact of all of ComEd’s tariffs and rate design options. The purpose of this rate design investigation must be read in conjunction with the statutory provisions relating to ComEd’s Multi-Year Integrated Grid Plan (“MYIGP”), which provides in part that:

> It is the policy of this State that holistic consideration of all related investments, planning processes, tariffs, rate design options, programs, and other utility policies and plans shall be required. To that end, the Commission shall consider, comprehensively, the impact of all related plans, tariffs, programs, and policies on the Plan and on each other[.]

220 ILCS 5/16-105.17(f)(4) (emphasis added). This mandate to conduct a holistic consideration of all related tariffs further supports the City’s position to revise Rider NS in this case.

The City asserts that the Commission’s prior findings on Rider NS, as well as the record evidence in this case, make clear that issues pertaining to Rider NS are properly within the scope of this rate design investigation. The City cites the Commission’s prior findings that Rider NS “concerns a basic principle of rate design.” Commonwealth Edison Co., Docket No. 05-0597, Order on Reh’g at 68 (Dec. 20, 2006) (citing ComEd Ex. 62.0 at 13-14). The City’s witness further explained in this case how Rider NS implicates cost causation questions:

> Those customers paying for standard service and facilities pay base rate charges. If a customer requires nonstandard service and facilities, then that customer must pay for the costs of such service and facilities that exceed the cost of standard service and facilities. Without the necessary clarity and transparency around the costs recovered under Rider NS, ComEd could be collecting rate base related expenses from individual customers under Rider NS or recovering the costs of Rider NS facilities under other Riders. Furthermore, as Rider NS specifies, a portion of the nonstandard service and facilities could be used for other retail customers (COC Ex. 1.02 at 3), further raising cost causation questions.

COC Ex. 1.0 at 8-9. ComEd witness Perkins confirmed that “Rider NS embodies a cornerstone principle of distribution ratemaking and has for decades” (ComEd Ex. 9.0 REV. at 6) and also explained the relationship between costs recovered under Rider NS and those under base rates. _See id._ at 9 (“[i]n the last five years, the collection of these customer advances has avoided at least $500 million of incremental costs that would have been borne by all customers if they had not been collected from these cost-causers.”).

The City also highlights the Commission’s prior findings that this rate design investigation is the appropriate forum to address revisions to Rider NS. In ruling on arguments pertaining to Rider NS in ComEd’s initial Grid Plan proceeding in the MYRP Docket, the Commission stated that “any concerns regarding the rate design delivery service cost allocation and rate design methodologies shall be considered in ComEd’s forthcoming [Rate Design Investigation] proceeding.” MYRP Docket at 500; _see also_ MYRP Docket, Proposed Order on Refiling at 332 (Oct. 18, 2024) (“[t]he Commission further agrees with ComEd and the City that revenue-neutral modifications to Rider NS should be raised in ComEd’s rate design investigation proceeding.”). The City states that it relied on these findings, and the Commission has no reasoned basis to depart from them here.

Finally, the City notes that ComEd’s argument that Rider NS tariff revisions are outside of the scope of this proceeding directly contradicts its position in other proceedings. ComEd’s position in the Refiled Grid Plan proceeding is that “changes to tariff language and rate design, if revenue neutral, would be properly addressed in the pending rate design investigation.” MYRP Docket, Proposed Order on Refiling at 332. Thus, the City makes clear that Rider NS issues are appropriately within the scope of this proceeding, and the Commission has an obligation to resolve these concerns in this docket.

### D. CUB/EDF’s Position

It appears to CUB/EDF that in rate design proceedings under Section 16-105.5, the intent of the General Assembly is to pursue the policy objectives of the rate plan and performance metric statute (Section 16-108.18), which must be consistent with the grid plan statute (Section 16-105.17). Under Section 16-105.17(f)(4), it is the policy of the State that “holistic consideration of all related investments, planning processes, tariffs, rate design options, programs, and other utility policies and plans shall be required.” 220 ILCS 5/16-105.17(f)(4). CUB/EDF thus conclude that this rate design proceeding must proceed with the purpose of accomplishing the policy directives of the comprehensive performance metrics, grid plan, and rate plan statutes of Sections 16-105.17 and 16-108.18.

To achieve the same policy objectives in this rate design proceeding, CUB/EDF assert the Commission should consider its authority in those performance metric and grid planning statutes, which include authority to consider TOU rates for both delivery and supply. 220 ILCS 5/16-108.18(e)(2)(A)(v). Similarly, the Commission’s authority includes consideration of “time-variant or dynamic pricing programs.” 220 ILCS 5/16-105.17(f)(4)(A). And Public Act (“P.A.”) 102-0662 is designed to require “rate design options, technologies or programs that facilitate load flexibility.” 220 ILCS 5/16-105.17(f)(2)(K). CUB/EDF argue TOU rates and load flexibility are important tools in pursuing the General Assembly’s finding that Illinois must “reduce energy usage generally and especially during times of greatest reliance on fossil fuels, and enhance customer engagement opportunities.” 220 ILCS 5/16-105.17(a)(2). P.A. 102-0662 further states the Commission should similarly encourage efficient energy use. 220 ILCS 5/16-105.17(a)(6). And finally, P.A. 102-0662 provides that the Commission should consider rate designs that “enable greater customer engagement, empowerment, and options for energy services.” 220 ILCS 5/16-105.17(d)(4).

In its Order approving ComEd’s Rate Residential Time of Use Pricing Pilot (“RTOUPP”), the Commission recognized that TOU pricing for bundled electric service achieves many goals set forth in P.A. 102-0662, including load flexibility, reduced greenhouse gas emissions, customer value and choice, and improved competition:

> The Commission further finds that Rate RTOUPP will make the benefits of time-variant rates available to residential customers in a manner that involves less price risk than hourly or real-time pricing. The time-sensitive rates will be based on historic prices which will be designed to encourage users to shift their load. Load-shifting has the potential to lower individual’s bills, benefit the grid by promoting grid resilience, benefit the environment through [Greenhouse Gas] reduction, positively impact distribution system investments, and provide for flexible rate design while promoting value, choice and competition.

Commonwealth Edison Co., Docket Nos. 18-1725/18-1824 (Consol.), Order at 8 (Oct. 2, 2019) (emphasis added) (“RTOUPP Order”). In this docket, CUB/EDF witness McCann also testified that a TOU rate could accomplish legislative goals of peak load flexibility, reduced and efficient energy usage, increased customer engagement, and customer options. CUB-EDF Ex. 1.0 at 9-11.

Finally, the Illinois General Assembly instructed the Commission not to approach rate design cases under P.A. 102-0662’s Section 16-105.5 like it did under formula rates law Section 16-108.5. P.A. 102-0662 found that processes and outcomes under the formula rates law were not sufficiently transformative in urgently moving utilities to the State’s energy policy goals and may have resulted in excess utility spending and guaranteed profits without meaningful improvements in customer experience, rate affordability, or equity. 220 ILCS 5/16-108.18(a). Rate design proceedings under the formula rates law, Section 16-108.5, limited rate design changes to “performance-based formula rates.” 220 ILCS 5/16-108.5(e). In contrast, Section 16-105.5 contains no limiting language, leading CUB/EDF to conclude it provides general and relatively unlimited authority to explore rate design issues so long as they are revenue neutral:

> The electric utility’s separate revenue-neutral rate design filing may either propose revenue-neutral tariff changes or refile the existing tariffs without change, which shall present the Commission with an opportunity to suspend the tariffs and consider revenue-neutral tariff changes related to rate design.

220 ILCS 5/16-105.5(c) (emphasis added). The Commission has broad authority to consider utility actions beyond any specific conduct questions in a docket, even a rate case filed under Section 9-201. Ill. Power Co. v. Ill. Com. Comm’n, 382 Ill. App. 3d 195, 203 (2003).

CUB/EDF dispute Staff and ComEd’s interpretation of Section 16-105.5. Both Staff and ComEd argue that this docket is limited to delivery service rates. CUB/EDF contend both ComEd and Staff’s arguments leave out the important regulatory mandates under P.A. 102-0662 to focus on load flexibility and reducing electricity usage during times of fossil fuel reliance. CUB/EDF argue Staff and ComEd also fail to acknowledge the Commission’s broad authority to consider utility actions beyond the specific conduct in question in a docket. Ill. Power Co., 382 Ill. App. 3d 195, 203.

First, CUB/EDF contend ComEd’s reading of Section 16-105.5 is so narrow that it would require the Commission to dismiss large and important sections of ComEd’s own Petition. ComEd takes the position that this case is “limited to revenue-neutral tariff changes to the allocation of delivery service costs among ComEd’s rate classes and changes to the rate design of [Rate MRPP’]” ComEd IB at 5. Yet, CUB/EDF note, ComEd’s own Petition ventures beyond Rate MRPP, asking for Commission approval to change Rate Basic Electric Service Hourly Pricing (“BESH”), Rate Retail Delivery Service (“RDS”), Rate RESS (Retail Electric Supplier Service), and Rider DG (Distributed Generation) Rebate. ComEd IB at 56-57 citing ComEd Exs. 3.01 and 6.01. ComEd also proposes a new Rider Electric Vehicle Elective Notification (“EVEN”), which CUB/EDF point out, again, is not Rate MRPP. Therefore, CUB/EDF contend, ComEd’s argument on the scope of this proceeding is inconsistent with ComEd’s own Petition. CUB/EDF urge the Commission to reject ComEd’s approach.

Second, ComEd compares Sections 16-105.5 and 16-108.5, and argues that Section 16-105.5 “mirrors” 16-108.5. As noted above, P.A. 102-0662 states that Illinois’ formula rates law, including Section 16-108.5, was not sufficiently transformative in urgently moving Illinois’ utilities to the State’s energy and climate goals. Section 16-108.5, the formula rate design statute, repeatedly limited rate design issues to “performance-based formula rates.” 220 ILCS 5/16-108.5. P.A. 102-0662’s rate design statute, Section 16-105.5, contains no such limiting language.

Third, CUB/EDF take issue with ComEd’s revenue neutrality argument. Revenue neutrality, explained CUB/EDF witness McCann, requires the Commission to avoid rate designs that would create windfall profits, and it should minimize cost shifting among customers. CUB-EDF Ex. 2.0 at 5. Dr. McCann contended the purpose of implementing TOU rates is to lower overall utility costs. _Id._ Dr. McCann testified that rate design must assign billing determinants accordingly in determining the contributions to revenues of customers on the TOU rate compared to customers not on the TOU rate. _Id._ Assuming the cost of service is equal for both sets of customers misses the entire goal of TOU rates, in Dr. McCann’s view, and it would result in an overcollection of revenues. _Id._ Despite this, ComEd assumes that customer behavior will not change, which Dr. McCann argued violates these revenue neutrality principles. _Id._ CUB-EDF argue their proposal addresses the flaws in ComEd’s approach and consequently is revenue neutral. _Id._

Fourth, CUB/EDF contend Staff’s reading that Section 16-105.5 is limited strictly to distribution rates as part of the Section 16-108.18 MYRP cannot be correct. The scope of this proceeding includes all rate design issues within the Commission’s authority, with the only limit being that rate designs be revenue neutral. 220 ILCS 5/16-105.5. Rate design decisions here go beyond the MYRP filed by ComEd under Section 16-108.18. For instance, ComEd is required by the Commission’s Order to propose EVEN to incentivize electric vehicle (“EV”) owners to participate in TOU rate offerings. BE Plan 1 Order at 258. That rate design requirement springs from proceedings held under Illinois’ Electric Vehicle Act (“EVA”), 20 ILCS 627/45, and not ComEd’s multi-year integrated grid plan under Section 16-108.18. CUB/EDF argue Staff’s overly narrow reading of the scope of this proceeding is not consistent with Commission practice and prior orders and must be rejected.

Fifth, CUB/EDF argue ComEd’s reliance on Section 16-103(e) is misplaced. CUB/EDF maintain the Commission has authority to consider and require a supply TOU rate in this proceeding. CUB/EDF ask the Commission to find that the issues raised in this case are within the scope of this proceeding and rule accordingly.

### E. Metra’s Position

Metra argues that there is no dispute that the Flow-Through Adjustment issues raised by Metra and CTA fall squarely within the scope of this proceeding. As such, that issue is uncontested.

While Metra was not the target of ComEd’s August 12, 2024 Motion to Strike Portions of the Pre-Filed Direct Testimony of certain REACT, ICEA, and CUB/EDF Witnesses (“ComEd’s Motion to Strike”), ComEd stated in a footnote that among other Intervenors, Metra “submitted testimony that is also beyond the narrow scope of this case.” ComEd Motion to Strike at 2, n.1. The ALJs denied ComEd’s Motion to Strike “because the testimony raises questions of fact that should be resolved based on the weight of the evidence” and because the testimony “allows the Commission to review a complete factual record to serve as a basis for a correct and legally sustainable decision as set forth by 83 Ill. Adm. Code 200.25(a).” Notice of ALJs Ruling (Aug. 27, 2024). Accordingly, Rider NS and Rider DE issues are appropriately raised here.

Expanding further on the ALJs’ ruling, Metra notes that both the Illinois Rules of Evidence and Commission Rules call for a comprehensive and complete evidentiary record. To begin, Illinois Rule of Evidence 401 defines “relevant evidence” as:

> Evidence having any tendency to make the existence of any fact that is of consequence to the determination of the action more probable or less probable than it would be without the evidence.

Ill. R. of Evid. 401 (emphasis added). Under Illinois Rule of Evidence 402 “[a]ll relevant evidence is admissible, except as otherwise provided by law.” Ill. R. of Evid. 402. The Commission adopts an expanded standard, as compared to civil cases in Illinois, for the admissibility of evidence. Section 200.610(b) of the Commission Rules states:

> Evidence - In contested cases, and licensing proceedings, the rules of evidence and privilege applied in civil cases in the circuit courts of the State of Illinois shall be followed. However, evidence not admissible under such rules may be admitted if it is of a type commonly relied on by reasonable prudent persons in the conduct of their affairs.

83 Ill. Adm. Code 200.610(b) (emphasis added). This provision originates from the Rules of Evidence provision in the Illinois Administrative Procedure Act (“APA”). _See_ 5 ILCS 100/10-40(a).

Metra contends that consistent with this expansive standard, the Commission Rules also advocate for an expansive evidentiary record. Section 200.25(a) of the Commission Rules addresses “Standards for Discretion” and states the goal of the hearing process:

> Integrity of the fact-finding process - The principal goal of the hearing process is to assemble a complete factual record to serve as a basis for a correct and legally sustainable decision.

83 Ill. Adm. Code 200.25(a). Section 200.340 states:

> It is the policy of the Commission to obtain full disclosure of all relevant and material facts to a proceeding. Further, it is the policy of the Commission to encourage voluntary exchange by the parties and staff witnesses of all relevant and material facts to a proceeding through the use of requests for documents and information.

83 Ill. Adm. Code 200.340. Therefore, so long as Metra demonstrates that its testimony regarding Rider NS and Rider DE are relevant to the substance of this proceeding, the Commission may consider and weigh the evidence that it presents.

Metra’s arguments on Rider NS and Rider DE are directly relevant to ComEd’s Rate Design. Indeed, ComEd initiated this proceeding by petitioning the Commission “under Section 16-105.5(a) and (c) of the [ ] Act…to approve its proposed revenue-neutral tariff changes and its proposed delivery time of use rate.” ComEd Pet. For Approval of Revenue-Neutral Tariff Changes Related to Rate Design at 1. Section 16-105.5(a) permits ComEd to file the rate design component of its MYRP separately from its underlying MYRP filing. 220 ILCS 5/16-105.5(a).

As REACT correctly notes in its Response to ComEd’s Motion to Strike, Section 16-105.5(c)(1) of the Act enables the Commission to consider “revenue-neutral tariff changes related to rate design” that do not change the amount of revenue ComEd is entitled to collect, and the Commission may change which customers ComEd collects the revenue from or how ComEd collects those costs. Docket No. 24-0378, Verified Response of the Coalition to Request Equitable Allocation of Costs Together to the Motion to Strike of Commonwealth Edison Company at 2–3 (Aug. 19, 2024).

CTA/Metra propose revisions to Rider NS that are “revenue-neutral tariff changes related to rate design,” which would direct ComEd to change how it collects revenues under Rider NS and Rider DE. Namely, the revisions would exempt members of the Railroad (“RR”) Class from “any non-standard service charges or fees” under Rider NS for:

> (i) any line extension by the Company or any design, installation, or construction for facilities that are used to provide charging of electric transit buses, (ii) extensions or additions to support electric rail service, or (iii) design, installation, or construction of facilities that are used to provide charging for battery-powered train sets. CTA/Metra Joint Ex. 1.03 at 1. Instead, ComEd would treat those services as standard services for the RR Class. _Id._ Because CTA/Metra’s proposed revisions to Rider NS only direct ComEd to change how ComEd classifies its service under Rider NS for the RR Class and do not deny ComEd any cost recovery, they are revenue neutral.

CTA/Metra’s proposed revisions to Rider DE would exempt the RR Class from providing a deposit or letter of credit for “any line extension by the Company or the design, installation, or construction of facilities that are used to provide charging of electric transit buses, extensions to service electric trains, or charging for battery-powered train sets.” _Id._ at 3. Metra contends that because this proposed change would only alter ComEd’s procedure for transactions with the RR Class under Rider DE and does not deny ComEd any cost recovery, it is revenue neutral.

ComEd witness Perkins testified that Metra witness Bachman’s testimony on Rider DE and Rider NS is out of scope because “[t]he mechanics and application of [Rider DE and Rider NS] are not related to ComEd’s delivery services rate design” and “[t]hey do not allocate costs of ComEd’s delivery services or its delivery services revenue requirement.” ComEd Ex. 4.0 at 11. Staff agrees with ComEd’s interpretation.

Both parties argue that since ComEd filed a Multi-Year Integrated Grid Plan (“MYIGP”) and an MYRP for delivery service rates, that necessarily means that the rate design under review here only concerns delivery service rates. ComEd IB at 5; Staff IB at 4. However, that limitation is not present in the plain language of Section 16-108.18 and Section 16-105.5 of the Act. Metra observes that Section 16-108.18, the statute through which ComEd files the MYRP, states, in relevant part, that:

> The Commission shall approve tariffs establishing rate design for all delivery service customers unless the electric utility makes the election specified in Section 16-105.5, in which case the rate design shall be subject to the provisions of that Section.

220 ILCS 5/16-108.18(d)(10) (emphasis added). Accordingly, Metra asserts there is no requirement that this proceeding’s rate design review be limited to delivery service rates; rather, the statute requires that the rate design be established for delivery service customers. Metra notes that this interpretation is shared by multiple intervenors in this proceeding; indeed, such intervenors request tariff amendments outside of delivery service rates. This interpretation makes sense given ComEd’s delivery service customers are often subject to rate design issues unrelated to and independent from setting delivery service rates. Section 16-108.18(d)(10) also states that if the utility “makes the election specified in Section 16-105.5,” then “rate design shall be subject to” the provisions of Section 16-105.5. _Id._ Section 16-105.5 does not include a requirement or limitation for rate design to only be related to delivery service rates.

ComEd states that subsections (a) and (c) of Section16-105.5 support its argument that the rate design investigation is “limited to revenue-neutral tariff changes to the allocation of delivery service costs among ComEd’s rate classes and changes to the rate design of [Rate MRPP].” ComEd IB at 5. However, ComEd’s constrained statutory interpretation is not supported by the plain language of such subsections. Subsection (a) of Section 16-105.5 simply permits ComEd to file the rate design component of its MYRP separately from its MYRP:

> An electric utility that files a general rate case pursuant to Section 9-201 of this Act or a Multi-Year Rate Plan pursuant to Section 16-108.18 of this Act may omit the rate design component of such filing and subsequently separately file this component with the Commission, subject to the requirements of subsections (b) and (c) of this Section.

220 ILCS 5/16-105.5(a) (emphasis added). Nothing in subsection (a) constrains the rate design review solely to the rate design of delivery service rates. And, as previously established, Section 16-108.18 does not limit ComEd’s rate design proceeding to only delivery service rates. Nor does Subsection (c) of Section 16-105.5. That provision clearly states that if ComEd elects to make the separate rate design filing, then Section 16-105.5(c) applies. Similarly, the statute does not limit the Commission to consider only issues related to “delivery service rate design” or cost allocation concerning ComEd’s “delivery services revenue requirement.” _See also_ Verified Response of the Coalition to Request Equitable Allocation of Costs Together to the Motion to Strike of Commonwealth Edison Company at 3.

Moreover, Metra contends that ComEd’s and Staff’s arguments directly contradict the Commission’s finding in the MYIGP proceeding regarding the proper case in which to address revisions to the terms of Rider NS and Rider DE. There, Metra and CTA proposed modifications to Rider NS and Rider DE. Metra states that in its Order, the Commission declined to consider those modifications, expressly referring them to this proceeding:

• In response to CTA/Metra’s proposed revisions to Rider NS, the Commission stated that “any concerns regarding the rate design delivery service cost allocation and rate design methodologies shall be considered in ComEd’s forthcoming RDI proceeding.” MYRP Docket, Order at 500.

• Likewise, in response to CTA/Metra’s proposed revisions to Rider DE, the Commission clearly reiterated that “any concerns regarding the rate design delivery service cost allocation and rate design methodologies shall be considered in ComEd’s forthcoming RDI proceeding.” _Id._ at 497. While the Commission’s directive resolves this issue, in that same case, Staff witness Hamidu agreed with the Commission that this proceeding is the appropriate venue for CTA/Metra to present their rate design methodologies. MYRP Docket, Staff Ex. 21.0 RGP at 9 (July 17, 2024). Further, ComEd witness Leick argued that CTA/Metra should address rate design issues in this RDI proceeding. MYRP Docket, ComEd Ex. 61.0 at 3 (Aug. 14, 2023). In particular, Mr. Leick testified that CTA/Metra raised two rate design-related issues: first, that ComEd should use a cost allocation methodology distinct from the Commission-approved embedded cost of service study (“ECOSS”); and second, that Rider NS and Rider DE should be revised. _Id._ at 4, 6. Metra contends that in addition to the Commission’s directive, these statements from ComEd and Staff show that it is entirely appropriate for the Commission to consider revisions to Rider NS and Rider DE in this proceeding.

As such, the Commission should find that Metra’s proposed revisions to Rider NS and Rider DE fall directly within the scope of this proceeding.

### F. REACT’s Position

REACT maintains that ComEd improperly challenged certain issues raised by REACT and other parties as being “beyond the scope” of the instant proceeding. REACT explains ComEd improperly asserts that this proceeding is limited to “the rate design portion of ComEd’s [MYRP]” and “the allocation of Commission-approved delivery service revenue requirement[.]” ComEd IB at 5. REACT notes that ComEd filed a motion to strike testimony earlier during this proceeding raising the identical issues. Code 200.25(a).” _Id._

REACT explains that ComEd’s arguments are contrary to the plain meaning of the statute and are an improper and untimely collateral attack on the ALJs’ prior ruling in the instant proceeding.

As an initial matter, REACT notes that ComEd mischaracterizes the testimony that was presented. Specifically, ComEd asserts that issues related to shifting delivery service revenue to supply revenue to reflect the utilization of ComEd customer call centers is outside the scope of this proceeding because it is not “revenue neutral”. _See_ ComEd IB at 6. However, REACT is not advocating that ComEd should collect less revenue; rather, REACT’s position is that ComEd should recover supply-related costs from its supply customers. _See_ REACT Ex. 6.0 at 20-24. REACT observes that this language empowers the Commission in this proceeding to consider all “revenue-neutral tariff changes related to rate design.” _Id._ (emphasis added). That is, although the Order in this case may not alter the amount of revenue ComEd is entitled to collect, it may change which customers ComEd collects that revenue from (e.g. residential, small commercial, large and extra-large load customers) or how it collects those costs (e.g. customer charge, demand charge, energy charge). These are the types of issues that the Commission historically addressed in the “rate design” portions of ComEd’s general rate cases. _See, e.g.,_ Commonwealth Edison Co., Docket No. 01-0423, Order at 138-154 (Mar. 28, 2003); Commonwealth Edison Co., Docket No. 05-0597, Order at 176-272 (July 26, 2006).

REACT explains that it would be improper for the Commission to read limitations into the Act as requested by ComEd. _See, e.g.,_ People v. Glisson, 202 Ill. 2d 499, 504 (2002) (“where a statute is clear and unambiguous, courts cannot read into the statute limitations, exceptions, or other conditions not expressed by the legislature”).

Although those are examples of the types of rate design issues that may be addressed, there is nothing in the statute that suggests that the Commission is limited to considering only those issues. Rather, the statute broadly references “changes related to rate design,” refers generally to “tariffs,” not just a single rate, and is not limited to only “delivery services” rate design. 220 ILCS 5/16-105.5(c)(1). The plain language of the Act makes it clear that it is appropriate for the Commission to address the rate design of rates and riders, including but not limited to Rate MRPP.

Second, REACT observes that the statute does not prohibit the Commission from revisiting previously issued rulings regarding rate design, what ComEd refers to as “collateral attacks.” _See, e.g.,_ ComEd IB at 8. Rather, the statute expressly contemplates that the Commission will consider “revenue-neutral tariff changes” to rates that the Commission previously approved. 220 ILCS 5/16-105.5(c)(1).

Moreover, ComEd provides no reason for why the General Assembly would have limited the scope of the Commission’s authority as ComEd proposes or why the Commission should read into the statute limitations that would tie the Commission’s hands and prevent it from considering issues that parties have raised, addressed and fully briefed. REACT maintains that the result ComEd seeks is directly contrary to the way in which the Commission is to exercise its discretion. _See_ 83 Ill. Adm. Code 200.25. To remove these issues into separate proceedings as ComEd proposes, with fewer parties to review the testimony and arguments set forth would undermine the fact-finding process and be less expeditious, less convenient and more costly.

Finally, REACT explains that ComEd’s argument also is an improper attack on the prior ALJs’ decision that ComEd did not appeal. ComEd did not request an interlocutory review of that decision, making ComEd’s arguments on this issue in its briefs untimely and improper. _See_ 83 Ill. Adm. Code 200.520.

### H. Commission Analysis and Conclusion

In this proceeding ComEd elected to file the rate design component of its MYRP, as permitted under Section 16-105.5 of the Act. The Commission maintains that its authority under Section 16-105.5 is limited to consideration of revenue-neutral changes to the rate design and allocation of costs among ComEd’s rate classes, including possible changes to the rate design of Rate MRPP and other delivery services tariffs.

While cognizant of these constraints, the Commission examines specific proposals ComEd alleges are outside the scope. For reasons specific to these proposals, the Commission finds that additional rate design-related issues should be addressed in this proceeding. The bases for examining these issues are discussed in the relevant sections of this Order. Broadly, review of such issues is supported by consideration of: administrative efficiency, including conservation of already strained time and resources; prior directives to address specific issues in this proceeding; and P.A. 102-0662’s mandate that the impact of ComEd’s tariffs and rate design be holistically considered. 220 ILCS 5/16-105.17(f)(4). Reasonable anticipation of new services and tariffs in furtherance of P.A. 102-0662’s objectives support more inclusive rate design proceedings.

## IV. SUPPLY-DELIVERY REVENUE REQUIREMENT ALLOCATION

### A. Proposed Movement of Delivery Costs to Supply

#### 1. ICEA/RESA’s Position

ICEA/ RESA strongly disagree with the statement or implication that the allocations addressed by ICEA/RESA witnesses Lacey and Giacchino allocate ComEd’s revenue requirement. According to ICEA/RESA, Mr. Lacey and Dr. Giacchino recommend allocation of costs between supply and delivery. ICEA/RESA further contend that neither Mr. Lacey nor Dr. Giacchino seek to modify ComEd’s capital investments, nor ComEd’s rate of return.

ICEA/RESA counter that as the Illinois Supreme Court identified, costs are only one component of the revenue requirement:

> In establishing the rates that a public utility is to charge its customers, the Commission bases the determination on the company's operating costs, rate base, and allowed rate of return. A public utility is entitled to recover in its rates certain operating costs. A public utility is also entitled to earn a return on its rate base, or the amount of its invested capital; the return is the product of the allowed rate of return and rate base. The sum of those amounts—operating costs and return on rate base—is known as the company's revenue requirement. The components of the ratemaking determination may be expressed in the classic ratemaking formula R (revenue requirement) = C (operating costs) + Ir (invested capital or rate base times rate of return on capital).

Citizens Utils. Co. of Ill. v. Ill. Com. Comm'n, 124 Ill.2d 195, 200-201 (1988) (internal quotes omitted). ICEA/RESA argue that the only question raised by Mr. Lacey and Dr. Giacchino is where portions of “C” are recovered — through supply or through delivery. ICEA/RESA note that nothing in this fundamental formula has changed. ICEA/RESA note that if the Commission believes removing capital costs to preserve revenue neutrality, ICEA/ RESA believe the Commission has the factual record and authority to do so.

Accordingly, ICEA/RESA continue, the Commission should view this common outline heading for what it is — an administrative convenience to organize issues to be decided by the Commission. ICEA/RESA argue that the common outline heading should not be read as making a substantive conclusion as to the various parties’ arguments organized underneath it. Indeed, ICEA/RESA conclude, they strongly disagree that revenue requirements are being allocated in this docket.

ICEA/RESA note that the Commission set out the foundational principal for the approach of ICEA/RESA witnesses Lacey and Giacchino in the 2013 RDI Order:

> The Commission agrees that ComEd is entitled to collect its customer care costs and entitled to a profit over and above those costs, but pursuant to long established policy, costs are to be collected from the cost causers, who in this case may include supply customers.

2013 RDI Order at 57. ICEA/RESA contend that there is no dispute in this docket whether ComEd’s costs are recoverable, meaning it is revenue neutral. ICEA/RESA contend that the relevant question is from whom, which includes to ComEd supply customers — as the Commission pointed out in the 2013 RDI Order, a revenue-neutral allocation docket under a predecessor statute to Section 16-105.5. ICEA/RESA highlight that, as the Commission held in the 2013 RDI Order, a revenue-neutral allocation docket under a predecessor statute, ComEd must recover its costs from the customers that cause them:

> ComEd also argues that because this is a proceeding to determine delivery service costs it would [be] improper to reduce the overall assessment of costs. However, if a fraction of customer service costs are attributable only to supply customers, they should be collected from supply customers and not through delivery service tariffs. ComEd can recover its costs, just not from the wrong customers.

2013 RDI Order at 57 (emphasis added).) Thus, ICEA/RESA conclude, the Commission can and should address ICEA/ RESA’s arguments on their merits.

In support of their request for reallocation between supply and delivery, ICEA/RESA demonstrate why the Commission must find, based on the record, that: (1) ComEd is currently over-allocating to delivery and under-allocating to supply; (2) this misallocation harms ratepayers, industries including the renewables, efficiency, and retail supply, and the State’s policy goals; and (3) ICEA and RESA have presented a fair allocation that ComEd must adopt.

On exceptions, ICEA/RESA provide an alternative reallocation of costs if the Commission decides that allocating costs from delivery to supply is not revenue neutral. ICEA/RESA contend there is sufficient basis in the record to exclude capitalized costs identified by ICEA/RESA witness Giacchino. This alternative proposal would reallocate $343 million to supply services.

##### a. ComEd is currently over-allocating to delivery and under-allocating to supply.

ICEA/RESA first note that ComEd, through separate proceedings, receives permission to recover a vast array of costs, from operational expenses to capital investments and many categories in between. However, according to ICEA/RESA, while ComEd is a single entity, ComEd essentially operates two Commission rate-regulated businesses: a delivery utility and a supplier to default supply customers. ICEA/RESA note that while ComEd has the exclusive right to deliver electricity to “retail customers” in its service area and has franchise agreements to provide delivery service with many municipalities within that area, ComEd provides default supply service on a non-exclusive, optional basis. ICEA/RESA note that default service is the supply component of Rate BES – Basic Electric Service (“Rate BES”) (and Rate BESH – Basic Electric Service Hourly Pricing (“Rate BESH”) for larger customers) that any customer may take. _See_ ICEA/RESA Ex. 1.0 at 4-5.

ICEA/RESA contend that they presented extensive evidence in this docket on the proper allocation of ComEd’s costs between the supply and delivery functions. ICEA/RESA note that witnesses Lacey and Giacchino looked at all aspects of ComEd’s service to identify functions that, in part, support default supply. ICEA/RESA argue that while the Commission has several times addressed allocation of “customer care costs” between supply and delivery functions, “[c]ustomer care costs are just a fraction of the total costs to serve default service. Customer care is just one function out of many that provide services that support the provision of default service.” ICEA-RESA Ex. 1.0 at 8; _see id._ at 5-6. Thus, note ICEA/RESA, in this docket, the Commission is being asked to apply cost-allocation concepts it has seen and used many times before to a more complete set of costs than those solely related to customer care.

According to ICEA/RESA, the stakes of proper allocation of costs are massive. ICEA/RESA highlight that Mr. Lacey points out, when viewed as a line of business within ComEd, ComEd’s default service function is a significant enterprise: “the scope of the default service business in 2023 was an approximately 30 billion kilowatt hour (“kWh”) business sold to approximately 3.2 million customers. Over 80% of that load was from residential and small commercial customers. This equates to almost $2 billion in revenue to ComEd.” ICEA/RESA Ex. 1.0 at 8. ICEA/RESA contend that ComEd performs an extensive set of transactions and functions in order to operate this business. _See id._ at 9.

ICEA/RESA note that from general economic theory to utility-specific scholars such as Professor James Bonbright and the National Association of Regulatory Utility Commissioners (“NARUC”), all agree that accurate allocation of costs to different functions (such as supply vs. delivery) are of critical importance. ICEA/RESA highlight that Prof. Bonbright explained that the purpose of rate-regulation is to have — in the absence of competition — rates set to reflect the market price if utilities were subject to competitive forces. _See_ ICEA/RESA Ex. 1.0 at 26-27, 35-36. ICEA/RESA further highlight that NARUC has directed that costs be allocated to causers and even (or especially) if utility cost segments such as default service are considered to be like a competitive service, full costs and value should be reflected in the rate. _See id._ at 27-28, 30-33. ICEA/RESA state that it is also common and prudent practice in the (non-rate regulated) business world to allocate costs accurately between business segments to ensure proper investment incentives and to avoid inefficiencies and incorrect internal signals. _See id._ at 37-41.

However, according to ICEA/RESA, ComEd does not follow these principles. ICEA/RESA emphasize that Mr. Lacey argued:

> Neither ComEd’s proposed distribution rates nor its default service rates are designed to recognize the costs that ComEd would have incurred to offer an identical product competitively. Instead, its rates appear to be designed to capture the “natural business incentive” articulated in the NARUC Guidelines, by subsidizing the competitive product with services from the regulated entity (i.e. distribution rates), with a captive customer base and a guaranteed collection of rates. ComEd’s default service rates are priced at a level that is under-market and the corresponding distribution rates, which are not subject to competitive pressures, are priced above what a fair-market price would yield.

ICEA/RESA Ex. 1.0 at 36-37. ICEA/RESA contend that the result is an over-allocation to distribution and an under-allocation to supply.

More specifically, ICEA/RESA note that Mr. Lacey identified that ComEd arrived at an over-allocation to delivery and under-allocation to supply because its cost categories that address services were primarily directly assigned to delivery rather than allocated between supply and delivery. ICEA/RESA Ex. 1.0 at 21. ICEA/RESA contend that the ultimate result is hundreds of millions of dollars in costs that were 100% directly assigned to the delivery function should have been allocated between supply and delivery based on appropriate allocation factors. _See_ ICEA/RESA Ex. 5.0 at 8.

In response to two ComEd arguments, first, ICEA/RESA note that ComEd argues that because “delivery services” are defined broadly by statute, all costs related to delivery services must be directly assigned to the delivery function. _See_ ComEd IB at 11-12. ICEA/RESA argue that ComEd appears to conflate the definition of “delivery services” in Section 16-102 — related to ComEd’s service obligation to provide “delivery services” under Section 16-103(b) — and a requirement that all costs at all related to delivery services must be directly and exclusively allocated to delivery service customers. ICEA/RESA counter that the Act provides no such requirement. To the contrary, ICEA/RESA respond, in Docket No. 14-0312, the Commission rejected a ComEd “switching study” approach to allocating customer care costs, which only attributed ComEd’s perceived marginal cost savings due to customer switching as supply related. _See_ Commonwealth Edison Co., Docket No. 14-0312, Order at 103 (Dec. 10, 2014). ICEA/RESA continue that the Commission also noted the same inconsistency in rejecting ComEd’s proposed allocation of $0 from customer care costs to supply in the 2013 RDI Order:

> The Commission notes that ComEd’s analyses on all other cost issues are presented as embedded cost studies. On this issue, it has repeatedly emphasized avoided cost studies. It has invariably concluded on the basis of these avoided cost studies that even when there were only a relative handful of delivery service customers, no customer care costs were allocable specifically to its supply customers.

2013 RDI Order at 56. ICEA/RESA argue that consistent with its criticisms and conclusions in the 2013 RDI Order and Docket No. 14-0312, the Commission should once again reject ComEd’s marginal cost approach and instead adopt ICEA/RESA’s allocation that takes an embedded (average) cost of service approach to properly allocate ComEd’s costs to its supply and delivery functions.

ICEA/RESA further note that ComEd argued that ICEA/RESA’s model is flawed because it does not assign costs directly to alternative retail electric supplier (“ARES”) customers even though ComEd receives substantial revenue from or on behalf of ARES customers for the services that ComEd provides. ICEA/RESA state that ComEd’s argument lacks merit. ICEA/RESA respond that ComEd admitted in discovery that ARES already pay for services such as Rider PORCB – Purchase of Receivables with Consolidated Billing (“Rider PORCB”) — assigning those costs for recovery again from ARES customers would be requiring double payment. _See_ ICEA/RESA Ex. 1.04. ICEA/RESA further respond that Mr. Lacey correctly identified in testimony that Rider PORCB revenue should be excluded from the revenue allocator. _See_ ICEA/RESA Ex. 4.0 at 20.

Second, and relatedly, ICEA/RESA note that ComEd cites to criticisms in ComEd witness Leick’s direct testimony regarding Dr. Giacchino’s allocation model. _See_ ComEd IB at 12-13. ICEA/RESA respond that both Mr. Lacey and Dr. Giacchino addressed Mr. Leick’s criticisms in rebuttal testimony — frequently accepting in the spirit of narrowing issues ComEd witness Leick’s representations for the purposes of this docket in the absence of additional information. _See, e.g.,_ ICEA/RESA Ex. 4.0 at 5-9; ICEA/RESA Ex. 5.0 at 2-4. ICEA/RESA highlight that where Mr. Lacey or Dr. Giacchino did not agree, they provided substantive responses. _See, e.g.,_ ICEA/RESA Ex. 4.0 at 10-15; ICEA/RESA Ex. 5.0 at 3-5. ICEA/RESA state that ComEd does not cite at all to how Mr. Leick responded to Mr. Lacey’s or Dr. Giacchino’s issue narrowing or substantive responses. Instead of critiquing the particular allocators proposed by Dr. Giacchino or providing a basis for excluding additional categories from embedded cost allocation, ICEA/RESA point out that Mr. Leick takes an all-or-nothing position that offers only blanket opposition to Dr. Giacchino’s proposed allocation. _See_ ComEd Ex. 11.0 at 30-31. ICEA/RESA conclude that the Commission has rejected similar all-or-nothing

arguments from ComEd in allocating customer care costs many times over, continually rejecting the premise that no customer care costs were attributable to the supply function.

ICEA/RESA assert that ComEd simply cannot justify directly assigning virtually all of its costs to delivery services, even though those costs also support ComEd’s supply function. According to ICEA/RESA, the Commission should thus find that ComEd over-allocated to delivery and under-allocated to supply.

##### b. The consequences of ComEd’s over-allocation include impeding Illinois’ policy goals favoring expansion of distributed generation and uptake of time-variant supply options.

ICEA/RESA note that contrary to its assertions in its opening Petition, ComEd is not meeting the Commission’s directives “in the best way possible” as it alleges. _See_ ComEd Petition at 1. Instead, ICEA/RESA retort, ComEd’s over-allocation to delivery impedes several policy goals of the General Assembly set out the Act and Illinois Power Agency (“IPA”) Act. ICEA/RESA highlight Mr. Lacey’s explanation that:

> [C]omEd’s proposed under allocation of costs to default service undercompensates net metering customers and subscribers to community renewable generation projects and also artificially make time-variant products (offered by ARES) and time-variant supply rates (offered by ComEd) to appear less attractive. As a result, these changes have not met the Commission’s directives in “the best way possible.”

ICEA/RESA Ex. 1.0 at 11. According to ICEA/RESA, each of the concerns raised by Mr. Lacey directly and adversely impacts customers, the renewable industry, the efficiency industry, and the state’s policy goals — all while allowing ComEd to recover the same costs as it would have with a proper allocation.

First, ICEA/RESA argue that Mr. Lacey demonstrated that when the supply rate is artificially lowered, residential and small commercial customer with net metered behind-the-meter generation eligible for supply-only net metering are undercompensated for their exports to ComEd’s distribution system. _See_ ICEA/RESA Ex. 1.0 at 12. ICEA/RESA continue that P.A. 102-0662 requires the IPA to procure at least 242 megawatts of distributed renewable energy generation devices powered by solar photovoltaics per year statewide through 2030; artificially suppressing supply net metering value makes achieving those goals unnecessarily more difficult. _See_ Docket No. 23-0714, Final 2024 Long-Term Renewable Resources Procurement Plan (“LTRRPP”) at 159 (Apr. 19, 2024); 20 ILCS 3855/1-75(c)(1)(C) (new solar procurement requirements from the Adjustable Block program); 1-75(c)(1)(K)(i)-(ii) (requiring at least 40% of the Adjustable Block Program to be from distributed renewable generation devices powered by solar photovoltaics).

Second, ICEA/RESA further argue that Mr. Lacey explained that subscribers to a community renewable generation project receive bill credits at the “price to compare” (i.e. default supply) rate, so under-allocation to supply means that subscribers receive fewer bill credits than they are entitled to receive. _See_ ICEA/RESA Ex. 1.0 at 12. ICEA/RESA similarly argue that P.A. 102-0662 requires the IPA to procure at least 174 megawatts of community renewable generation projects powered by solar photovoltaics per year through 2030; unfairly reducing bill credit values from which subscribers derive benefits

benefit and making development riskier. _See_ Docket No. 23-0714, Final 2024 LTRRPP at 159; 20 ILCS 3855/1-75(c)(1)(C) (new solar procurement requirements from the Adjustable Block program); 1-75(c)(1)(K)(iii) and (v) (requiring at least 35% of the Adjustable Block Program to be from community renewable generation devices powered by solar photovoltaics).

ICEA/RESA highlight that Mr. Lacey explained that undermining deployment of distributed energy resources such as behind-the-meter solar or community solar impedes the energy transition contemplated by P.A. 102-0662 that seeks to retire fossil-based generation and replace it with new renewable generation — with a large portion of it net metered solar. _See_ ICEA/RESA Ex. 1.0 at 14. ICEA/RESA continue that federal policies supporting distributed energy resources such as Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (“FERC”) Order 2222 are further undermined by artificially suppressing the value of default supply and thus disincentivizing deployment of new distributed energy resources. _See id._ at 14-16.

In contrast, ICEA/RESA respond, ComEd witness Leick argues the opposite, suggesting that increasing allocation to supply (and in equal amount away from delivery) would subsidize net metering customers and subscribers. _See_ ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 48. In response, ICEA/RESA note that Mr. Lacey contended:

> Given that the General Assembly has spoken quite clearly in the net metering statute (16-107.5 of the Public Utilities Act), it is a mere mechanical calculation to take the supply retail rates — which both ComEd and I expect to increase based on accurately allocated costs — and translate them to net metering benefits. It is not for me or anybody else to question the General Assembly’s policy decisions in this venue. All I know is the answer is not to artificially deflate retail supply rates by misallocating what should be supply-related costs to the delivery function. Today Rider POGNM [Purchase of Generation Net Metering] and POGCS [Parallel Operation of Retail Customer Generating Facilities Community Supply] customers are not being compensated for a true value of energy but that will change with an accurate allocation of costs to the supply function.

ICEA/RESA Ex. 4.0 at 24-25. ICEA/RESA respond that the Commission should not be fooled by an attempt to turn ComEd’s undermining of the State’s policy goals on its head by accepting ComEd’s self-serving proposition that accurate allocation is a subsidy.

Third, ICEA/RESA continue, Mr. Lacey showed that by under-allocating to supply, the fixed-rate Price to Compare artificially appears more attractive and lower priced relative to time-of-use rates or products offered by both ARES and ComEd. _See_ ICEA/RESA Ex. 1.0 at 13. This in turn, ICEA/RESA posit, disincentivizes customer switching to (and encourages customer switching away from) time-of-use rates and other products. ICEA/RESA argue that the General Assembly recently passed P.A. 103-237 that provided foundational data access in support of ARES time-variant products and services, while the General Assembly enacted Section 16-107 of the Act to require ComEd and Ameren Illinois Company d/b/a Ameren Illinois (“Ameren”) to offer residential real-time pricing. ICEA/RESA argue that ComEd’s under-allocation to supply undermines the goals of these (in the case of P.A. 103-237, very recently effective) requirements and opportunities.

Fourth, ICEA/RESA note that Mr. Lacey explained that any service — from ARES supply service to efficiency and other curtailment or load shifting — that relies on accurate pricing of supply looks artificially less attractive in the face of under-allocation to (and thus artificially low pricing for) default supply. _See_ ICEA/RESA Ex. 1.0 at 13. ICEA/RESA note that the Commission oversees and ComEd administers a massive energy efficiency portfolio. ICEA/RESA argue that all of the values for efficiency and load shifting measures would increase with accurately priced supply rates, while ARES products and services would not be subject to unfair and unmerited competition from an artificially low supply rate.

ICEA/RESA state that it is important to note that the artificially suppressed supply price with all of these adverse practical and policy effects is not a discount or free money for customers. Instead, ICEA/RESA counter, ComEd is simply hiding costs in its delivery function (paid by all customers) that should be allocated to supply function, as explained by Mr. Lacey:

> My proposal, to allocate costs fairly to default service, is also a revenue-neutral tariff change. I do not request a single dime of disallowance of ComEd costs. Instead, my testimony and [ICEA/RESA] witness Dr. Giacchino simply present an approach to accurately allocate costs between supply and delivery functions.

ICEA/RESA Ex. 1.0 at 16. In other words, ICEA/RESA clarify, ComEd’s misallocation between supply and delivery harms customers, the distributed renewable generation industry, the efficiency industry, and state policy goals — all while ComEd recovers all the same costs but simply in a way that disadvantages all of these groups.

On reply, ICEA/RESA contend both ComEd and Staff make some form of rate shock argument. ICEA/RESA respond that in this docket are several entities with statutory obligations to act as consumer advocates (including for residential customers) or that function as advocates for a subset of ComEd’s customers. ICEA/RESA counter that based on their review, none of those entities have raised rate shock concerns. However, ICEA/RESA continue, even if the Commission takes these rate shock concerns at face value, rate shock is a basis for mitigation and using the Commission’s principal of gradualism — not a basis to remain with the status quo.

For instance, ICEA/RESA argue, in Docket No. 10-0467, the Commission rejected arguments that rate shock concerns should prevent increases to cost allocations to certain classes — instead, rate shock meant that increases in allocations should be gradual over time. _See_ Commonwealth Edison Co., Docket No. 10-0467, Order at 264 (May 24, 2011). ICEA/RESA note that neither ComEd nor Staff has proposed rate shock mitigation from ICEA and RESA’s proposed reallocation of costs and has instead suggested no movement of allocations to accurate reflection of cost causation. ICEA/RESA aver that ICEA and RESA have seen many instances of the Commission addressing rate shock arguments and the Commission ordering mitigants, but to the knowledge of ICEA and RESA the Commission has not rejected even a proposed rate increase (rather than impose mitigations) due to rate shock.

Second, ICEA/RESA contend that ComEd makes a specious argument that ARES will receive a windfall with increased costs, particularly for a specialized product where the ARES charges the same as ComEd. ICEA/RESA note that the perception of a windfall was debunked:

> ICEA and RESA note that if a product or service has a large profit margin, competitors will be able to offer competing products with less of a profit margin. In other words, if an ARES product tied to Rate BES was no longer competitive in the marketplace, ICEA and RESA as a general matter would expect customers (or in this case municipalities — local governments with access to brokers and consultants) to comparison shop for better pricing. Furthermore, under 815 ILCS 505/2EE(c)(9), all residential and small commercial retail customers may terminate their ARES contract with no early termination fee.

ComEd Ex. 10.07 at 3. ICEA/RESA argue that ComEd incorrectly interpreted this response as supporting its windfall theory (see ComEd Ex. 10.0 at 37) when in reality it explains why customers are likely to see the competitive market offer savings compared to the (properly allocated) supply price.

ICEA/RESA conclude that neither the rate shock argument nor ComEd’s specious ARES windfall argument address the negative impact ComEd’s misallocation have on net metering customers, customers considering or implementing energy efficiency, or customers considering or on TOU rates or ARES products. ICEA/RESA argue that ComEd does not — because it cannot — rebut that its misallocation directly undermines the goals of P.A. 102-0662 and its ambitious clean energy goals from deployment of solar to expansion of energy efficiency to uptake of TOU supply options.

ICEA/RESA urge the Commission to thus find that ComEd’s misallocation causes harm to customers and the policy goals of the State of Illinois.

##### c. ICEA/RESA witness Giacchino’s allocation factors fairly allocate between supply and delivery.

ICEA/RESA argue that the Commission should adjust ComEd’s supply-delivery allocation to fairly allocate costs and avoid the harms from its current under-allocation to supply, which the Commission can do by adopting ICEA/RESA witness Giacchino’s allocation methodology. ICEA/RESA highlight that Dr. Giacchino, in collaboration with Mr. Lacey, identified the universe of cost categories (updated after ComEd provided additional information in the rebuttal testimony of Mr. Leick) that impact both supply and delivery and a fair allocator for each category based on the type of cost. ICEA/RESA continue that Dr. Giacchino provided his model and results using available data (as opposed to ComEd’s actual future costs), and the Commission should direct ComEd to apply the allocation approach identified by Dr. Giacchino.

ICEA/RESA highlight that according to Dr. Giacchino, ComEd, generally, did not allocate costs between supply and delivery services. _See_ ICEA/RESA Ex. 2.0 at 3. ICEA/RESA set out that in order to create a proper allocation in his direct testimony — reflected in ICEA/RESA Ex. 2.03 — Dr. Giacchino took the following steps:

• First, Dr. Giacchino reviewed ComEd Ex. 2.01 (ComEd’s 2026 test year base ECOSS).

• Second, Dr. Giacchino used switching statistics from the Commission’s Office of Retail Market Development to create a “customers” allocator and used responses from ComEd in discovery regarding its 2022 revenues to create a revenue allocator. _See_ ICEA/RESA Ex. 2.0 at 5; _see also_ ICEA/RESA Ex. 2.03.

• Third, Dr. Giacchino applied the allocator data to ComEd’s ECOSS, specifically applying the number of customers allocators to Uniform System of Accounts (“USoA”) accounts that in Dr. Giacchino’s judgment are “reasonably considered a fixed cost from an economic point of view” and the revenue allocator to USoA accounts that in Dr. Giacchino’s judgment are “reasonably considered a variable cost from an economic point of view.” _See_ ICEA/RESA Ex. 2.0 at 5-6.

• Fourth, Dr. Giacchino recalculated the cost of service for each customer class. _See id._ at 6.

ICEA/RESA argue that in rebuttal testimony, Dr. Giacchino updated his model to reflect: (1) ComEd’s updated ECOSS in ComEd Ex. 5.03; and (2) updated 2023 revenue from ComEd’s Customer Care Supply Allocation (“CCSA”) Study. _See_ ICEA/RESA Ex. 5.0 at 2. ICEA/RESA point out that Dr. Giacchino also made several corrections based on hard-coded (i.e. non-formulaic) entries in ComEd Ex. 2.01 that he later caught or were pointed out by ComEd witness Leick. _See id._ at 2, 5. ICEA/RESA further point out that because Dr. Giacchino and Mr. Lacey were not able to conduct an independent study to confirm or refute, Dr. Giacchino for the purposes of modeling accepted several representations by ComEd witness Leick about the nature of costs that ComEd had placed in certain USoA. _See id._ at 3-4; _see also_ ICEA/RESA Ex. 4.0 at 5-7. ICEA/RESA argue that these changes were reflected in Dr. Giacchino’s updated model, ICEA/RESA Ex. 5.01. ICEA/RESA state that these changes and modifications reflect the extent of the good-faith efforts by Dr. Giacchino to provide accurate and fair allocation, rather than simply maximizing allocation to one category over another.

According to ICEA/RESA, it is critically important that the Commission recognize that the allocation approach proposed by Dr. Giacchino is designed specifically to not only reflect accurate allocations for a snapshot in time but to also ensure the allocations remain fair. ICEA/RESA Ex. 1.0 at 22. ICEA/RESA argue that the Commission previously expressed concerns about allocating costs to default customers during times of high switching, but Dr. Giacchino’s allocators remove those concerns by then allocating more to delivery at times when the number of customers and revenue from supply customers shrink. _See id._ at 6-7. However, ICEA/RESA respond, if the Commission remains concerned, Mr. Lacey proposed an additional mitigation mechanism to add a rider that reconciles the resulting charges from the last supply-delivery allocation with what they would have been (respectively) under an up-to-date allocation. _See id._ at 43-46.

On reply, ICEA/RESA stress the importance of the embedded (average) cost model. ICEA/RESA contend that applying the embedded cost of service methodology proposed by ICEA and RESA in this case and using stand-in values provided by ComEd as placeholders until the Commission issues an order on reopening in the MYRP Docket, Dr. Giacchino identifies costs that should be reallocated from ComEd’s delivery service to its supply service based on cost-causation principles. ICEA/RESA note that amount would then be allocated between Rate BES and Rate BESH ComEd supply customers. ICEA/RESA Ex. 5.0 at 6; ICEA/RESA Ex. 5.0 at 7. ICEA/RESA note that Dr. Giacchino translated the additional revenue from supply per rate class into a per kilowatt-hour impact using recent data for usage for each customer class taking service on ComEd supply (both the default rate and hourly). ICEA/RESA Ex. 5.0 at 8.

ICEA/RESA concede that in a vacuum, these values may appear to be cost increases — but ICEA/RESA counters that they are not. First, ICEA/RESA note, for every additional dollar in costs allocated to supply for a particular customer class, that customer class’s delivery charges are reduced by an equal amount. According to ICEA/RESA, Dr. Giacchino’s model does not modify interclass allocations of costs — only allocations within a customer class between supply and delivery.

Second, ICEA/RESA continue, ComEd argues that “[t]o put in the context of the bill impact, a single-family without electric space heating Rate BESH customer using an average of 615 kWh per month would see a monthly bill increase of over $120.” ComEd IB at 13 (emphasis in original). ICEA/RESA state that even accepting ComEd’s assertion that 615 kWh per month is average and ignoring the benefits from reduced delivery charges, Dr. Giacchino addressed this very issue and identified its root cause as ComEd’s ECOSS. ICEA/RESA Ex. 5.0 at 8-9. Thus, ICEA/RESA note, if the Commission is concerned about the issues raised by ComEd, the Commission need only approve Dr. Giacchino’s alternative allocators.

Accordingly, ICEA/RESA argue that while approximately $492 million may seem like a lot, this revenue would still be collected by ComEd, just from the appropriate customers (or, more specifically, supply customers or delivery customers) that caused the costs to be incurred. ICEA/RESA raise that Dr. Giacchino describes in great detail how he conducted his analysis using the embedded cost of service methodology, and how the Commission could take proactive steps to address any rate shock concerns on top of other potential mitigants.

In this case, ICEA/RESA request that the Commission direct ComEd to use the embedded cost of service methodology proposed by Dr. Giacchino (including, if the Commission prefers, Dr. Giacchino’s alternatives to resolve the perceived bias against residential customers from ComEd’s ECOSS) to allocate costs between its supply and delivery service functions. Then, ICEA/RESA conclude, the Commission should direct ComEd to utilize the allocation factors to the costs approved in the Commission’s Order on Reopening in Docket Nos. 23-0055/24-0181 (Consol.) to allocate costs fairly between supply and delivery functions.

#### 2. ComEd’s Position

ComEd argues that the Commission should reject ICEA/RESA’s proposal to shift nearly $500 million from ComEd’s delivery service revenue requirement to the supply revenue requirement, a proposal that ComEd argues would essentially be a transfer of wealth from customers to retail electric suppliers (“RESs”). _See_ ICEA/RESA Ex. 4.0 at 8. According to ComEd, the net effect of this proposal would be to shift nearly 13% of ComEd’s Commission-approved delivery service revenue requirement to only ComEd-supplied customers. ComEd also notes that, if adopted, ICEA/RESA’s proposal would result in a monthly bill increase of over $120 for a residential (single family; without electric space heat) customer taking supply under ComEd’s hourly supply rate. ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 32. ICEA/RESA do not contest ComEd’s impact calculations. In addition, ComEd argues that ICEA/RESA fail to provide evidentiary support that the proposed cost shifts are actually supply-related or are services provided by RESs, thus failing to adhere to principles of cost causation. ComEd notes that this would unfairly saddle ComEd-supplied customers with RES-attributable supply costs and would result in increased rates for both ComEd-supplied customers and RES customers. ComEd argues that the only group to benefit from this proposal are the RESs, who would experience lower costs for the same service. ComEd also notes that Staff agrees with ComEd that ICEA/RESA’s proposal could lead to “significant rate increases for customers who continue to take supply service from ComEd,” potentially leading to “rate shock” and “further economic hardship, especially for vulnerable populations.” Staff IB at 5. Staff also noted that ICEA/RESA’s proposal constituted an “unsubstantiated reclassification of costs[.]” _Id._

ComEd argues that, as a preliminary matter, ICEA/RESA’s claim that their proposal only seeks to shift “costs” between delivery and supply and does not impact ComEd’s “revenue requirement” is incorrect and fundamentally misunderstands the revenue requirement formula. ICEA/RESA IB at 7-8; ComEd RB at 9. ComEd notes that ICEA/RESA admit that “costs are …one component of the revenue requirement.” ICEA/RESA IB at 7. ComEd argues that any movement of costs therefore alters ComEd’s revenue requirement, and the Commission should reject it outright.

According to ComEd, ICEA/RESA propose to move nearly $500 million from a number of delivery subfunction-related FERC accounts to supply because ICEA/RESA claim that those accounts “were not directly assigned to supply or delivery, [but] are included in ComEd’s [ECOSS] results and which include costs related to the provision of default service.” ICEA/RESA Ex. 1.0 at 19. ComEd notes that ICEA/RESA initially proposed to shift nearly $1 billion of ComEd’s delivery service revenue requirement to supply. _See_ ICEA/RESA Ex. 1.0 at 17 (recommending shift of $924 million). ComEd also notes that ICEA/RESA later reduced this proposed amount to $492 million after ComEd witness Leick noted in rebuttal testimony numerous flaws in ICEA/RESA’s analysis (including ICEA/RESA’s double counting of the Illinois Electricity Distribution Tax and proposal to shift this amount to supply). _See_ ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 51-60; ICEA/RESA Ex. 4.0. at 8. ComEd argues that such a massive amount of erroneous proposed cost shifting raises questions regarding ICEA/RESA’s modeling. ComEd further argues that ICEA/RESA asserted, without support, that the proposed cost shifts are not delivery service and are instead attributable to or related to supply. ICEA/RESA Ex. 1.0 at 18. According to ComEd, the Act defines “delivery services” as “those services provided by the electric utility that are necessary in order for the transmission and distribution systems to function so that retail customers located in the electric utility’s service area can receive electric power and energy from suppliers other than the electric utility, and shall include, without limitation, standard metering and billing services.” 220 ILCS 5/16-102. ComEd argues that all the costs ICEA/RESA propose to shift to supply are necessary for ComEd to deliver power to customers, whether they take supply service from ComEd or RESs. _See_ ComEd 10.0 at 31, 33.

ComEd explains that ICEA/RESA’s proposal identifies several FERC accounts that ICEA/RESA claim are not directly assigned to either supply or delivery, but are allegedly related to the provision of default ComEd-supplied service. ICEA/RESA Ex. 1.0 at 19-21; ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 45-46. ICEA/RESA then propose to allocate those costs, using either a Revenue allocator (derived from the revenues attributable to customers on various ComEd or RES supply rates) or a Customer allocator (derived from the number of customers on various ComEd or RES supply rates). ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 47 (citing ICEA/RESA Ex. 2.0 at 11-12). ComEd argues that ICEA/RESA’s studies are flawed and fail to identify what functions their proposed shifted costs serve and why it would be reasonable to allocate any of the costs for those subfunctions to supply.

ComEd argues that the costs that ICEA/RESA propose to shift go beyond the customer care costs that ComEd already allocates to supply using its CCSA Study and extend into basic distribution equipment. ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 43. ComEd argues that witness Leick explained in extensive testimony that these costs are appropriately allocated to the delivery function. _Id._ at 52-60. According to ComEd, he explains the purpose of each sub-function from which ICEA/RESA proposed to shift costs and provides evidence that they are either not provided by RESs or that their function does not change when customers shift from ComEd supply to RES supply. _Id._

ComEd notes that ICEA/RESA argue that eight cost categories reflecting a “service-oriented classification” were misallocated and should be allocated between delivery and supply. ICEA/RESA IB at 11. These “service-oriented” subfunctions that allegedly have supply-related components are: (1) Shared Distribution Lines (Over 4kV); (2) Secondary Voltage Distribution Lines; (3) Customer Installation – Other; (4) Metering Services; (5) Bill Issue & Processing; (6) Billing – Computation & Data Management; (7) Indirect Uncollectibles; and (8) Customer Information. _See_ ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 30. ComEd argues that ICEA/RESA do not identify the supply-related components of any of the eight cost categories – nor can they – because either no supply-related components exist, or they have already been allocated to supply.

ComEd explains that both Shared Distribution Lines (Over 4kV) and Secondary Voltage Distribution Lines are used to provide delivery service and do not implicate supply services whatsoever. _See id._ at 52-54. Similarly, ComEd explains that Customer Installation – Other relates predominantly to transformers and other facilities located on customers’ property to provide them delivery service. _Id._ at 55-56. ComEd argues that RESs do not provide any of this distribution equipment and the need for this equipment does not change when customers switch between suppliers. _Id._ ComEd argues that ICEA/RESA have offered no evidence why any of these categories are supply-related and none of these costs should be allocated to supply.

ComEd then explains that Metering Services relate to costs for providing, installing, testing, exchanging, and removing metering equipment; installing and maintaining meter communication devices; and reading and processing meter usage data. _See id._ at 56-57. ComEd notes that Bill Issuing and Processing relates to costs for printing and mailing

bills to customers (as well as determining credits provided to RESs that issue single supply and delivery bills to customers). _Id._ at 58-59. ComEd argues that, under the Act, both standard metering and billing services are statutorily defined as “delivery services,” and none of these costs should be allocated to supply. _See_ 220 ILCS 5/16-102.

ComEd then explains that Billing – Computations and Data Management relates to costs for “Customer Accounts” that ComEd already allocates fairly between delivery and supply using the CCSA Study and that no additional costs should be allocated to supply. ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 57-58.

ComEd explains that Indirect Uncollectibles relate to costs incurred to connect or disconnect service and engage in revenue management with customers that may eventually have their bill moved to uncollectible expenses. _Id._ at 57. ComEd argues that these costs do not change when customers shift from ComEd to RES supply and any uncollectible costs for customers on RES supply are already captured under Rider PORCB – Purchase of Receivables with Consolidated Billing. _Id._

Additionally, ComEd explains that Customer Information relates to information to encourage safe, efficient, and economic use of electricity, including safe behavior around electrical equipment, energy efficiency, demand response and dynamic pricing, and customer satisfaction surveys. _Id._ at 59. ComEd argues that this information is not related to supply issues and is targeted at all ComEd’s customers and none of these costs should be allocated to supply. _Id._

ComEd also argues that an even more troubling aspect of ICEA/RESA’s proposal is that their proposed Revenue and Customer allocators contain proportions attributable to retail electricity supplier “RES” customers ranging from roughly 11% to 21%. _See id._ at 47. ComEd notes that ICEA/RESA remain silent on these portions, and because ComEd does not currently have a mechanism to bill RESs or their customers for these amounts, they are, by definition, left in the delivery function that all ComEd’s customers pay. _Id._ at 47-48. ComEd argues that ICEA/RESA is essentially proposing that ComEd-supplied customers shoulder a disproportionate share of delivery costs, and that RES-specific supply costs be included in ComEd’s delivery function and socialized across all its customers. _Id._ According to ComEd, not only is the proposal to shift these delivery service costs to supply fundamentally flawed in this RDI proceeding, but such a result would be patently inequitable.

ComEd also argues that, if implemented, ICEA/RESA’s proposal would likely constitute rate shock for some ComEd-supplied customers. ComEd calculates that supply rates for single-family without electric space heating Rate BES customers would increase by 2.55 cents/kWh and supply rates for single-family without electric space heating Rate BESH customers would increase by 19.77 cents/kWh. ComEd notes that, to put this in the context of the bill impact, a single-family without electric space heating Rate BES customer using an average of 615 kWh per month would see a monthly bill increase of over $15 and a single-family without electric space heating Rate BESH customer using an average of 615 kWh per month would see a monthly bill increase of over $120. _Id._ at 32; _see also_ ComEd Ex. 10.0 at 35 (calculating that the supply rate for the Watt-Hour Delivery Class would have more than doubled under ICEA/RESA’s proposal if it were included in the recently updated October 2024 rate). ComEd notes that ICEA/RESA do not contest ComEd’s rate impact calculations.

Finally, ComEd argues that ICEA/RESA’s proposal would also harm customers served by RESs. This is because certain RESs utilize ComEd’s Price-to-Compare feature – which is a combination of ComEd Rate BES supply and transmission rate – to set their own supply prices. ComEd Ex. 10.0 at 35. ComEd argues that, if ComEd’s default supply rate is increased by ICEA/RESA’s proposal to move $500 million of delivery costs to the supply function, then the price RES customers pay under the Price-to-Compare will increase commensurately with no change in service. _See id._ at 36. In ComEd’s view, the windfall price increase that RESs could command by increasing the Price-to-Compare under ICEA/RESA’s proposal could be captured entirely by the RESs as profit. _See id._ at 36-37; ComEd Ex. 10.07 at 2. Conversely, ComEd argues that RES customers and municipal leaders that entered into municipal aggregation agreements could see increased supply prices in excess of $39 million. ComEd Ex. 10.0 at 36-37 (presenting representative example of estimated additional cost to municipal aggregation participants).

ComEd argues that ICEA/RESA’s proposal only furthers their own interests to the detriment of customers, and that the Commission should reject their BOE that is not supported by the evidentiary record. Further, and aside from whether this issue is outside the scope of this proceeding, ComEd argues that ICEA/RESA’s contention that the Commission “order additional factfinding through the rehearing process” is improper because all the facts necessary to make a determination on this issue were in existence and presented during the course of this proceeding – any alleged shortcoming in their presentation lies entirely with ICEA/RESA.

#### 3. Staff’s Position

Staff states that the Commission should reject ICEA/RESA’s proposal to allocate additional costs to default service because the proposal would change the delivery service revenue requirement and thus the proposal is not revenue neutral. Staff Ex. 5.0 at 21.

Staff explains that “default service” is the utility supply rate charged to a customer who takes supply from ComEd. ComEd allocates some call center services costs, which it refers to as “customer care costs” to default service. ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 30. ICEA/RESA argue ComEd should increase this amount and that over $900 million should be shifted from ComEd’s delivery service revenue requirement to ComEd customers who take default service. ICEA/RESA Ex. 2.0 at 9. However, this proceeding is limited to making revenue-neutral adjustments to ComEd's delivery service rate design. ICEA/RESA’s proposal to shift over $900 million in delivery costs to ComEd’s supply function is not revenue neutral. As ComEd noted, ICEA/RESA appear to recognize this fact. ICEA/RESA witness Giacchino “identified approximately $149 million of capital costs that they believe that ComEd would no longer be able to earn a return, roughly lowering ComEd’s revenue requirement several million dollars.” ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 31. Staff states that any proposed changes from delivery costs to supply charges that would result in a change to the revenue requirement, are not revenue neutral and thus outside the scope of this proceeding.

Moreover, Staff agrees with the policy concerns about ICEA/RESA’s proposal raised by ComEd. Implementation of ICEA/RESA’s proposal could lead to significant rate increases for customers who continue to take supply service from ComEd, because the proposal would decrease delivery service rates for all customers but increase supply rates for only ComEd customers. An increase in supply rates creates potential rate shock for those customers. ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 32. This could lead to further economic hardship, especially for vulnerable populations. In Staff’s opinion, shifting delivery costs to supply in the manner proposed by ICEA/RESA would not only violate the statutory requirement for revenue neutrality in this proceeding, but it could also lead to unfair rate impacts and an unsubstantiated reclassification of costs that could harm supply customers. Staff Ex. 5.0 at 20-21. For these reasons, Staff recommends that the proposal be rejected. _Id_

Staff further states that this proceeding is limited to delivery service rates, but ICEA/RESA’s proposal requires a change to supply rates. Staff explains that the portion of customer care costs assigned to ComEd default service is determined annually pursuant to CCSA, which was approved in ComEd’s 2014 formula rate update, Docket No. 14-0312. ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 33. Changes to the inputs to or results of the allocation study may impact ComEd’s delivery service revenue requirement and thus future MYRP proceedings may be a more appropriate venue to consider this proposal. Staff states that it is also possible the issue could be addressed through a separate investigation docket or through a rider; Staff takes no position on how or where any changes in allocation should be addressed, other than to oppose changes in allocation in this docket that is, by statute, a revenue neutral rate design investigation into ComEd’s delivery service rates.

#### 4. REACT’s Position

REACT explains that certain costs that are related to ComEd’s provision of supply services are being improperly recovered in ComEd’s delivery services rates. _See_ REACT Ex. 6.0 at 20-24. Specifically, REACT has highlighted apparent errors in ComEd’s CCSA Study. To address this misallocation, REACT requests that the Commission order ComEd to allocate costs for ComEd customer care call centers based on: (i) whether the customers using the call center services receive supply from ComEd or an ARES; and (ii) if the topic of calls received by the call centers relate to supply issues.

REACT supports the arguments advanced by ICEA/RESA explaining that customers are harmed when ComEd’s supply rate is artificially lowered. _See_ ICEA/RESA Ex. 1.0 at 14. REACT notes that ICEA/RESA have not proposed disallowing any cost recovery, but rather has requested that all costs be accurately allocated between ComEd’s supply and delivery functions. _See id._ at 12-15. Accurate cost allocation benefits the competitive market and benefits customers, consistent with the direction given to the Commission by the General Assembly. _See_ 220 ILCS 5/16-101A(d) (“The Illinois Commerce Commission should act to promote the development of an effectively competitive electricity market that operates efficiently and is equitable to all consumers.”).

#### 5. Commission Analysis and Conclusion

ICEA/RESA propose to reallocate approximately $492 million from ComEd’s delivery service revenue requirement to supply services. ICEA/RESA contend that ComEd is over-allocating costs to delivery services and under-allocating costs to ComEd’s supply service customers. ICEA/RESA argue that their proposal is revenue neutral and therefore falls within the scope of this proceeding. The Commission disagrees. ICEA/RESA’s proposal alters ComEd’s delivery services revenue requirement and is not revenue neutral. As Staff and ComEd note, even ICEA/RESA appear to recognize this fact when witness Giacchino admits that a capital cost allocated to default supply would not be able to earn a rate of return. ICEA/RESA Ex. 2.0 at 13; ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 30-31. ComEd highlights the Company would no longer be able to earn a return on approximately $149 million of capital costs. ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 31. Accordingly, the Commission finds that ICEA/RESA’s proposal is not revenue neutral, and therefore it is rejected as being outside the scope of this proceeding.

ICEA/RESA’s alternative proposal raised in their BOE is untimely. Further, it is not clear that shifting only the costs identified within the alternative proposal is revenue neutral or appropriate.

Notwithstanding that determination, the Commission is concerned with the potential impacts caused by misallocation between supply and delivery service costs. The Commission finds the possible negative impact on the State’s ability to meet P.A. 102-0662 procurement goals and incentivize participation in renewable programs particularly concerning. The record lacks complete evidence as to the validity of ICEA/RESA’s complaints. Accordingly, the Commission directs Staff to conduct an investigation into the appropriate allocation between supply and delivery service costs and to engage with the Company and interested stakeholders on the appropriate allocation method. Staff shall file a report with the Commission discussing its findings and providing recommended revisions or actions no later than one year of the date of this final Order.

The Commission shares ComEd and Staff’s policy concerns with reallocation leading to substantial increases to ComEd supply customers’ bills and what would inevitably lead to rate shock. Under ICEA/RESA’s revised proposal, a ComEd supply customer’s monthly bill would increase by over $61 for a residential (single family; without electric space heat) customer taking supply under ComEd’s hourly rate. _See_ ICEA-RESA Ex. 5.01, Table “Taking Hourly Price Service from ComEd”. The Commission emphasizes that even if the allocation of supply and delivery costs needs to be remedied, any proposal that, as a result, would increase customer’s costs must be reviewed by the Commission under the relevant ratemaking provisions.

### B. Proposed Revisions to Customer Care Supply Allocation Study

#### 1. ComEd’s Position

ComEd argues that the Commission should reject REACT and ICEA/RESA’s proposals to revise the CCSA Study to shift additional customer care costs from delivery to supply. ComEd claims that REACT’s proposal to revise the CCSA Study lacks any evidentiary support, and its cost shifting proposal is based on an under-developed and

unreasonable allocator, and therefore must be rejected. _Id._ at 3, 27 (referencing REACT Ex. 6.0 CORR.).

ComEd explains that, in ComEd’s 2014 formula rate update case, Docket No. 14-0312, ComEd presented three options to allocate certain customer care costs between the delivery and supply functions. ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 33. The Commission approved the CCSA Study as the means by which ComEd would allocate costs. _Id._ at 34; _see also_ Docket No. 14-0312, Order at 103-104.

The CCSA Study identifies whether any costs change with changes in the level of customers switching between ComEd and RES supply and uses the embedded cost method to allocate the amount of those costs between delivery and supply functions. ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 34. The CCSA Study operates by: (1) compiling current costs recorded in FERC Account 903 – Customer Records and Collection Expenses; (2) compiling call center activities and allocators; (3) computing the call center allocator; and (4) assigning indirect costs. _Id._ at 35-42. ComEd notes that application of the CCSA Study to 2023 call center costs resulted in allocating $10 million of direct costs and $11 million of indirect costs (for a total of $21 million) to the supply function. _Id._ at 43.

ComEd notes that it has presented the CCSA Study, updated with recent data, in its annual rate cases (both formula rate updates and annual reconciliations under Rider DSPR – Delivery Service Pricing Reconciliation). ComEd also notes that Staff and any interested intervenors have had the opportunity to review and comment on it in each filing. _See_ ComEd Ex. 10.0 at 27. In each of the last three CCSA Study updates, ComEd has allocated over $20 million in customer care costs to supply. ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 34-35 (citing updates in 2022 formula rate update case and 2023 and 2024 Rider DSPR reconciliations). ComEd notes that, in this proceeding, Staff supported ComEd’s CCSA Study, found ComEd’s assignment of costs and application of factors to be reasonable, and that ComEd’s explanation addressed the concerns raised by REACT. _See_ Staff Ex. 5.0 at 23.

ComEd argues that REACT claims that, because the CCSA Study was developed and approved during the “formula rates era,” the regulatory structure “prioritized expediency in approving rate increases by not allowing the Commission to formally review costs and cost recovery mechanisms.” REACT Ex. 6.0 CORR. at 22. ComEd argues that REACT is wrong and explains that ComEd updates the CCSA Study every year and any interested party has had the opportunity to review and comment on its customer care supply allocation, regardless of whether it is a formula rate update proceeding or an MYRP proceeding (or their respective annual reconciliations). _See_ ComEd Ex. 10.0 at 27.

ComEd explains that REACT also argues that the CCSA Study fails to allocate costs based on cost causation because it does not track the number of calls received by the call center that were directly concerned with supply-related issues or the number of callers taking supply under ComEd versus a RES. REACT Ex. 6.0 CORR. at 5-7, 23, 26-27. First, ComEd does not have a means of identifying whether a call directly concerns

supply-related issues; indeed, calls will often touch on multiple topics and not necessarily be limited strictly to “supply-related issues.” _Cf._ REACT Cross Ex. 9 (ComEd’s Data Request Response to REACT 6.05(b)(i)-(ii)). Second, there is no indication that just because a RES-supplied customer calls ComEd that their question concerns supply. _See_ ComEd Ex. 10.0 at 28. ComEd argues that neither of these allocators would result in costs being allocated between delivery and supply in a manner consistent with cost-causation.

REACT recommends that the Commission find an alternative to the CCSA Study and, in the interim, utilize a proposed “Average Number of Services” allocator to allocate customer care costs between delivery and supply based on the relative proportions of ComEd and RES-supplied customers. REACT Ex. 6.0 CORR at 23. Under this proposal, REACT identified roughly $61 million of customer care costs it claimed should be allocated to only ComEd-supplied customers. _Id._ at 24.

ComEd argues that REACT’s proposed allocator suffers from two flaws. First, ComEd argues that it assumes – without evidence or explanation – that entire calls related to “Revenue” or “Bill” categories are supply-related based only on the relative percentages of customers taking supply from ComEd versus a RES. ComEd explains that the absence of any evidence that such calls were in fact supply-related means there is no logical connection between the costs and REACT’s proposed allocation. _See id._ at 28.

Second, ComEd argues that REACT’s proposal directly attributes 13.9% of calls to “Customers on Retail Supply,” yet presents no mechanism to assign those costs to RES customers. In ComEd’s view, this would ensure that the portion that REACT assigned as being attributable to RESs would stay in delivery rates and be paid by both RES-supplied and ComEd-supplied customers. _Id._ at 28. ComEd notes that, while REACT downplays this concern, such a crucial detail should not be summarily dismissed and ComEd argues that it is REACT’s burden to present the Commission with a full proposal that could be implemented. ComEd argues that, if REACT’s proposal is adopted, all ComEd customers would unfairly subsidize RES customers.

ComEd argues that the Commission should also reject ICEA/RESA’s proposal to revise the CCSA Study to include additional costs functionalized to supply. ComEd explains that ICEA/RESA criticized ComEd’s CCSA Study and claimed that ComEd: (1) misallocated calls to ComEd’s call center related to start/stop service requests and outage and account history; and (2) included revenue related to neither supply nor delivery in the calculation of its Revenue allocator. ICEA/RESA IB at 19-20. ComEd notes that ICEA/RESA also claim that ComEd utilized a “meaningless” allocator to calculate its Billing allocator. ICEA/RESA IB at 20. However, ComEd notes that, in testimony, ICEA/RESA witness Lacey mentioned this criticism only in passing as an issue that he would have addressed if ICEA/RESA viewed the purpose of this proceeding as seeking to correct the CCSA Study. _See_ ICEA/RESA Ex. 4.0 at 22. ComEd argues that witness Lacey made clear, however, that ICEA/RESA did not view the purpose of this docket as seeking to correct the CCSA Study and did not further elaborate on the critique of ComEd’s Billing allocator as “meaningless.” _Id._ ComEd argues that, because ICEA/RESA expressly disclaimed analyzing this alleged critique in testimony, the Commission should find that ICEA/RESA has waived this issue. _See_ ComEd RB at 15 n.3 (citing Del Real v. N.E. Ill. Reg’l Commuter R.R. Corp., 404 Ill. App. 3d 65, 74 (2010) (holding arguments that are not properly developed are waived). ComEd argues that ICEA/RESA’s critiques are unfounded and misunderstand the nature of the various accounts in FERC Uniform System of Accounts (“FERC Accounts”). _See_ ComEd Ex. 10.0 at 34.

ComEd first argues that it did not misallocate calls to its call center between delivery and supply. ComEd notes that calls made to start and stop service are fundamentally related to delivery service and that they are a request to either deliver electricity or cease delivering electricity. _See_ ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 37 (explaining that calls related to “Moving” are delivery-related). They are not calls related to ComEd’s procurement of electricity supply. ComEd explains that, if a customer places a start service call, but utilizes no electricity supply, ComEd is still obligated to utilize its distribution system to be able to deliver supply. Additionally, ComEd argues that calls related to outages and account history are related to delivery service. _See id._ ComEd argues that outages are overwhelmingly caused by issues with the distribution system, rather than a lack of available supply, so they are properly allocated to the delivery function. Finally, ComEd argues that calls related to account history concern the history of ComEd’s delivery of electricity to customers.

ComEd next argues that ICEA/RESA’s understanding of ComEd’s calculation of its Revenue allocator is incorrect. ComEd explains that ICEA/RESA claimed that ComEd included $970 million in “Other Operating Revenue” from various FERC Accounts (i.e., Account Nos. 450-457.2) that are unrelated to delivery service in the calculation of its Revenue allocator. ComEd explains that it did not include revenue from the aforementioned accounts in the calculation of its Revenue allocator. ComEd argues that the $970 million that ICEA/RESA claim are included in the calculation are not present and produced an analysis matching the revenues it used in its calculation of the Revenue allocator with FERC Accounts other than Account Nos. 450-457.2. _Id._ ComEd also argues that ICEA/RESA’s critique of its inclusion of revenues related to Rider PORCB is unfounded because the Commission approved inclusion of those revenues when it approved the CCSA Study in ComEd’s 2014 formula rate update proceeding. _See id._ at 18 (citing Docket No. 14-0312, Order at 104 (adopting CCSA Study with no mention of striking out Rider PORCB costs)).

#### 2. Staff’s Position

Staff does not oppose using ComEd’s CCSA Study to allocate customer care costs because the study considers the indirect costs raised in REACT’s concern. Staff Ex. 5.0 at 21. Staff proposes such a study being limited to customer service costs that are already included in the delivery service revenue requirement. _See_ Section IV.A for Staff’s position on why Staff opposes the allocation of additional costs to default service as part of this proceeding. REACT raised concerns that the use of the CCSA Study may lead to the potential misallocation of overhead and building-related costs to ARES-supplied customers. REACT Ex. 2.0 at 27. More specifically, REACT witness Pruitt questioned whether ComEd had appropriately included indirect costs, such as those associated with building structures, furnishings, utilities, capital equipment, and depreciation related to call centers, in its allocation of Supply Administration charges. _Id._ If these costs were not properly allocated, REACT argued that ARES customers would bear an undue portion of these expenses through delivery service charges. REACT Ex. 2.0 at 27-28. However, ComEd clarified that its CCSA Study included both indirect and direct costs, including the indirect costs raised in REACT’s concern; therefore, Staff does not find REACT’s proposed changes necessary and is unopposed to ComEd’s use of the CCSA study to allocate customer care costs. ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 63.

#### 3. ICEA/RESA’s Position

ICEA/RESA argue that customer care costs are a subset of the supply-delivery allocation issues presented by ICEA and RESA in the present docket. ICEA/RESA Ex. 1.0 at 8. ICEA/RESA contend that Mr. Lacey testified that allocating only customer care costs would not address the systemic issues with over-allocation to delivery and under-allocation to supply. _See_ ICEA/RESA Ex. 4.0 at 16-17, 22. ICEA/RESA report that Mr. Lacey testified, “the purpose of this docket is not to correct [ComEd’s customer care cost allocation] — flawed as it is — but address the broader misallocation between default service and delivery.” _Id._ at 22. ICEA/RESA argue that the Commission should thus not simply allocate customer care costs but instead should approve the broader allocation between supply and delivery identified in Section IV.A supra.

ICEA/RESA explain that Mr. Lacey testified that massive changes since 2014 (when the Commission first approved the CCSA), including FERC Order 2222, emphasize “the need for more accurate consumer pricing signals.” _Id._ at 23. ICEA/RESA warn that the General Assembly’s ambitious renewable energy targets will be hindered by underpayment for net metering due to supply-delivery misallocation. _See id._ at 23.

ICEA/RESA continue that, in the alternative, if the Commission were to focus on customer care costs alone, the Commission should reject ComEd’s CCSA Study and use the allocation of customer care costs identified by Mr. Lacey and Dr. Giacchino. ICEA/RESA notes that while Mr. Lacey’s critiques of the CCSA “are to further illustrate how and why some of ComEd’s costs have been misallocated” (_Id._ at 22) and Mr. Lacey disagrees with ComEd’s methodology (_see id._ at 21), Mr. Lacey offered several corrections.

First, ICEA/RESA highlight that Mr. Lacey explained that ComEd’s allocation of 2.5 million calls for the call center to delivery and 0.5 million to supply misallocates 500,000 start/stop service calls as delivery (even though the delivery apparatus is already in place and the customer is seeking or ceasing supply) and 400,000 related to outage, account history, and other similar inquiries as purely delivery. _See_ ICEA/RESA Ex. 4.0 at 18-19.

Second, ICEA/RESA continue, in creating a revenue allocator, ComEd adds in $970 thousand of revenue that is neither supply nor delivery (such as transmission, which in Illinois is the obligation of the energy supplier to pay for) as if it is delivery to allocate revenue between supply and delivery. _See_ ICEA/RESA Ex. 4.0 at 19-21. As a result, ICEA/RESA argue, ComEd overestimates delivery revenue and underestimates supply revenue by almost nine percentage points. ICEA/RESA highlight that as Mr. Lacey testified, the proper allocation (within the flawed CCSA framework) is 63.43% delivery- 36.57% supply, not 72.29% delivery-27.71% supply as the CCSA provides. _See id._

### B. Railroad Class Flow-Through Adjustment

#### 1. ComEd’s Position

The RR Class Flow-Through Adjustment is a Commission-directed policy-related adjustment specific to the RR Delivery Class. _See_ Docket No. 10-0467, Order at 274-275 (ordering RR Flow-Through Adjustment for the first time). The adjustment provides a revenue requirement reduction to the RR Delivery for electricity flowing through RR- owned substations to serve non-RR customers in ComEd’s service territory. ComEd Ex. 2.0 at 47.

ComEd notes that it takes no position on whether the Commission should award the RR Class an additional adjustment for prior use of railroad facilities; however, to the extent the Commission elects to order such compensation, ComEd argues that its methodology, presented in testimony, is a reasonable approach to calculate the value and is supported by reliable, publicly available sources over the duration of the prior use. ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 8-9. ComEd argues that, where data was not readily available due to the extended timeframe of the analysis, the values used in estimating the compensation amount are reasonable and appropriate. _Id._ at 9.

In the 2020 RDI, the Commission directed ComEd to “initiate [a] working group, including any additional interested stakeholders, to determine a present dollar value of compensation for prior use of RR facilities, if any, and provide a report, including an explanation for how such amount was developed, in the next rate design investigation proceeding.” 2020 RDI Order at 19. ComEd explains that, per the Commission’s directive, it initiated a series of meetings with interested stakeholders to discuss and determine the present dollar value for its use of RR substations in prior years, specifically from 1958 (the year in which ComEd first began supplying electricity to each CTA substation) through to 2010 (the last year before ComEd began providing an annual RR Flow-Through Adjustment amount to RR Delivery Class customers). ComEd Ex. 2.0 at 51-52. In this current RDI, ComEd argues that it developed an approach to calculate the present dollar value for compensating the RR Class for the previous utilization of their traction power substations using the 2010 Commission-approved methodology for determining the present Flow-Through Adjustment amount. _Id._ at 53-55 (describing inputs and calculation); _see also_ Docket No. 10-0467, Order at 275. ComEd notes that, in the event the Commission elects to order such compensation, the optional compensation amount for past use of RR facilities is $8,365,299. ComEd Ex. 2.0 at 54-55.

ComEd notes that it also takes no position on whether the Commission should discontinue the RR Delivery Class Flow-Through Adjustment. However, ComEd argues that, if the Commission elects to discontinue the Flow-Through Adjustment, the Commission should adopt ComEd’s proposed 12-year phase-out period as reasonable and appropriate. ComEd also argues that the Commission should reject Staff witness Ashbaugh’s proposal presented in his rebuttal testimony for ComEd and CTA/Metra to reconfigure their systems to end physical flow-through conditions. _See_ Staff Ex. 7.0 at 15-16. ComEd argues that such a reconfiguration would saddle both ComEd and CTA/Metra with unnecessary costs when the Commission could simply end the Flow-Through Adjustment amount for policy reasons. _See_ ComEd Ex. 10.0 at 13-14. ComEd notes that the Commission previously rejected a similar proposal in ComEd’s 2020 RDI case as not being revenue neutral. _See_ 2020 RDI Order at 19

ComEd argues that, in the event the Commission elects to order an adjustment to the RR Delivery Class for past use of traction power substations to serve other customers, and elects to phase out the compensation for current use over ComEd’s proposed 12-year timeline, the total annual RR Class Flow-Through Credit is described in ComEd Ex.

2.15. _See also_ ComEd Ex. 2.14 (calculations showing Flow-Through Amount determination).

#### 2. Staff’s Position

The Commission should approve Staff’s recommendation to eliminate the flow-through adjustment to the RR Class, which consists solely of Metra and the CTA. The Flow-Through Adjustment was designed to compensate the RR Class for the incidental flow of electricity through its substations based on purported benefits to customers outside of the RR Class. ComEd Ex. 2.0 at 47. It would reduce the revenue requirement allocated to the RR Class to account for the electricity that flows through substations owned by the RR Class to non-RR Class customers in ComEd’s service territory. The Adjustment would increase the revenue requirement allocated to non-RR customers by the same amount, ensuring revenue neutrality.

Staff explains that an adjustment was initially proposed in 2005 to account for the flow-through of electricity through the RR substations, based on the premise that the RR Class provided a benefit to the broader system, although the specific purpose and impact of this benefit were not consistently articulated over time. Docket No. 05-0597, Order at 255. However, the Commission determined that there was insufficient evidence to confirm that ComEd or its other customers received meaningful benefits from this flow-through and consequently declined to approve an adjustment at that time. _Id._ The first adjustment was approved in 2010, when the Commission determined ComEd was dependent on some railroad facilities to provide electricity to some other, non-RR customers. Docket No. 10-0467, Order at 273. The Commission approved compensation in the amount of $678,103 annually starting in 2011; the amount was later modified to $668,008 annually in the 2020 RDI, based on updated cost inputs. Docket No. 10-0467, Order at 274-275; 2020 RDI Order at 19. Importantly, the Commission made clear it did not intend for the adjustment to be permanent. Instead, the Commission noted that:

> …the CTA, Metra, ComEd and the general public are best served by finding a solution, or solutions, to this dilemma. These interests are not well-served by a great amount of compensation to Metra and the CTA, as such an amount only provides the CTA and Metra with the motivation to continue being compensated, instead of solving the problem.

Docket No. 10-0467, Order at 274. In 2017, Staff recommended elimination of the Flow-Through Adjustment. While the Commission declined to adopt Staff’s recommendation, it again noted the solution was intended to be temporary and directed ComEd “to address whether, and to what extent, the Flow-Though Adjustment should be modified in its next RDI proceeding.” 2017 RDI Order at 43. In 2020, the Commission once again reiterated that the Flow-Through Adjustment was intended to be temporary and directed ComEd to evaluate how to reduce and/or phase out the Flow-Through Adjustment in the next RDI proceeding. 2020 RDI Order at 19.

Both CTA and Metra continue to suggest the Commission is presented with a binary choice between reconfiguring the remaining traction power stations or continuing to provide an adjustment to the RR Class. However, elimination of the Flow-Through Adjustment does not necessitate system reconfiguration. Just as there are no operational benefits that support continuing the adjustment, there are no operational benefits that requires reconfiguration in the alternative. While both CTA and Metra lament about the high cost of reconfiguring the power stations, neither party has demonstrated that reconfiguring is necessary in the absence of a continued payment. As Staff noted in testimony, the Flow-Through Adjustment has become a subsidy rather than a true adjustment (Staff Ex. 7.0 at 13) and it should be eliminated. It does not follow, however, that reconfiguration is necessary.

Staff points out that the Flow-Through Adjustment was intended to “both compensate, to some extent, the RR Class for prior years’ use of [railroad] facilities and to compensate the [railroad] class for current use of RR facilities.” 2017 RDI Order at 43. Thus, the Commission must make two determinations: first, whether the adjustment should continue and second, if so, whether the adjustment should include compensation for past use. Staff states the short answer to both these queries is no.

##### a. There are no operational benefits that justify continued adjustments.

Staff states that the core issue is not the mere existence of flow-through but whether that flow-through provides operational benefits to non-RR customers or indicates a dependence on RR Class facilities — neither of which has been demonstrated. The mere existence of flow-through does not equate to a benefit. Staff explains that electricity flows through RR Class substations due to the laws of physics — electricity naturally follows the path of least resistance, which means it can flow through the RR facilities to reach other customers incidentally. Staff Ex. 3.0 at 12. Thus, although flow-through conditions may technically exist, this incidental power flow no longer provides significant operational benefits to non-RR customers. Staff Ex. 7.0 at 15-16.

The Commission has consistently questioned whether the flow-through provides sufficient operational benefits to non-RR customers to justify the adjustment. _See_ e.g., Docket No. 05-0597, Order at 254; 2017 RDI Order at 43. For example, in Docket No. 05-0597, the Commission expressed doubt about the flow-through’s operational value, noting insufficient evidence to support a meaningful benefit to non-RR customers. “The fact that electricity flows onto and off of CTA’s system according to the laws of physics does not necessarily mean that significant benefits accrue to ComEd or its other customers.” Docket No. 05-0597, Order at 255.

A crucial distinction must also be made between incidental flow-through and actual dependence. The Commission has repeatedly ruled that non-RR customers do not depend on RR Class facilities for reliable electricity service. The Commission confirmed in its 2020 RDI Order that ComEd is no longer dependent on RR Class substations, describing the use of these substations as more of a convenience than a necessity. 2020 RDI Order at 17. This conclusion is supported by the Commission’s findings in 2017 RDI Order, in which the Commission explicitly concluded that "ComEd’s system would not experience fault conditions or overloads if electricity could no longer flow through [RR] Class power stations." 2017 RDI Order at 43. This outcome was the result of ComEd’s compliance with the 2013 Commission directive to eliminate its dependence on RR traction power facilities while maintaining the looped circuits benefiting the RR Class. 2020 RDI Order at 17. Furthermore, Staff’s review of ComEd’s data request responses in this docket supports this conclusion, showing no quantifiable evidence of any reliability improvements attributable to the flow-through arrangement. Staff Ex. 7.0 at 7.

Metra suggests that Staff’s position on this issue is flawed, in part because Staff did not conduct any independent analysis. Not only is it nonsensical to suggest that Staff had an obligation to conduct any sort of analysis to disprove a claim the RR Class failed to support, but this suggestion also conflates the issues. Staff considered the flow-through data provided by the parties in this docket, as well as those publicly available in Docket Nos. 05-0597, 07-0566, 10-0467, 11-0721, 13-0387, 17-0049, and 20-0832, when making its recommendation but did not conduct any further analyses because Staff does not question the accuracy or reliability of the data. Rather, Staff questioned the accuracy of making conclusions about the level of flow-through or the benefit to non-RR customers from 1958 through 2010 based on data collected starting in 2010. Staff Cross Ex. 1.0 at 1. Moreover, ComEd’s admission that “ComEd is not dependent on the substation traction power substations, in that ComEd would not have overload conditions without the closed bus tie under normal operating conditions” made further analysis moot. Metra Cross Ex. 3.0 at 1 (ComEd response to Metra Data Request 4.01).

Staff notes that, although flow-through still exists, it does not confer a measurable operational benefit on non-RR customers. Staff Ex. 3.0 at 10; Staff Ex. 7.0 at 14. Since the adjustment was first implemented in 2011, the total kWh flow through these substations has been declining, with a 46% reduction from 2011 to 2023 as the percentage of energy flowing through RR Class facilities has decreased from 33.9% in 2011 to 26.03% in 2023. ComEd Ex. 2.0 at 48-49. This decline in flow-through further underscores that, even if incidental electricity passing through RR Class facilities provided a benefit at some past point, it is no longer provides the same magnitude of benefits at present. Additionally, ComEd's eight infrastructure projects, completed between January 2014 and October 2015, eliminated the risk of overloads, proving that the flow-through no longer impacts reliability. Staff Ex. 7.0 at 16. ComEd’s system has been designed to operate independently of these substations, demonstrating that the flow-through does not materially affect reliability. Staff Ex. 7.0 at 15-16.

Staff states that, if the adjustment continues, non-RR customers will be subsidizing the infrastructure costs of the RR Class, even though the non-RR customers derive little to no benefit from these facilities. _Id._ Neither the RR Class nor ComEd has explored options to eliminate flow-through, and both have advocated for continued subsidization. ComEd proposes a 12-year phase-out, gradually reducing the adjustment until it is eliminated. ComEd Ex. 2.0 at 57. The RR Class, on the other hand, advocates for a 25-year phase-out. CTA/Metra Joint Ex. 1.0 at 13. Given that the facilities have a 40-year lifespan and payments began in 2011, approximately 25 years remain in the useful life of the facilities. 2020 RDI Order at 17.

According to Staff, CTA and Metra’s arguments misrepresent the actual beneficiaries of RR infrastructure. Both parties appear to assume that all ComEd ratepayers benefit from RR facilities. This assumption conflates general societal benefits with power delivery or reliability benefits specifically resulting from the Flow-Through Adjustment - benefits that have not been proven to apply uniquely or operationally to ComEd’s non-RR customers. CTA, for example, emphasizes its broader societal and environmental contributions, suggesting these benefits extend to all ratepayers, justifying continued support from other ratepayers for RR Class infrastructure. Similarly, Metra argues for the continuation of the Flow-Through Adjustment based on "important public policy considerations," implying that RR services broadly benefit ComEd’s entire customer base. Metra IB at 5.

Staff states that these assumptions fail to consider the many non-RR customers who derive no direct benefit from RR-owned facilities and who do not rely on these services. Non-RR customers should not be required to subsidize infrastructure that serves only a specific customer segment unless the RR class can demonstrate reliability or operational advantages, which it has failed to do. Further, as it did with CTA’s argument about modifications to Rider NS, the Commission has already decided that non-RR customers should not bear the responsibility for infrastructure on the RR’s side of the meter making this argument unpersuasive. BE Plan 1 Order at 69 (CTA’s proposal for full cost of make-ready infrastructure on CTA’s side of the meeting to be included in standard service was rejected as “unfairly preferential to a specific customer”). By charging non-RR customers, including those outside RR-served areas, for services they neither use nor depend on, CTA and Metra ignore critical distinctions within ComEd’s customer base. Cost allocation should reflect the direct benefits conferred, not generalized societal impacts.

Staff argues that CTA and Metra also fail to explain why ComEd’s broader ratepayer base should be compelled to subsidize RR-owned infrastructure. The Commission has consistently championed equitable rate design principles, mandating that costs be proportionately borne by those who benefit directly from specific infrastructure investments. A. Finkl & Sons Co. v. Ill. Com. Comm’n, 250 Ill. App. 3d 317, 329 (1993). Subsidizing RR facilities through the Flow-Through Adjustment unfairly burdens non-RR customers who receive no direct benefit, which violates the Commission’s principle of fair, benefit-based cost allocation intended to prevent cross-subsidies that disproportionately impact one ratepayer group to benefit another. _See_ 220 ILCS 5/16-108(c) (“Charges for delivery services shall be cost based, and shall allow the electric utility to recover the costs of providing delivery services through its charges to its delivery service customers that use the facilities and services associated with such costs.”).

Staff states that the RR Class members' proposal to continue the adjustment for 25 more years contradicts the Commission's directive that the adjustment be treated as temporary, not a long-term financial commitment. 2020 RDI Order at 18. Moreover, the Commission previously rejected ComEd’s 2020 proposal to phase out the adjustment by amortizing construction costs over the remaining lifespan of the RR Class facilities, as it would have effectively made the adjustment a permanent credit, which Staff argues was never the Commission’s original intention. 2020 RDI Order at 18.

Staff explains that if the flow-through did result in any benefit to non-RR customers or an operational necessity for ComEd, such benefits would not cease merely because an approved payment period ended. However, the proposed solution to extend the adjustment during the phase-out period includes no reconfiguration during the phase out to address any identified problem. This underscores Staff’s recommendation to eliminate the adjustment now, as maintaining it provides no operational benefits to the customers who would fund it and the “solution” is not designed to address a problem. Staff’s recommendation aligns with the Commission’s consistent rulings rejecting similar arguments for compensation. For example, in Docket No. 05-0597, the Commission rejected the CTA’s proposal for credits, citing insufficient evidence that the RR Class facilities provided a meaningful benefit to ComEd or its customers stating that:

> While it is true that physics dictate the flow of electrons on the ComEd/CTA distribution system, the CTA has not proven that its equipment, either by design or by chance, improves the reliability of ComEd’s system or allows ComEd to avoid incurring costs. The fact that electricity flows onto and off of CTA’s system according to the laws of physics does not necessarily mean that significant benefits accrue to ComEd or its other customers.

Docket No. 05-0597, Order at 255.

Staff states that the current situation mirrors this earlier ruling, as the RR Class has not demonstrated that non-RR customers receive a substantial or measurable benefit from RR Class infrastructure. Staff Ex. 3.0 at 10; Staff Ex. 7.0 at 14.

Additionally, the Commission's directive in the 2020 RDI Order instructed ComEd to evaluate and present a proposal for reducing or phasing out the Flow-Through Adjustment, or discontinuing it entirely if warranted by the facts, reiterating that this adjustment was initially intended to be temporary and should not continue indefinitely, 2020 RDI Order at 17. Staff proposes discontinuing the Flow-Through Adjustment entirely. If the Commission declines to immediately eliminate the adjustment, Staff proposes two alternative options: reconfiguration or a phase-out over a maximum of 12 years as follows:

- **(1)** Option A (Reconfiguration Option): This option involves reconfiguring the system to eliminate flow-through conditions entirely, ensuring that no incidental power from Railroad class facilities flows to non-RR customers. Staff Ex. 7.0 at 16. While not Staff’s primary recommendation, Staff witness Ashbaugh testified that reconfiguring the system to eliminate the flow-through conditions entirely would remove the flow-through condition ensuring no incidental power flows from RR Class facilities to non-RR customers, providing a long-term solution. Staff Ex. 7.0 at 16. The RR Class agrees this is possible. CTA/Metra Ex. 1.0 at 10-11. This option should only be considered if the Commission disagrees with Staff’s primary recommendation and determines that any flow-through warrants a payment to the RR customers despite the fact that the flow-through does not benefit non-RR customers.

- **(2)** Option B (Phase-Out Option) (maximum of 12-Year): If the Commission declines to immediately eliminate the Flow-Through Adjustment, Staff supports a 12-year phase-out of the adjustment as proposed by ComEd, as a preferable alternative to the 25-year phase-out proposed by CTA and Metra. _Id._ This option allows for a gradual reduction of the adjustment, giving RR class customers time to adjust while easing the burden on non-RR customers. _Id._ A 12-year period may strike a reasonable balance between immediate elimination and a lengthy adjustment period. _Id._ Staff recommends the Commission reject the 25-year phase-out proposed by Metra and CTA, as it unnecessarily prolongs the financial burden on non-RR customers. It is also contrary to the Commission directive that the phase-out be temporary and not to be based on the remaining life of the equipment, which in this case is likely the 25-year period suggested by the RR Class. 2020 RDI Order at 18; Staff Ex. 7.0 at 16.

##### b. Compensation for past flow-through is retroactive ratemaking.

Staff opposes compensating the RR Class for past use of their facilities, as it violates the prohibition against retroactive ratemaking. ComEd’s 12-year phase out plan includes annual payments comprising compensation for past use and credit for ongoing flow-through during the 12-year phase out period. ComEd Ex. 2.0 at 62. While the credit for on-going flow-through would gradually decrease over 12 years, reaching $0 in year 13, proposed compensation for past use would remain at $697,108 per year, for 12 years, for a total of $8,365,296. The RR class does not specify the proposed annual amount for past use over a 25-year phase out but agrees that ComEd’s approach to arrive at the total amount is “one reasonable way to determine the dollar amount.” CTA/Metra Joint Ex. 1.0 at 9.

Setting aside Staff’s significant reservations about the validity of this sum (Staff Ex. 7.0 at 9), Staff states that there is no question that the proposal is compensation is for past use. The meetings detailed by ComEd about this issue were:

> …to discuss and determine the present dollar value for its use of RR substations in prior years, specifically from 1958 through to 2010. The specified time period is appropriate because 1958 is when ComEd and the CTA entered an Electric Service Agreement wherein ComEd agreed to supply electricity at each CTA Substation. This period encompasses Metra as well because ComEd and Metra entered into an Electric Service Agreement wherein ComEd agreed to supply electricity at each Metra Substation in 1984. And, in 2011, ComEd started providing the annual RR Flow-Through [A]djustment amount to compensate RR customers.

ComEd Ex. 2.0 at 52 (emphasis added). It is also clear that any adjustment for past use would be retroactive ratemaking. The request for compensation for past use is premised on the RR Class’s allegation that their past rates were too high because rates did not include reimbursement for use of the RR substations which allowed electricity to flow-through to non-RR customers, and that non-RR rates were too low because the non-RR customers would fund any reimbursement to the RR to ensure the rate design was revenue neutral. The RR Class is asking non-RR customers to pay an additional $697,108 per year to essentially payback the RR class for past use of its equipment.

Staff explains that the prohibition against retro-active ratemaking is a long-established principle in Illinois law. The Illinois Supreme Court and appellate courts have consistently ruled that utility rates must reflect current costs and conditions, not be adjusted to correct past discrepancies. The Court held that "the rule against retroactive ratemaking prohibits refunds or surcharges to compensate for utility revenues that were too high or too low in a past period." Citizens Util. Co. v. Ill. Com. Comm'n, 124 Ill. 2d 195, 207 (1988). This principle was reaffirmed in People ex rel. Hartigan v. Ill. Com. Comm'n, 148 Ill. 2d 348, 391 (1992), in which the Court underscored that adjustments for past periods are impermissible because they undermine regulatory stability. _See also_ Mandel Bros., Inc. v. Chi. Tunnel Terminal Co., 2 Ill. 2d 205, 211-12, 117 N.E.2d 774, 777 (1954) (“…the rate approved by the Commerce Commission as just and reasonable was the rate which the utility was required to charge so long as the order of the commission remained in effect. It cannot therefore be said that in charging that rate the utility charged an excessive rate which gave rise to a claim for reparations.”); Bus. & Pro. People v. Ill. Com. Comm'n, 146 Ill. 2d 175, 243 (1991) (“Once the Commission establishes rates, the Act does not permit refunds if the established rates are too high, or surcharges if the rates are too low…This rule is consistent with the prospective nature of the Commission's legislative function in ratemaking. In addition, this rule promotes stability in the ratemaking process.”).

According to Staff, this legal framework underscores that retroactive adjustments are inconsistent with the principles of stability and predictability in rate-setting. Metra suggests it was unable to address this concern in its IB “because Staff has not explicitly stated the legal basis for its suggestions about retroactive ratemaking.” Metra IB at 29. This suggestion is somewhat surprising, Staff notes, both because the proposition has been enshrined in Illinois law for over half a century and because there has never been any doubt that the rationale for the adjustment and calculation of the adjustments itself are premised on the idea that the RR Class should be compensated for past use of its facilities. As Staff detailed, the portion of the adjustment attributed to compensation for past use would remain constant over any phase out period, while the compensation for actual present-day use would decline over time.

Moreover, Staff states, even if the Commission were to approve compensation for past use, it is not clear that the value for past compensation is appropriate. While the post-2010 flow-through data measured by ComEd is likely reliable, ComEd did not begin measuring the current flowing through the RR Class substations until 2011. ComEd Ex. 2.0 at 48-49. Given that reliable flow-through data was only collected starting in 2011, compensating for periods prior to this is based on speculation rather than verifiable evidence, further rendering any retroactive adjustments inappropriate and unreliable. Staff Ex. 3.0 at 9. Additionally, the substation cost data provided by the RR Class for periods before 2011 has not been independently verified. _Id._ Given the speculative nature of the data, compensating RR Class customers for past use would be unjustifiable and unreliable. Even if retroactive adjustments were allowable in theory, there is no verifiable flow-through data from before 2011 from which to calculate an adjustment in practice. Without definitive historical data linking RR Class facilities to documented reliability or operational benefits for non-RR customers, any claims for retroactive adjustments are speculative and unreliable, as no reliable records demonstrate the extent of any historical benefits to non-RR customers. Staff Ex. 3.0 at 6-7. This absence of verification makes retroactive adjustments both impractical and legally unviable, as it forces the regulatory framework to depend on assumptions that cannot be substantiated. Allowing retroactive adjustments based on unverifiable historical data would also set a dangerous precedent, potentially undermining regulatory consistency by permitting modifications without a reliable factual basis. Staff states that this approach would contradict Illinois’ commitment to fair, evidence-based rate determinations that maintain stability and protect customers from the uncertainty of unsupported retroactive claims. Citizens Util. Co., 124 Ill. 2d 195, 206-07.

#### 3. CTA’s Position

The CTA explains that the need for ComEd to compensate the RR Class for ComEd’s use of the CTA’s traction power facilities as described above was recognized by the Commission in Docket No. 10-0467. In Docket No. 10-0467, the Commission found that the RR Class has “a unique relationship with ComEd whereby ComEd uses facilities on the CTA’s and Metra’s premises to provide electricity to other customers.” Docket 10-0467, Order at 273. The Order also noted that “[t]his relationship should be approached with caution because disentanglement of this relationship could prove to be costly for all parties.” _Id._ To compensate the RR Class for the use of the RR Class facilities, the Commission adopted the RR Class flow-through credit that over time “may vary, as these substations are reconfigured and redesigned.” _Id._ at 274. The Commission also found, because of the long-time use of RR Class facilities, that “some additional amount of compensation is warranted, due to the fact that ComEd has been using these facilities for decades, without compensation.” _Id._ at 275.

The CTA explains that the RR Class differs from other ComEd classes in several significant ways. First, there are only two members of the RR Class — the CTA and Metra. Second, this Commission through its orders in previous dockets has determined that there are public policy considerations that must be taken into account to avoid any adverse effects on the RR Class. Third, because power flows through the CTA’s traction power substations to serve other ComEd customers, the Commission adopted the RR Class flow-through adjustment to partially compensate the RR Class members for ComEd’s use of the RR Class member’s equipment. CTA/Metra Joint Ex. 1.0 at 3.

The CTA states that the electricity delivered by ComEd to the RR Class powers the CTA’s electric trains. For over 75 years, the CTA traction power substations have received and continue to receive service from ComEd using two separate, independent 12.6 kV lines. The reason for the two-line feed is to ensure that there is uninterrupted power to the substation (and ultimately the rapid transit trains) so that, if power is lost on one ComEd line, the other line can instantly pick up the service so that a rapid transit train does not lose power. A loss of power to a train can be hazardous, especially if the train is on an elevated track or in the underground subway. Such a stoppage between passenger stations creates a danger to the passengers, potentially the general public, and it disrupts train operations. CTA Ex. 2.00 at 3. The two lines are connected to breakers in the CTA traction power substation. This breaker is normally operated in the closed position so that the power flows from one line to the other. If the breaker operates in the open position then the railroads lose the dual feed necessary to operate the trains. CTA Ex. 2.00 at 4.

According to the CTA, no qualified, credible witness in the docket disputes that power flows through certain CTA traction power substations to serve other ComEd customers. The CTA points out that even Staff agreed that the fact that “power flows though the substations is not in dispute in this proceeding and the power flow studies included in the records are not contested.” CTA Cross Ex. 2. The CTA has 64 traction power substations. For 41 of the CTA traction power substations, the two main breakers are operated in the above described “closed” position. The configuration of the substations has remained unchanged for over 75 years — two feed lines connected to each traction power substation connected together at a breaker operating in the closed position so that the power can, and does, flow in both directions. In other words, by operation of physics, power has flowed into and out of the traction power substations to serve other ComEd customers. No party to this proceeding has concluded “that the meter readings by ComEd for the CTA traction power substations are inaccurate or unreliable.” CTA Cross Ex. 4. Further, no party to the proceeding claims “that the flow-through data by CTA traction power substations is inaccurate or unreliable” nor has any party concluded that “the accuracy or reliability of the flow-through data” is invalid.

The CTA explains that the number of traction power substations with kWh flow through has been declining and will continue to decline as more CTA traction power substations are reconfigured over time. The CTA explains that 23 of its traction power substations have been reconfigured by changing out CTA-owned equipment. After the reconfiguration, only one main breaker is operated in the closed position, resulting in the elimination of ComEd power flowing through the CTA substation to serve other ComEd customers. If the ComEd line fails or faults, then the other breaker closes.

The CTA is in the process of converting one additional traction power substation by 2025. Because the CTA lacks funds and resources to immediately reconfigure all the remaining substations to eliminate the closed two-breaker operation, reconfiguration occurs only when there is a major reconstruction of one of the transit lines. In addition, the CTA currently is building three new traction power substations that will have no flow-through capabilities. CTA Ex. 2.00 at 6.

ComEd Ex. 2.00 Table 7 shows the overall flow through has decreased from nearly 34 percent in 2011-2012 to 25.9 percent in 2023/2024, reflecting the fact that the total number of stations with no flow through has increased from 8 to 30. For traction power substations where flow through does exist, it accounts for nearly 40 percent of the power delivered to and through the substation. This is not an incidental flow but rather is significant and relies on facilities owned and operated by the CTA. The costs on the CTA facilities must be compensated. As the CTA reconfigures its traction power substations and brings new substations on line in the reconfigured mode, the amount of the flow-through will continue to decline.

The CTA explains that in Docket No. 20-0832, the Commission recognized that the flow-through has been declining as the stations are redesigned and reconfigured. As a result, the Commission directed ComEd in its next RDI docket “to evaluate, and present a proposal about how to reduce and/or phase-out the Flow-Through Adjustment.” 2020 RDI Order at 18. In Docket No. 20-0832, the Commission also ordered ComEd to initiate a working group “to determine a present dollar value of compensation for prior use of railroad facilities.” 2020 RDI Order at 18.

In response to the Commission Order, the CTA observes that in this docket ComEd offered modifications to the flow-through credit calculation of the present dollar value for prior use of railroad facilities and to set a 12-year phase out of the credit. Mr. Leick originally proposed to a stakeholder group comprised of ComEd, the RR Class, and Staff a 25-year phase out considering the remaining life span of the RR Class facilities based on when the flow-through started. He changed his recommendation based on objections from Staff, noting in a data response to Staff:

> Staff indicated that 25 years—the remaining asset life—was too long of a time period even if energy still flows through the railroad facilities because the Commission indicated that it may not be reasonable to use the remaining useful life and that a phase-out period should be reasonably short. [_See_ 2020 RDI Order at 18]. Consequently, ComEd revised its proposal and reduced the time period by more than half, proposing a 12-year phase-out.

CTA/Metra Joint Ex. 1.02.

CTA/Metra witness Bachman testified that ComEd’s proposed 12-year phase out is not based on any rational data. Rather, the 12 years is a decision by ComEd:

> …to “split-the-baby” between the [ICC] Staff’s apparent desire (as expressed in the working group setting) to terminate the flow-through credit either immediately or on a much more expedited schedule and the 25-year period. In short, ComEd switched from using the rational, fact-based useful life standard as explained by Mr. Leick at ComEd Exhibit 2.0 at 57 and ComEd’s response to Data Request DLA 2.07... to proposing the 12-year phase-out period that does not provide adequate time for either ComEd or the railroads to reconfigure the substations and lines in an orderly, cost-effective manner.

CTA/Metra Joint Ex. 1.0 at 12-13.

In surrebuttal testimony, ComEd evaluated Staff’s options that the Commission either “(1) mandate an evaluation and implementation of a system reconfiguration to eliminate the flow-through conditions, with all reconfiguration costs incurred fairly distributed among all ComEd customers; or (2) adopt ComEd’s proposal to phase out the Flow-Through Adjustment over a 12-year period.” ComEd Ex. 10.0 at 13. Of Staff’s two options, ComEd noted the second option “is consistent with ComEd’s proposal.” _Id._ ComEd’s rejection of Staff’s proposal to require ComEd to reconfigure the RR Class traction power substations is consistent with the Commission’s previous rejection of a similar Staff proposal in Docket No. 20-0832. 2020 RDI Order at 19.

The CTA states that the cost to replace the switchgear on the CTA side of the meter at a single traction power substation is estimated to be $3.3 million. CTA Ex. 2.00 at 7. It is unclear whether Staff’s first option would reimburse the CTA for the costs for this work. This figure does not include the costs that ComEd imposes on the CTA for any work on ComEd’s side of the meter. _Id._ at 7. In other words, the cost to immediately reconfigure the remaining 41 traction power substations would be over $135 million “an amount that could not be immediately funded in today’s environment.” CTA Ex. 3.00 at 4. The CTA lacks funds and resources to immediately reconfigure all the remaining substations to eliminate the closed two-breaker operation. _Id._ at 6. The original 25-year phase-out proposal by ComEd gives the CTA time to continue the process of converting the existing traction power substations to eliminate the flow. As previously noted, all new traction power substations or routine rebuilding of existing substations are being planned so that there is no flow-through.

Adding compensation for ComEd’s prior use of CTA facilities ($697,108 per year over a 12-year period) to the flow-through credit has insignificant to no impact on ComEd’s other customers. ComEd Ex. 2.0 at 63. The CTA argues that in other words, Staff’s concern about impact on customers of the flow-through credit and compensation to the CTA for ComEd’s prior use of CTA facilities is not valid.

The CTA opines that as this Commission has found in the past, “the Commission must consider the potential adverse impact of utility rate increases on entities that provide public transportation.” Docket No. 05-0597, Order at 190. Imposing additional costs on the RR Class “runs counter to this Commission’s policy of encouraging the use of public transportation for environmental reasons.” Commonwealth Edison Co., Docket No. 09-0263, Order at 43 (Oct. 14, 2009). Allowing for an orderly phase-out over 25 years avoids rate shock, allows for an orderly transition, and complies with the Commission’s directive in Docket No. 20-0832 that ComEd and the parties “present a proposal about how to reduce and/or phase-out the Flow Through Adjustment and to “determine a present dollar value of compensation for prior use of railroad facilities.” 2020 RDI Order at 18.

Staff argues that recovering this amount, which is consistent with the calculation methodology of the flow-through credit since 2011, would be retroactive ratemaking. The CTA argues that Staff’s analysis is incorrect. The CTA notes that the Commission would be setting ComEd’s rates on a going-forward basis. As the court found in United Cities Gas Co. v. Ill. Com. Comm'n, 163 Ill. 2d 1 (1994), there is no retroactive ratemaking when the proceeding does “not make adjustments to or rescind” orders of previous cases. 163 Ill. 2d at 16. United Cities distinguished the results from Citizens Util. Co. v. Ill. Com. Comm’n, 124 Ill. 2d 195 (1988), cited by Staff, noting that, unlike United Cities, Citizens disturbed prior Commission findings. Here, the rates are being set on a going-forward basis and there is no reversal or disturbing of prior Commission cases.

Mandel Bros., Inc. v. Chi. Tunnel Terminal Co., 2 Ill. 2d 205, cited by Staff, is inapplicable. Mandel concerned a request by a customer for a refund of a Commission-approved rate. While the court in People ex rel. Hartigan v. Ill. Com. Comm'n, 148 Ill. 2d 348 (1992) affirmed the finding in Mandel that the “Commission’s function is legislative in nature and the rates that it sets are prospective in operation,” the court approved refunds to customers under its equitable powers. 148 Ill.2d at 397. Unlike Mandel and Hartigan, there is no request for a refund in this docket. The flow-through credit is based upon usage of RR Class owned facilities and equipment — not a refund for past charges. In other words, ComEd is paying for facilities it uses and the Commission has previously found payment should be made for this use.

Staff cites Bus. & Prof'l People for Pub. Interest v. Ill. Com. Comm'n, 146 Ill. 2d 175, in support of its retroactive ratemaking argument. However, in that case, the Commission allowed ComEd to recover deferred charges because the “charges are not intended to correct an error made in prior rate orders which had included these plants in rate base. Rather, Edison argues deferred charges are merely one aspect of the reasonable and prudent costs of the plants which should be included in rate base. We agree with Edison. The fact that deferred charges represent capital costs incurred in the past does not make recovery of these costs retroactive.” 146 Ill. 2d at 243-244. The flow-through credit is based upon usage of RR Class-owned facilities and equipment — not a refund for past charges. In other words, ComEd is paying on a deferred basis for facilities it has used and continues to use. The Commission has previously found payment should be made for this use. The CTA concludes that the flow-through credit portion for past use is not retroactive ratemaking.

The CTA urge the Commission to adopt ComEd’s calculation for past usage of railroad facilities and continue the RR Class flow-through credit for the full term of 25 years as originally advocated by ComEd.

#### 4. Metra’s Position

Metra notes that the Commission first adopted the Flow-Through Adjustment in 2011 to compensate CTA/Metra because “ComEd uses facilities on the CTA’s and Metra’s premises to provide electricity to other customers” and “disentanglement of this relationship could prove to be costly for all parties.” Docket No. 10-0467, Order at 273. ComEd initially proposed an estimate of 33% of the cost of replacement facilities for the credit owed to CTA/Metra, but Metra recommended a figure of 50% or 100% of the cost of replacement facilities because ComEd has had “unfettered use of these facilities for at least 40 years without any compensation.” _Id._ at 275.

> In opposition to Metra’s and CTA’s recommendations to increase the Flow-Through Adjustment, Staff recommends discontinuing the Flow-Through Adjustment altogether, which it also recommended in the 2017 RDI. Other than changes in the number of substations that have flow-through and the volume of energy flowing through those substations, the general facts remain the same in this docket as in the 2017 RDI, specifically that (1) ComEd is not dependent on RR facilities, and (2) ComEd still uses RR facilities. The Commission finds, as it did in the 2017 RDI Order, that the RR Class should continue to receive some compensation for the use of the facilities at this time.

2020 RDI Order at 19 (emphasis added).

Notably, ComEd also agrees that the Flow-Through Adjustment is not strictly based on operational benefits, but rather on use: “The adjustment provides a revenue requirement reduction to the RR Delivery Class as a result of electricity flowing through RR-owned substations to serve non-RR customers in ComEd’s service territory.” ComEd Ex. 2.0 at 47. In short, Staff’s operational benefits argument does not negate the Commission’s multiple prior RDI decisions approving the Flow-Through Adjustment. Nor is there any dispute that ComEd continues to use the RR Class substations to serve non-RR class customers. This is confirmed unequivocally by ComEd in its discovery response to Metra-ComEd 4.01, which is in the record as Metra Cross-Ex. 3.0.

Metra argues that even if operational benefits were part of the calculus, record evidence shows that ComEd and non-RR Class customers do in fact benefit from use of the substations that the RR Class owns and maintain. It is undisputed that “[e]lectrons do flow through the RR facilities to serve other ComEd customers” at a total kWh Flow Through of 140,945,611. ComEd Ex. 2.0 at 49, 56. Notwithstanding, Staff argues that even though the flow-through still exists, there are no operational benefits to non-RR Class customers. Metra contends that Staff disregards Mr. Vitalis’s testimony, which states that the electricity that flows through Metra’s Substations goes to certain customer facilities that require reliable, constant power, such as the University of Chicago and the University of Chicago Hospital. Metra Ex. 1.0 at 5. Metra is responsible for maintaining the substations in the MED and ensures that the flow through is reliable. _Id._ at 7. Mr. Vitalis states that Metra spends approximately $3.29 million annually to maintain the substations in the MED. _Id._

Staff also argues that since ComEd’s system no longer relies on the substations, this demonstrates “that the flow-through does not materially affect reliability.” Staff IB at 12. Metra argues that this ignores the fact that ComEd agrees with CTA/Metra that reconfiguring the substations to eliminate the flow-through would be complex: “to do so in 12 years would be a challenging task.” ComEd IB at 21 n.7. And while ComEd may not rely on the substations to the degree that it did in 2011 (when the Flow-Through Adjustment was first authorized), the Substations are significantly embedded into ComEd’s system, enough so that it would require a significant and lengthy timeline for ComEd to end such use.

Metra states that Staff’s argument also underestimates the degree of ComEd’s reliance on the substations, which is demonstrated by the need for the RR Class to obtain ComEd permission to open the substations breakers. As Mr. Bachman explained:

Neither Staff witnesses nor any other witnesses take issue with this fact. This is a critical point. If ComEd does not rely on the CTA and Metra-owned substations to serve non-RR Class customers, then ComEd would not need to control how and when CTA and Metra operate those substations, and ComEd would not dictate, for example, when CTA or Metra may implement planned outages and other substation-related work. But the uncontested facts show that ComEd does rely on the RR Class-owned substations and therefore does dictate how and when CTA and Metra can work on those substations. In other words, ComEd relies upon the [RR] Class’s facilities to serve other customers.

CTA/Metra Joint Ex. 2.0 at 8. Mr. Vitalis also documented the difficulty of obtaining a planned outage from ComEd, noting that the Metra substations clearly provide a valuable service to non-RR Class customers because ComEd and Metra need to extensively coordinate and plan even the most routine scheduled outages. Metra Ex. 3.0 at 3.

So long as ComEd uses the substations, it should not do so for free. Accordingly, Metra recommends that the Commission direct ComEd to compensate the RR Class for such use with a Flow-Through Adjustment. In addition, even if operational importance were required to justify the Flow-Through Adjustment, the record evidence shows that the electricity flowing through the substations provides reliable power to those non-RR class customers requiring consistent and reliable electricity.

Metra states that if the Commission decides that the Flow-Through Adjustment should be phased out, that phase out should occur gradually over 25 years, as ComEd initially proposed. As ComEd explained, the 25-year phase-out period is aligned with the life span of the substations, based on subtracting the period that ComEd began providing the Flow-Through Adjustment to present (15 years) from the 40-year useful life of the substations. ComEd Ex. 2.0 at 56. Mr. Bachman noted and agreed that the ComEd approach is well reasoned and appropriate. CTA/Metra Joint Ex. 1.0 at 11-12.

ComEd confirmed this in response to data request DLA 2.07, stating that in working group meetings to discuss the Flow- Through Adjustment, ComEd initially proposed a 25-year phase-out period and later revised it after Staff opined that the 25-year period was too long. Staff that wanted the flow-through to be stopped immediately.” CTA/Metra Joint Ex. 2.0 at 2. For these reasons, if the Commission determines that the Flow-Through Adjustment should be phased out, it should adopt a 25-year phase out period to align with the life span of the substations versus ComEd’s unsupported compromise position, that ComEd itself doubts is practicable.

Metra states that in his rebuttal testimony, Mr. Ashbaugh proffered two options for the Commission if it decides not to immediately eliminate the Flow-Through Adjustment – Option A (Reconfiguration Option) and Option B (12-year phase out)

Metra further argues that the Commission should authorize ComEd to compensate the RR Class for past use of its facilities. Metra notes that in Docket No. 20-0832, the Commission recognized that the Flow-Through Adjustment was designed to both partially compensate the RR Class for ComEd’s prior years’ use of RR Class facilities and directed ComEd to initiate a working group to determine a present dollar value of compensation for prior use of RR facilities. 2020 RDI Order at 18–19. Mr. Leick presented a present dollar value calculation based on the 2010 Commission approved methodology for determining the Flow-Through Adjustment. ComEd Ex. 2.0 at 53.

Staff argues that ComEd should not compensate the RR Class for historical use, notwithstanding that the Commission in Docket No. 20-0832 specifically directed that steps be taken to compensate the RR Class for ComEd’s past usage of the RR Class Substations. Metra argues that none of Staff’s arguments stand up to scrutiny.

First, Staff argues that ComEd’s present dollar value calculation is based on speculative data due to lack of reliable or precise historical records on power flows and associated costs. Staff Ex. 3.0 at 9. However, Mr. Leick explains that the calculation of payments that ComEd utilizes is supported by “reliable, publicly available sources from 1958 to 2010” and when the data was not readily available, ComEd used appropriate assumptions to supplement the inputs. ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 9. Mr. Bachman also notes that Staff did not present any analysis or workpapers to the working group to question ComEd’s calculations. CTA/Metra Joint Ex. 2.0 at 6.

Second, Mr. Ashbaugh argues that there is no evidence to support the historical number of substations used in ComEd’s calculations and that numbers provided by Metra and CTA were presented without “verification or detailed records.” Staff Ex. 3.0 at 9. Mr. Bachman explains that in the working groups, ComEd and the RR Class worked collaboratively to determine the historical number of substations for each year from 1958 through 2010. CTA/Metra Joint Ex. 2.0 at 5. Further, Mr. Ashbaugh admits that he did not “conclude that the historical number of Metra traction power substations is inaccurate or unreliable.” Metra Ex. 3.03.

Third, Mr. Ashbaugh states that ComEd lacked evidence to support the value of substations and that it was inappropriate for ComEd to utilize the 2010 values for the cost of substations as it may not reflect the historical costs of the facilities. Staff Ex. 3.0 at 9. However, both Mr. Bachman and Mr. Leick note that the formula to determine compensation for ComEd’s historical use of the substations is based on the 2010 Commission-approved methodology in Docket No. 10-0647. CTA/Metra Joint Ex. 2.0 at 7; ComEd Ex. 2.0 at 52. Mr. Leick makes clear that “ComEd used the Commission-approved formula utilizing the 2010 values for the cost of substations and the revenue requirement factor.” ComEd Ex. 2.0 at 52–53. Metra argues that the estimated value of the compensation owed to the RR Class should reflect ComEd’s “unfettered use of [the] facilities for at least 40 years” and thus the Flow-Through Adjustment should be either 50% or 100% of the replacement costs of the Substations, valued at 2010 levels. Docket No. 10-0467, Order at 275. The Commission stated that it agreed with Metra’s argument that the Flow-Through Adjustment should account for ComEd’s historical use of the substations, and therefore “concluded[d] that the correct credit should be the 50% figure, or $678,104.” _Id._ The Commission’s prior decision demonstrates that the Flow-Through Adjustment formula’s inputs and assumptions already contemplate using the Flow-Through Adjustment to compensate the RR Class for ComEd’s historical use of substations. Mr. Ashbaugh does not provide any evidence to show that the historical value of substations should be significantly different than the 2010 value of the substations. Therefore, Metra contends the Commission should defer to its own prior decisions and expertise in this matter.

Fourth, Mr. Ashbaugh disputes the energy usage factor of 33% used by ComEd because “the absence of precise historical data on power flows undermines the validity of the proposed compensation amounts.” Staff Ex. 3.0 at 9. However, Metra notes, Mr. Ashbaugh admits that he did not “review, analyze or prepare any data” related to power flow through the Substations,” and Mr. Ashbaugh did not testify to the accuracy or reliability of flow-through data. CTA/Metra Joint Ex. 2.0 at 7; CTA/Metra Joint Ex. 2.02. Metra urges the Commission to ignore Mr. Ashbaugh’s unsupported assertion that the energy usage factor used by ComEd is flawed.

Finally, Mr. Ashbaugh argues that the RR Class and ComEd must provide evidence to support their claims and ComEd “must demonstrate that any offered amount is directly related to the use of railroad facilities.” Staff Ex. 3.0 at 10. As Metra has pointed out, it is well settled by the RR Class, ComEd, and the Commission that the Flow-Through Adjustment is partially designed to compensate the RR Class for ComEd’s historical use of their substations. Docket No. 10-0467, Order at 275. Mr. Ashbaugh cites Docket No. 05-0597 and states that the Commission rejected “CTA’s proposal due to the lack of evidence showing meaningful benefits to ComEd and other customers” and claims that “[i]t is unclear what evidence exists now to support Metra and CTA’s claim that did not exist 20 years ago.” Staff Ex. 3.0 at 10. However, Mr. Ashbaugh disregards the record evidence. In Docket Nos. 10-0467, 13-0837, 17-0049, and 20-0832, the RR Class and ComEd have repeatedly justified the Flow-Through Adjustment and the Commission has repeatedly reaffirmed the continuation of the Flow-Through Adjustment. Metra IB at 17– 19. Mr. Ashbaugh also states that neither the RR Class nor ComEd has the incentive to “negotiate the most cost-effective or justified solution.” Staff Ex. 3.0 at 10. However, Metra contends that the Commission, a neutral arbiter in this situation, has weighed the evidence presented before it and has determined that the Flow-Through Adjustment is a necessary and appropriate component of ComEd’s rate design, and the fact that the parties, including Staff, entered into a working group process to negotiate and arrive at a cost-effective and justified solution supports that determination.

Staff further argues that the substation cost data provided by the RR Class has not been independently verified. However, Metra notes that the RR Class provided the historical number of substations for each year to ComEd’s satisfaction. ComEd Ex. 2.0 at 52. In sum, Metra argues Staff has failed to provide any reasoning or evidence demonstrating why the historical number of substations provided by the RR Class is inaccurate. CTA/Metra Joint Ex. 2.0 at 6.

Metra contends that Staff also did not point to any authority requiring the RR Class’s substation cost data be “independently verified.” Staff IB at 17. Metra states that it is not even clear what such a requirement would mean in the context of a Commission proceeding wherein each party has its own witnesses, and there is no witness that is “independent” of a given party. In any event, expert witnesses for both ComEd and CTA/Metra supported the suitability and accuracy of the calculations based on the available data. Indeed, Metra observes that the Commission quite regularly accepts and approves those types of calculations made by utilities and other parties, without applying an “independent verification” requirement. _See, e.g.,_ Staff IB at 29–31 (detailing Staff’s proposal for a DTOU charge).

Metra further argues that Staff is incorrect to characterize the Flow-Through Adjustment as retroactive ratemaking. Metra observes that in ComEd’s 2020 RDI, the Commission specifically directed ComEd and interested stakeholders to calculate the compensation to be paid prospectively for past use of the RR Class Substations. 2020 RDI Order at 18–19. Notwithstanding, Staff now argues that calculating prospective compensation to the RR Class for ComEd’s past use of RR facilities constitutes retroactive ratemaking. Metra asserts that this argument is not only contrary to prior Commission direction, but contrary to the Commission’s previously-stated rationale for the Flow-Through Adjustment and is unsupported by applicable law.

Metra contends the touchstone for evaluating potential retroactive ratemaking is whether a Commission order adjusts or rescinds a prior order. In United Cities Gas Co. v. Ill. Com. Comm’n, 163 Ill. 2d 1 (1994), the Illinois Supreme Court found that there was no retroactive ratemaking when the proceeding “did not make adjustments to or rescind” orders of previous cases. 163 Ill. 2d at 16. United Cities distinguished the results from Citizens Util. Co. v. Ill. Com. Comm’n, 124 Ill. 2d 195 (1988), which is cited by Staff, noting that, unlike United Cities, Citizens disturbed prior Commission findings. Metra points out that unlike Citizens, this proceeding is establishing the rate design on a prospective basis, and there is no request or need for reversal or disturbance of a prior Commission order or cases. Indeed, the whole point of the payment calculation at issue is to conform with and fulfill the Commission’s directive in ComEd’s 2020 RDI.

Metra explains that under Illinois case law, the prohibition against retroactive ratemaking is expressed as follows: “Once the Commission establishes rates, the Act does not permit refunds if the established rates are too high, or surcharges if the rates are too low.” Bus. and Prof. People for Pub. Int. v. Ill. Com. Comm'n, 136 Ill. 2d 192, 209; _see also_ Mandel Bros. v. Chi. Tunnel Terminal Co., 2 Ill. 2d 205, 211–12 (“It follows that the rate approved by the Commerce Commission as just and reasonable was the rate which the utility was required to charge so long as the order of the commission remained in effect. It cannot therefore be said that in charging that rate the utility charged an excessive rate which gave rise to a claim for reparations.”). In sum, retroactive ratemaking occurs when a utility establishes a scheme whereby it provides refunds to its consumers when its rates are too high and surcharges when its rates are too low. People ex rel. Madigan v. Ill. Com. Comm’n, 2013 IL App (2d) 120243, ¶ 9. Once the Commission approves a ratemaking plan, “it cannot later modify that plan to correct an error.” _Id._ at ¶ 24. These principles clearly do not apply to the Flow-Through Adjustment. Metra notes that all of the cases cited by Staff involve issues about refunds or reparations. Metra states that the Flow-Through Adjustment does not confer a refund or reparations, nor has the RR Class made a claim for such a refund or reparation. Metra asserts that in this proceeding, the rate design being set is prospective and there is no request by any party to modify any prior Commission cases or orders. Metra notes the entire point of the payment calculation at issue is to comply with the direction contained in the Commission’s Order in ComEd’s 2020 RDI. Metra observes that through its historic, repeated approval of the Flow-Through Adjustment, the Commission has not engaged in any refund process or attempted to change the amount of revenue eligible for ComEd to recover. Nor would the Commission be doing so now through an extension of the Flow-Through Adjustment, including partial compensation for ComEd’s past use of the RR Class Substations. Metra contends the Commission would not even be retroactively changing how the compensation ComEd provides to the RR Class is calculated because ComEd has utilized the Commission-approved formula to calculate the compensation for past use. ComEd Ex. 2.0 at 52–53.

Staff further argues that the compensation for past use would be retroactive ratemaking because the request for past use “is premised on the RR [C]lass’s allegation that their past rates were too high because rates did not include reimbursement for use of the railroad substations…and that non-railroad rates were too low because the non-railroad customers would fund any reimbursement to the RR to ensure the rate design was revenue neutral.” Staff IB at 16. Again, Metra argues that Staff’s argument falls flat in light of the Commission’s specific direction that ComEd work with Staff and stakeholders to calculate the present value of its past use of the RR Class’s facilities for prospective payment. CTA/Metra Joint Ex. 1.0 at 7.

#### 5. Commission Analysis and Conclusion

The Commission approved the Flow-Through Adjustment in 2011 on the basis that ComEd was reliant on RR Class facilities to provide service to non-RR customers – “approximately 12 facilities.” Docket No. 10-0467, Order at 273. In that proceeding, the Commission adopted a flow-through adjustment based upon the record and included a consideration for past use of CTA and Metra facilities. _Id._ at 275. In this proceeding, CTA and Metra continue to support the flow-through adjustment and request an increase. Though the Commission acknowledges the temporary intention of this adjustment, the record demonstrates that flow-through continues to occur at levels that warrant compensation. Accordingly, the Commission adopts the 12-year phase-out, gradually reducing the adjustment until it is eliminated, as proposed by ComEd. ComEd Ex. 2.0, at 62, Table 8.

The Commission does not agree discontinuation of the flow-through adjustment necessitates reconfiguration. The record evidence indicates that it is not cost beneficial to reconfigure the system to eliminate electricity from flowing through RR facilities. While Staff suggests that reconfiguring the system to eliminate flow-through conditions is an option, no party, including Staff, ultimately supports this option. Moreover, as the Commission previously determined, this proposal is not revenue neutral as it would require ComEd to invest in reconfiguring RR Class substations and is therefore outside the scope of this proceeding. 2020 RDI Order, at 19. The Commission affirms reconfiguration is not revenue neutral and declines to require it. The Commission agrees with ComEd that discontinuation of the flow-through adjustment, rather than requiring reconfiguration, may be determined as a matter of policy.

The Commission finds the 12-year phase out reasonably and appropriately compensates the RRs while considering the sustained decline of flow-through. In Docket No. 20-0832, the Commission recognized the decline of flow-through and directed ComEd to present a reduction, elimination, or phase-out option for the Flow-Through Adjustment. The total kWh flow-through of RR substations declined 46% between 2011 and 2023. ComEd Ex. 2.0, at 48-49. ComEd, CTA, and Metra have all conducted projects that contribute to the decrease in flow-through. CTA admits that flow-through will continue to decline as each party continues to update and reconfigure its system. However, for the substations where flow-through exists, it accounts for nearly 40% of the power delivered to and through the substation. The Commission agrees with CTA that this is not incidental flow. The 12-year phase-out option provided by ComEd appropriately addresses flow-through while considering its decline and the Commission’s prior Order.

CTA and Metra contend that, if electricity flows through its facilities that connect to non-RR Class customers, the RR Class should get a subsidy. Alternatively, CTA and Metra support a 25-year phase-out to account for the useful life of the RR equipment. The Commission is not convinced the fact that electricity flows through RR facilities as a matter of physics is justification for a lasting subsidy. Further, the record does not support the proposition that ComEd will continue to use the facilities for the rest of the equipment’s useful life. In the 2020 RDI docket, the Commission stated, “it is not reasonable to require non-RR Delivery Class customers to subsidize the RR Delivery Class for the remaining useful life of the RR facilities regardless of whether those facilities are actually used by ComEd in the future to serve non-RR customers.” Docket No. 20-0832, Final Order, at 18. The Commission in the 2020 RDI Order emphasized the temporary intention of the adjustment and advised use of a shorter phase-out option. The Commission finds past Commission decisions and the record evidence demonstrating a sustained decrease in flow-through supports a 12-year phase-out over either a 25-year phase-out or a continuation of the adjustment.

The Commission appreciates parties’ efforts to quantify any potential compensation for ComEd’s prior use of RR facilities as directed in the 2020 RDI Order. The directive to calculate such compensation was not implicit approval that it was allowed or warranted. The Commission agrees with Staff that compensation for past use would constitute retroactive ratemaking as it would provide an adjustment to correct past discrepancies. Further, the Commission, though not bound by previous Commission Orders, acknowledges the long discussion of this adjustment starting in the early 2000s. Despite arguments by CTA and Metra in support, the Commission did not find the Flow-Through Adjustment appropriate until 2011. The Commission finds that there should be no additional compensation for prior use of RR facilities going back to 1958. Accordingly, the Commission adopts a 12-year phase out of the Flow-Through Adjustment, without consideration of past use as presented in ComEd Ex. 2.0, at 62, Table 8.

### C. Next Step to Full Revenue Responsibility for the RR Delivery Class

ComEd notes that the Commission has been moving the RR Delivery Class toward 100% revenue responsibility in steps since 2008, the most recent of which was step 4 out of 10 in the 2020 RDI. 2020 RDI Order at 23. The RR Delivery Class is currently at 87.0% revenue responsibility and ComEd has calculated the next step revenue responsibility to be 89.2% (step 5 of 10). _See_ ComEd Ex. 2.0 at 66-67. ComEd explains that, if the Commission chooses to assign the RR Delivery Class 89.2% of revenue responsibility, the remaining 10.8% will be reassigned to all other delivery classes based upon their assigned revenue from the ECOSS, and the change would become effective beginning with the January 2026 monthly billing period and continue until ComEd’s next RDI, at which time the RR Delivery Class’s progress toward the next subsequent step to full revenue responsibility can be evaluated. _Id._ at 67. ComEd notes that Staff supports moving the RR Delivery Class to the next step toward 100% revenue responsibility, and no party opposed this recommendation. _See_ Staff Ex. 2.0 at 9-10; ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 11.

The Commission finds that ComEd should take the next step – step 5 of 10 – towards full revenue responsibility for the RR Class. The Commission directs ComEd to assign the RR Delivery Class 89.2% of revenue responsibility beginning with the January 2026 monthly billing period and reassign the remaining 10.8% of the RR Delivery Class’s revenue responsibility to all other delivery classes based upon their assigned revenue from the ECOSS.

### D. Beneficial Electrification Allocation

#### 1. ComEd’s Position

ComEd argues that the Commission should approve ComEd’s proposed BE Plan cost allocation. ComEd argues that it has proposed a reasonable and appropriate allocation of BE Plan costs that reflects its considered judgment on assigning costs using the information currently available to ComEd. ComEd’s allocation includes revisions to certain aspects proposed by Staff and various intervenors in this proceeding. ComEd requests that the Commission reject certain intervenors’ proposals that would alter which allocation factors are applied to BE Plan costs.

ComEd proposes in direct testimony an allocation of the BE Plan costs. _See_ ComEd Ex. 2.0 at 67-68. This allocation resulted in 12.1% of BE Plan costs being assigned to residential delivery classes using the forecasted usage (in kWhs) for each delivery class and 87.9% of BE Plan costs being assigned to the non-residential/lighting delivery classes using the forecasted billing demands (with the load profile of the Watt-Hour and Lighting Delivery Classes being used to compute an equivalent billing demand). _See id._ at 68; _see also_ REACT Cross Ex. 12.0 (explaining how ComEd developed its BE Plan cost allocation methodology). ComEd allocates no BE Plan costs to the RR Delivery Class because the two members of that delivery class are already electrified. ComEd Ex. 2.0 at 68. ComEd proposes using billing demand as an allocation factor for non-residential BE Plan costs after considering intervenor feedback on the issue in the BE Plan 1 docket. _See_ REACT Cross Ex. 12 (explaining that ComEd originally proposed to use total kWhs as an allocation factor for BE Plan costs but revised its proposal to use billing demand in response to recommendations from intervenor Walmart). Billing demand is a reasonable and appropriate approximation of delivery classes’ use of ComEd’s distribution system and the fact that all customers benefit from a reduction in emissions (which are predominantly caused by larger load customers). ComEd Ex. 10.0 at 16--17. ComEd also proposes in its direct testimony to revise how it allocates BE Plan costs within the High Voltage (“HV”) Delivery Class’s subfunctions, affecting the Primary Voltage Distribution Facilities Charge (“DFC”), Secondary Voltage DFC, and High Voltage DFC for that delivery class. ComEd Ex. 10.0 at 69-71.

ComEd argues that its proposed BE Plan allocation, as of Initial Briefs, reflects collaboration between ComEd, Staff, and intervenors. For example, in its rebuttal testimony, ComEd incorporated some of REACT’s feedback and proposals, and revised aspects of its BE Plan cost allocation. _See_ ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 18-19. Specifically, ComEd (1) revised its cost allocation between residential and non-residential delivery classes for certain BE Plan Pilot costs; (2) allocated Customer Education and Awareness (“CE&A”) costs evenly (50/50) between residential and non-residential delivery classes; and (3) removed the allocation of BE Plan costs to HV distribution facilities’ billing demand while maintaining billing demand allocation for service points connected at Primary Voltage and Secondary Voltage demands. _See_ ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 18-19. In surrebuttal testimony, ComEd accepted and implemented additional proposals from Staff related to allocation of certain BE Plan pilots. _See_ ComEd Ex. 10.0 at 15-16. And in response to a REACT data request, ComEd also revised its BE Plan CE&A residential/non-residential spend allocations to 15/85% based on the actual and estimated BE Plan 1 CE&A spend allocation from November 2023 through December 2024. _See_ Staff Cross Ex. 2.0. ComEd notes that these revisions result in an overall residential/non-residential BE Plan cost allocation of 17.75/82.25%. _See_ REACT Cross Ex. 10.

ComEd notes that certain intervenors criticize ComEd’s proposed BE Plan cost allocation, particularly the HV Delivery Class’s allocation (see, e.g., Nucor Ex. 1.0 at 10-11; IIEC Ex. 1.0 at 2, 17-18; REACT Ex. 2.0 at 12) and ComEd’s methodology of allocating BE Plan costs in the non-residential sector (see IIEC Ex. 2.0 at 11-12; REACT Ex. 6.0 CORR. at 11-12). ComEd argues that both criticisms are wrong. ComEd argues that its proposed BE Plan cost allocation methodology is a reasonable and appropriate means of implementing these new programs, particularly given the short time period over which the BE Plan rebates have been available. Moreover, ComEd notes that it has agreed to present rebate cost information by delivery class (or other information as available) in its next RDI after the rebates have been distributed for a longer period of time. ComEd Ex. 10.0 at 18. ComEd states that interested parties will be able to utilize that information to inform their proposals on future allocation and recovery of BE Plan costs. _Id._

Additionally, ComEd argues that the alternative allocation proposals suggested by certain intervenors cannot be adopted because they would lead to an inequitable allocation or would not reflect BE Plan budget forecasts for residential and non-residential sectors. _See_ ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 14-16. Specifically, intervenors criticize ComEd’s allocation among non-residential customer classes using billing demand and ComEd’s allocation of CE&A costs between residential and non-residential customer classes.

ComEd argues that the Commission should reject criticisms and proposals by BOMA/Chicago, IIEC, Nucor, and REACT regarding ComEd’s use of billing demand to allocate BE Plan costs among non-residential classes. _See_ BOMA/Chicago IB at 4-5 n.1 (noting alternatively that “[i]t may be fair to allocate BE Plan costs among delivery classes based on their percentage of the non-residential MYRP revenue requirement” or allocating costs through “the use of a fixed charge”); IIEC IB at 5, 7 (proposing to allocate costs using overall delivery service revenue requirements of each class due to the purported “public benefit” nature of the BE Plan costs); Nucor IB at 5-6 (proposing to allocate costs using overall non-BE Plan delivery service revenue requirements); REACT IB at 18-21 (proposing to allocate costs using Average Number of Customer Accounts).

ComEd argues that this argument is incorrect because the Commission has previously rejected it. In ComEd’s BE Plan 1 docket, the Commission denied ComEd’s request for rider treatment for BE Plan costs and ordered that those costs be recovered in ComEd’s delivery service rates in its MYRP proceeding. BE Plan 1 Order at 222. ComEd argues that, as a result of that Order, the Commission has clearly determined that BE Plan costs are “delivery services” for costs recovery purposes.

ComEd argues that the Commission should reject IIEC’s and Nucor’s proposal to allocate non-residential BE Plan costs using non-BE Plan delivery service revenue requirements. ComEd also argues that the Commission should reject REACT’s proposed Average Number of Customers allocator. ComEd explains that, under REACT’s proposal, the recipients of rebates would then

need to be responsible for their costs, effectively eliminating the rebates. ComEd argues that this is at odds with the goals of beneficial electrification, which include incentivizing lower emissions through electrification of various sectors of the economy. _See_ 20 ILCS 627/45(b).

ComEd also argues that REACT provides no support for its assumption that rebate adoption or cost causation is proportional to the number of customers in the class. ComEd argues that this betrays a fundamental misunderstanding of the features of ComEd’s BE Plan, which ComEd notes is predominantly aimed at incentivizing fleet vehicle adoption: ComEd’s Commission-approved BE Plan 1 includes $8 million for Residential programs and $58 million for Non-Residential programs. _See_ BE Plan 1 Order at 130. ComEd argues that the Commission should reject REACT’s proposal to use the Average Number of Customers allocator. ComEd notes that REACT also requests that the Commission order ComEd to categorize future BE Plan expenditures and budgets based on the various programs and customers served, and “create clear metrics for those expenditures and then track which customer class is participating[.]” ComEd argues that it already categorizes BE Plan expenditures and budgets by program. _See_ BE Plan 1 Order, Compliance Filing (May 25, 2023) at 31-44. The “CE&A budget and spend is separate and distinct from any delivery and marketing efforts under each Program and Sub-Program.” _Id._ In response to REACT data requests, ComEd notes that it separately analyzed whether its CE&A budget and spend was related to residential or non-residential customers. _Id._

ComEd notes that IIEC argues that CE&A costs should be split equally between the residential and non-residential sectors (essentially ignoring updated evidence), and REACT criticized the substance of the conclusion, reiterating its request that all BE Plan costs be allocated using the Average Number of Customers Allocator. _See_ IIEC IB at 4-5; REACT IB at 15-18. ComEd maintains that all of these arguments are without merit and the Commission should reject them.

ComEd argues that its proposed 85%/15% split is based on actual spend and reflects the most recent data available about the allocation of CE&A costs between the non-residential and residential sectors. _See_ Staff Cross Ex. 2. ComEd argues that the record evidence in this case is uncontroverted: the BE Plan 1 spend breakdown is the best proxy for the BE Plan 2 spend breakdown. _See id._ ComEd argues that BOMA/Chicago’s claim that the spend might change in the future would unnecessarily elevate hypothetical speculation over uncontroverted, up-to-date evidence and the Commission must reject it. ComEd notes that BOMA/Chicago also advocated for a “mechanism for annual reconciliation of BE Plan costs” based on the same assumption that future spending may differ from BE Plan 1 spending that formed the basis for ComEd’s allocation. BOMA/Chicago IB at 4. ComEd notes that it presented that feature in its proposed Rider BE, which the Commission rejected. _See_ ComEd Ex. 10.0 at 18-19. ComEd argues that, moreover, the suggestion is unnecessary because BE Plan

costs are already subject to annual reconciliation as part of ComEd’s MYRP. _See_ Rate MRPP, ILL. C. C. No. 10, Original Sheet No. 620.

ComEd argues that REACT’s claims that “ComEd has not explained how it determined what portion of the [CE&A] is ‘related to’ residential or non-residential customers” are also unfounded. REACT IB at 17. ComEd notes that it responded to data requests from REACT explaining the cost breakdown to residential and non-residential customers by tactics, vendor, and advertising channel. ComEd argues that, even if the Commission is inclined to give credence to REACT’s citation, there is no connection between “inclusive marketing,” i.e., marketing focused on reaching diverse audiences, and limiting such marketing to individuals as REACT insinuates – it could just as plausibly be directed to business owners or decisionmakers from diverse audiences.

ComEd argues that REACT appears to fundamentally misunderstand how the BE Plan budget operates, and its CE&A allocation proposals are meritless. ComEd requests that the Commission reject REACT’s critiques of ComEd’s proposed CE&A spend allocation and approve ComEd’s proposed allocation.

Staff notes that IIEC’s primary proposal to the Commission was that ComEd should adopt a uniform allocation of a 2% share of BE costs to customers classes in the ECOSS rather than using demand as an allocation factor. IIEC Ex. 1.0 at 11. Staff witness Sanders expressed concern that this allocation would not accurately reflect the varying contributions of different customer classes to BE Plan efforts or of the impacts to those different classes. Staff Ex. 5.0 at 6. As a result, it oversimplifies the allocation because it does not consider the relative contributions of large customers versus small users to BE goals such as emissions reduction and increased electrification. _Id._ Larger industrial and commercial customers generally have greater energy demands and emissions footprints. _Id._ IIEC’s alternative proposal calls for using ComEd’s assignment of costs to residential and non-residential customer groups as a baseline, and then updating it to allocate BE Plan costs. IIEC Ex. 1.0 at 17. Staff also disagrees with this approach because, according to ComEd, the delivery class data that would be required is not yet available. Staff Ex. 5.0 at 6. Staff Ex. 5.0 at 8. #### 3. REACT’s Position

REACT observes that there is unanimous agreement that the Commission should direct ComEd to revise the way in which it recovers the costs associated with its BE program. REACT notes that as a result, the question for the Commission is which allocator most accurately reflects cost causation.

REACT proposes a single, straightforward approach: assign all BE Program costs using the Average Number of Customers in each delivery services class. _See_ REACT Ex. 2.0 at 22. The Average Number of Customers allocator should be used both to divide the cost between residential and non-residential customers, as well as to allocate these costs among the non-residential customer classes. The one exception is that REACT agrees the RR Class should be assigned no costs since they can receive no benefit under these programs.

REACT observes that ComEd’s position regarding the allocation of its BE Plan costs has shifted significantly throughout this proceeding and has not been tied to an overarching allocation philosophy. In its most recent iteration, ComEd proposes to allocate 17.75% of all BE Program costs to residential customer classes and 82.25% to the non-residential customer classes. Further, ComEd proposes to allocate costs among the non-residential customer classes based on customer class billing demand. Staff agrees with ComEd’s position, even though neither ComEd nor Staff have performed any analysis that demonstrates a relationship between customer billing demand and participation in the BE Program.

REACT has consistently advocated for the use of the Average Number of Customers allocator to allocate BE Program costs between residential and non-residential customers. Because the BE Program is intended to support EV adoption in Illinois, and the majority of automobiles in Illinois are owned by individual consumers, REACT notes that it is logical to conclude that individual consumers present the highest possibility for participating in and benefitting from the BE Program. REACT cites a recent Illinois Secretary of State report which confirms that over 75% of motor vehicles in Illinois are registered to individuals. _See_ All Active Registrations Based on Expiration Date, November 2024 (last visited Nov. 7, 2024). REACT concludes that the Average Number of Customers allocator would most closely track the scalar potential of individual and commercially owned vehicles and thereby provide the most accurate allocation of BE Program costs to BE Program causers.

REACT notes that ComEd dismissed the use of the Average Number of Customers allocator and other recommendations out of hand, asserting without any analysis that “the alternative allocation proposals suggested by certain intervenors cannot be adopted because they would lead to an inequitable allocation or would not reflect BE Plan budget forecasts for residential and nonresidential sectors.” ComEd IB at 25. However, REACT observes that ComEd’s own proposal to assign costs based on customer demand is an inequitable allocation as it bears no relationship to the BE Plan budget forecasts and by ComEd’s own admission has no connection to likely participation in the BE Program. _See_ REACT Cross Ex. 8.03.

REACT explains that Staff likewise suggests that the Average Number of Customers allocator is inappropriate because it fails to account for “the increased financial capacity” and “the greater emissions-impact of larger customers.” Staff IB at 20. However, Staff provided no evidence regarding the financial capacity or the emissions impact of various customer classes. Moreover, such considerations have no bearing on the cost causation principles that are to guide the Commission in this proceeding. _See_ 220 ILCS 5/1-102(d)(iii); 220 ILCS 5/16-108(c).

REACT closely analyzed ComEd’s varied treatment of the CE&A program costs for the BE Program, which is a primary reason for ComEd shifting its allocation of BE Program costs between residential and non-residential customer classes. The CE&A budget amounts to $8.2 million per year and is supposed to inform ComEd customers of the opportunities available through the BE Program. _See_ Staff Cross Ex. 4.0. However, ComEd has no workpapers to support the “residential – nonresidential” split reflected in its budget and the CE&A budget lacks a level of completeness, detail, and structure that would be required to properly allocate these costs. _See_ REACT Cross Ex. 10. For instance, the CE&A budget does not mention or allocate costs to support primary outreach programs for large energy users (e.g., engagement by existing account managers, a “Fleet Electrification Assessment” tool) that ComEd claims will occur in its

BE Plan. _See_ Docket No. 24-0484, ComEd Beneficial Electrification Plan 2 at 46. Instead, ComEd indicates that $6.2 million (76%) of the CE&A budget is committed to engaging a third-party company that has no evidence of experience in marketing EV infrastructure to commercial or industrial clients. _See id._ at 46-47. REACT concludes that this and other aspects of the CE&A budget suggest that non-residential customer classes would be saddled with costs for outreach that will never orient towards their needs.

REACT also advocates for use of the Average Number of Accounts allocator to assign BE Program costs among the non-residential customer classes, noting that this approach would more accurately represent the potential for non-residential customers to participate in the BE Program. REACT Ex. 2.0 at 21-22. This approach would more appropriately result in BE Program costs reflecting the relatively smaller likelihood of large vehicle fleets being operated by customers in the Very Large, Extra Large, and HV customer classes. _See id._ at 22.

REACT notes that multiple parties agree that there is no relationship between cost causation and cost allocation under ComEd’s proposal to assign BE Program costs among non-residential customer classes based on customer class demand. ComEd was not able to produce any workpapers to support its unsupported assertion that customer class demand was an appropriate allocator for the BE Program costs among non-residential customers. _See_ REACT Cross Ex. 11. ComEd’s own witness could not provide any evidence, analysis, rationale or even a hint at how customer class demand for electricity in any way determines that a customer is more or less likely to participate in or benefit from the BE Program. _See_ Tr. 181-182.

#### 4. IIEC’s Position

IIEC points out that ComEd includes $77 million of BE Plan costs in its cost of service study for developing rates for service beginning in 2026. This amount was approved by the Commission as the average annual cost of BE in the period 2023-2025. IIEC asserts that the amount for 2026 is not yet known, but presumably ComEd will include, in 2026 and beyond rates, only that amount which, eventually, is approved for spending in those years.

IIEC notes that Illinois law is silent on how the BE Plan costs are to be allocated and collected among the customer classes. See, inter alia, IIEC Ex. 1.0 at 4, ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 11; see generally, 20 ILCS 627/45. IIEC maintains that BE Plan costs are not delivery services, as those are defined in Section 16-102 of the Act (220 ILCS 5/16-102). IIEC reasons that although the BE Plan costs may not be delivery services, per se, normal rate making principles and fundamental fairness suggest that they should be allocated and recovered from customers similar to delivery services, i.e., in accordance with Section 16-108(c) of the Act, which states: “Charges for delivery services shall be cost based, and shall allow the electric utility to recover the costs of providing delivery services through its charges to its delivery service customers that use the facilities and services associated with such costs.” 220 ILCS 5/16-108(c). IIEC believes that the Commission should allocate costs consistent with normal cost of service principles, to the extent feasible, and that such a determination should be made in this case.

IIEC states that the BE Plan costs were introduced into ComEd’s rates during the pendency of ComEd’s recent MYRP Docket. ComEd Ex. 2.0 at 67; IIEC Ex. 1.0 at 3. However, IIEC points out that both ComEd and the ALJs in that case indicated that testimony related to cost allocation and rate design were to be addressed in this rate design investigation case. _See_ IIEC Ex. 2.0 at 10. Thus, IIEC posits that the method proposed and adopted in the ComEd MYRP case has no precedential or determinative value in this case, as the merit of the allocation and recovery method was not addressed by other parties until this case.

IIEC notes that in its proposed cost of service study, ComEd followed a two-step process for assigning BE Plan costs to the rate classes. First, ComEd estimates the cost of BE Plan programs directed to the two large customer sectors, residential and non-residential, based on estimated distribution of subsidies like vehicle rebates and other BE Plan costs to these two sectors, and segregates those costs between these two sectors accordingly. Second, ComEd proposes to allocate the BE Plan costs allocated to the two sectors among the customer classes within these sectors in proportion to the forecasted energy consumption of the classes for the residential classes, and forecasted billing demand units of each class for the non-residential classes. IIEC also observes that ComEd proposes to exempt the RR Class from BE Plan cost recovery, under the guise of it already being fully electrified. ComEd Ex. 2.0 at 68.

Several parties, including IIEC, take exception to ComEd’s proposed allocation of costs arguing they are not cost justified, nor fair in application. _See_ IIEC Exs. 1.0, 2.0; REACT Exs. 2.0, 6.0; Nucor Ex. 1.0. IIEC notes that Staff was silent on the allocation of BE Plan costs in its direct testimony and waited until its rebuttal testimony to support ComEd’s position. _See generally_ Staff Exs. 2.0, 5.0. IIEC asserts that this procedural approach effectively deprived intervenor parties an opportunity to respond to Staff’s position in testimony. Further, IIEC maintains that this deprivation of opportunity is exacerbated, due to the fact that ComEd and Staff modified their testimony positions based on twelfth-hour responses to data requests, which were allowed into the record as purported cross-examination exhibits. IIEC argues that the Commission should give such un-vetted and procedurally unfair position changes little weight in its determination on this issue.

IIEC presented the direct and rebuttal testimonies of IIEC witness Stephens, a former Staff employee and long-time expert on electric utility cost of service matters. IIEC Exs. 1.0, 2.0. Mr. Stephens explained that ComEd’s BE Plan costs are for items that are not associated with traditional delivery service. He also explained that the nature of these costs, which are not delivery service costs, does not lend itself well to standard classification and allocation methods within a typical cost of service study. IIEC argues that rather, ComEd’s BE Plan costs, which are primarily for subsidies associated with rebates for EVs and associated charging equipment and infrastructure, should be allocated to classes based on the overall delivery service revenue requirements of each class. IIEC witness Stephens noted this is the same method used for allocating the cost of the ongoing subsidy to the RR Class, which the Commission determined was warranted due largely to the public benefit aspect of mass transit. IIEC Ex. 1.0 at 12; Docket No. 10-0467, Order at 259-260. IIEC reasons that, as in the case in which the railroad subsidy was approved, the costs here should be associated with purported public benefits. Further, the allocation of the RR Class subsidy has been previously vetted and approved by the Commission. IIEC Ex. 1.0 at 2, 5-12. Thus, IIEC asserts that the Commission’s accepted approach for allocating these public benefit costs should be extended to the BE Plan costs, since much of the cost is for public benefits associated with mass transit. _Id._ at 12.

IIEC points out that although Mr. Stephens’ initial position would not have required segregation between customer sectors at all, in rebuttal testimony, Mr. Stephens accepted ComEd’s step 1 segregation proposal as an effort to assign the BE Plan costs to the cost causers as much as possible. Mr. Stephens indicated that the Commission should require ComEd to be as granular as possible in this segregation, i.e., assign such costs to more than two sectors, residential and non-residential. _Id._ at 2, 17; IIEC Ex. 2.0 at 6-7. However, he noted that ComEd indicated in rebuttal testimony that assigning the costs to only two sectors is as granular as ComEd can get at this time for segregating based on cost. ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 15. Mr. Stephens concluded that such granularity will have to suffice until such time as it can be improved. IIEC Ex. 2.0 at 7.

IIEC notes that in rebuttal testimony, ComEd modified its segregation of costs between the residential and non-residential sectors to a more reasonable value proportion than ComEd presented in direct testimony, i.e., BE Plan costs allocated to the residential sector changing from 12.1% to 21.3%, and the non-residential section changing from 87.90% to 78.63%. ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 19; ComEd Ex. 5.02. IIEC supports the values in ComEd’s rebuttal testimony. IIEC points out that ComEd also modified its treatment of the HV class, to utilize only the determinants of the primary and secondary voltage portions of the customers in the allocation. ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 19. IIEC supports this change as well.

Considering step 2 of ComEd’s BE Plan cost proposal, IIEC insists that with regard to the allocation of the segregated sector costs to the rate classes within the sectors, there simply is no cost basis on which to allocate the charges as would be done with traditional delivery service costs. IIEC Ex. 1.0 at 8, 10. ComEd proposed the use of the billing demands of each class, which IIEC argues has no bearing on or relation to BE Plan costs. As such, IIEC witness Stephens proposed an alternative allocation than the one proposed by ComEd. Specifically, he proposed to recognize the similarities between ComEd’s BE Plan costs, which are primarily for subsidies associated with rebates for EVs and associated charging equipment and infrastructure, and the subsidy afforded the RR Class, and recommended that the BE Plan costs should be allocated to rate classes within the two large sectors based on the overall delivery service revenue requirements of each class, which he noted is the same method used for allocating the cost of the ongoing subsidy to the RR Class. IIEC points out that in both cases, the costs are associated with purported public benefits, and the allocation of the RR Class subsidy has been previously approved by the Commission. IIEC Ex. 1.0 at 2, 10-12. Accordingly, IIEC argues that allocation based on delivery service revenue is not only a similar and valid precedent for how to treat the BE Plan costs but is also reasonable. ComEd’s approach is completely novel and is not reasonable.

IIEC argues that ComEd’s use of billing demands as an allocation factor is unprecedented in its experience. IIEC Ex. 1.0 at 9. IIEC notes that Staff also confirmed this novelty in its response to IIEC Data Request 1.03. IIEC points out that in the response, Staff indicates it is not aware of any instance in which class billing demands have been used to allocate costs in a cost of service study. IIEC Cross Ex. 1.0. IIEC explains that billing demands are not used in cost of service allocations generally; rather, they are used in the rate design process, once cost of service determinations have been made. IIEC asserts that actual measures of external allocation factors used in a cost of service study, such as class demands, whether coincident peak, non-coincident peak, or temporal peaks are used for allocations, not billing demands. IIEC Ex. 2.0 at 9. IIEC does not find a logical nexus between BE Plan costs and the customers’ unrelated usage. IIEC claims that if the Commission approves a billing unit as a cost of service allocator, it may be the only public service commission in the country to reverse the uses of these factors, and in IIEC’s opinion, would be doing so without good reason. IIEC states that its proposal recognizes the Commission’s clear precedent on allocation of costs of transportation for public benefit subsidies and allows for consistency with prior Commission determinations and should be adopted.

IIEC finds Staff witness Sanders’ testimony that “[BE Plan] costs should primarily be socialized to ensure broad participation and shared responsibility” to be troubling. Staff Ex. 5.0 at 8-9. IIEC points out that Staff fails to cite to any statutory authority for this socialization. In addition, IIEC notes that Staff fails to explain why IIEC’s proposed method of allocation would not provide an acceptable form of socialization as well, albeit different from ComEd’s baseless approach. IIEC points out that in instances where the Illinois General Assembly wished to socialize costs, such as for renewable energy and carbon mitigation, it specified the recovery method. IIEC suggests that because the General Assembly did not do so for BE Plan costs, the Commission should direct ComEd to recover the costs fairly and with as much cost basis as possible, as is generally mandated under the Act. 220 ILCS 5/1-102(d)(iii). IIEC says that its proposal best fits these criteria. IIEC posits that absent any support for the ComEd allocation over the IIEC allocation, the Commission should approve IIEC’s allocation recommendation, as consistent with precedent for the RR Class subsidy costs.

Responding to Staff’s claim that IIEC’s proposal “does not consider the relative contributions of large customers versus small users to BE goals such as emissions reduction and increased electrification,” Staff Br. at 19, IIEC avers that this is not a legitimate criterion for allocation. Staff cites to no legislative authority relating BE to contributions to emissions reduction or increased electrification, as none exists. The relevant statutory authority for BE costs is 20 ILCS 627/45, which is focused on reducing emissions from the transportation sector and increasing access to electrification for all consumers. IIEC claims that this is not related to allocating BE Plan costs among delivery service rate classes. Further, IIEC avers that even if such legislative authority existed, which it does not, Staff has identified no metric for assessing the relative contributions of existing customer class loads which could be use in allocating costs. Certainly customers’ electric billing demand has no relationship to emissions reduction in the transportation sector or increased electrification.

#### 5. Metra’s Position

Metra supports the BE cost allocation methodology proposed by ComEd. Metra agrees with setting the RR Class cost allocation to zero because the RR Class’s infrastructure is already electrified. ComEd Ex. 2.0 at 68. Metra notes that the

Commission has approved ComEd’s recommended approach. _Id._ Since the MED is fully electrified, Metra would receive none of the benefits from ComEd’s BE Plan. CTA/Metra Joint Ex. 2.0 at 9–10.

#### 6. BOMA/Chicago’s Position

BOMA/Chicago’s position is that it generally supports the BE Plan cost apportionment between residential and commercial customers proposed by ComEd witness Leick in his rebuttal testimony (ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 20). BOMA/Chicago Ex. 2.0(Rev) at 3. This allocates 78.63% of BE Plan costs to non-residential customers and the remaining BE Plan costs to residential customers based on anticipated program budgets and customer participation levels between residential and non-residential customers. BOMA/Chicago Ex. 2.0(Rev) at 4. BOMA/Chicago’s position is that BE Plan cost recovery should adhere as closely to direct assignment as possible between residential and non-residential customer groups because a critical objective of fair rate design is “ensuring that customer groups that cause program costs due to participation are assigned a commensurate cost responsibility to the program.” _Id._ Thus, “direct assignment is a more appropriate basis for allocating BE Plan costs.” _Id._

Further, BOMA/Chicago opposes ComEd’s reallocation of CE&A costs as presented in ComEd’s surrebuttal testimony. BOMA/Chicago explains that until more robust data can be collected, BOMA/Chicago supports the recommendation made by ComEd witness Leick in rebuttal testimony to “allocate Customer Education costs evenly between residential and non-residential customers.” ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 18. During evidentiary hearings Mr. Leick expressed that his proposed reallocation of these costs in surrebuttal to a 15% residential and 85% non-residential split was due to the fact that he was “able to speak to people within the BE plan program that are more familiar with the customer education spend plan 1, and they told me that their budget plan and actual spend to date would be based on 15[%]residential, 85[%] non-residential, and not the 50/50 split.” Tr. at 111-112. However, BOMA/Chicago’s position is that this 15% residential and 85% non-residential split discussed by Leick based on internal discussions should be rejected because it is based on limited experience and information, and there is no evidence that the spend trend will continue or that it accurately reflects BE Plan 2.0 costs.

BOMA/Chicago also states that, while ComEd has provided current expectations for BE Plan program budgets and customer participation levels, actual budgets and participation are subject to uncertainty and potential future changes. ComEd acknowledges that BE Plan rebates have been available only for a “short time period.” ComEd IB at 25. Given the limited history of BE Plan expenditures, “[a]ctual expenditures are likely to vary significantly relative to initial program budgets as ComEd gains more experience with customer preferences and actual update of rebates.” _Id._ at 94-96. BOMA/Chicago is supportive of ComEd’s agreement to present actual rebate cost information in the next rate design investigation, after rebates have been distributed for a longer period of time, so that proposals for BE Plan cost allocation in that proceeding can more fairly align with principles of cost causation. ComEd IB at 25, BOMA/Chicago RB at 1. However, BOMA/Chicago is supportive of the Commission adopting a mechanism for reconciliation of BE Plan costs as part of this rate design proceeding so that apportionment between residential and non-residential customers reflect actual BE Plan budget and spending as they change compared to current expectations. BOMA/Chicago Ex. 2.0(rev) at 3.

BOMA/Chicago further agrees with positions taken by other intervenors noting that, BE Plan costs are different from traditional delivery service costs as defined in Section 16-102 of the Act and BE Plan cost causation is not driven by distribution demand. _Id._ at 10; _see also_ IIEC Ex. 1.0 at 5-6, 11-12; Nucor Ex. 1.0 at 7, 9. BOMA/Chicago notes:

> BE programs are defined in statute as those “that lower carbon dioxide emissions, replace fossil fuel use, create cost savings, improve electric grid operations, reduce increases to peak demand, improve electric usage load shape, and align electric usage with times of renewable generation” and “provide for incentives such that customers are induced to use electricity at times of low overall system usage or at times when generation from renewable energy sources is high.”

BOMA/Chicago Ex. 2.0(Rev) at 8 (citing 20 ILCS 627/45(b)); IIEC Ex. 1.0 at 5. BOMA/Chicago’s position is that an allocation based on peak demand is not fair because “[c]ustomer peak demand is unrelated to BE Plan costs and consumer adoption of BE Plan rebates.” _Id._ at 8; see also, REACT Ex. 2.0 at 20-22, IIEC Ex. 1.0 at 2, 6, 8; Nucor Ex. 1.0 at 9. “Since BE Plan programs are not core delivery service functions, it is unreasonable to allocate costs to customers on that basis.” _Id._ at 9. BOMA/Chicago notes that BE Plan costs are intended to support programs that incentivize use during periods of low overall demand and “[a]ll customers benefit to some degree from the environment improvements generated by BE programs such as reduced emissions and increased utilization of the distribution system through off-peak EV charging and other off-peak electrification measures. As a result, it is important to recognize these widespread benefits of BE Plan programs in a rate design that distinguishes these charges from demand-driven delivery service costs.” _Id._; 20 ILCS 627/45(b). BOMA/Chicago requests that the Commission consider these fairness issues before assigning BE Plan costs to non-residential customers based on peak demand, noting that base rates for ComEd customers may be based on peak demand but may also be assigned in alternate ways.

#### 7. Nucor’s Position

Nucor witness Pollock expressed concern that ComEd is proposing two different and completely inconsistent methods to allocate BE costs; not using the same direct assignment approach to allocate BE costs within the residential and non-residential customer classes. Nucor Ex. 1.0 at 4-5. Mr. Pollock explained that using billing demand to allocate BE costs among the non-residential customer classes fails to recognize the nature of the costs incurred and, thus, is not consistent with cost causation. Nucor Ex. 1.0 at 5-6. Mr. Pollock testified that there is no evidence that larger customers benefit more from emissions reductions than smaller customers and that use of billing demand, or any other metric such as kWh usage, as a proxy for measuring customer benefits is illogical. Nucor Ex. 1.0 at 7. Mr. Pollock’s recommendations included BE costs allocated to the HV class being recovered either as a per meter charge or as a percentage uplift to the delivery charge, the latter being essentially a revenue requirement allocation. Nucor Ex. 1.0 at 11.

Nucor supports the following ComEd revisions to the ECOSS model for the allocation of BE Plan costs:

- **(1)** Revise the cost allocation to residential and non-residential sectors by changing how the BE Pilot costs are allocated to residential and non-residential as seen in ComEd Ex. 5.02.

- **(2)** Allocate Customer Education costs evenly between the residential and non-residential sector (in other words, split it 50/50 between sectors).

_See_ ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 18. In addition, Nucor supports ComEd’s modification in its treatment of the HV Class to the extent the Company utilizes only the determinants of the primary and secondary voltage portions of the customers in the allocation.

Among Nucor witness Pollock’s primary concerns was that the use of billing demand to spread BE Plan costs within the non-residential customer classes is not consistent with cost-causation principles. For this reason, Nucor recommended that BE Plan costs be recovered as either a monthly charge per meter or “uplifted” to the delivery charges, which would effectively allocate and recover these costs in proportion to all other delivery charges. _See_ Nucor Ex. 1.0 at 5-9.

The Commission should, consistent with the testimony and. recommendations of IIEC witness Stephens and Nucor witness Pollock (see Nucor Ex. 1.0 at 4-7), direct ComEd to allocate BE costs within both the residential and non-residential sectors in accordance with non-BE Plan class revenue requirements.

#### 8. Commission Analysis and Conclusion

There is no specific mandate in P.A. 102-0662 on how BE Plan costs should be allocated or recovered. The Commission primarily supports cost causation-based allocation. Nevertheless, given the limited data currently available for these programs and the goals of P.A. 102-0662, the Commission must also consider whether BE Plan cost allocation strikes a fair balance between BE Plan program users and customers’ ability to contribute to those costs. No party appears to contest zero allocation of costs to the RR Class, and the Commission finds this a reasonable proposal given the unique nature of that class.

The primary issues raised by intervenors are ComEd’s use of billing demand to allocate costs among non-residential customers and the proposed allocation of CE&A costs between residential and non-residential classes. BOMA/Chicago, IIEC, Nucor, and REACT oppose using billing demand as an allocator for non-residential customers and recommend various adjustments. ComEd urges the Commission to reject intervenors’ alternative allocator recommendations for various reasons including: (1) the allocator does not reflect BE Plan budget forecasts; (2) the allocator does not consider contributions of various customers to conditions such as total emissions; (3) the allocator underestimates both benefits received by larger non-residential classes and their greater contributions to emissions; (4) the information to effectuate the allocator is not yet available; and (5) the proposal is unclear. The Commission agrees with ComEd’s concerns regarding the various proposals proffered by intervenors and those proposals are rejected. ComEd asserts that the billing demand allocation is appropriate because “that is the primary driver for the allocation amount.” ComEd Ex. 2.0 at 69. The Commission accepts this reasoning for now given the evidentiary record. As more data becomes available, the Commission expects that there may be appropriate adjustments to the cost allocation methodology adopted in this proceeding.

BOMA/Chicago, IIEC, and REACT also recommend the Commission reject ComEd’s proposal to allocate CE&A costs as 15% directed to residential customers and 85% to non-residential customers. In rebuttal testimony, ComEd supported a 50/50 split of CE&A costs between the residential and non-residential because “education and marketing materials are often designed as joint residential/non-residential material.” ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 18-19. It is apparent that ComEd’s BE Plan allocation recommendations have changed throughout the course of this proceeding in response to alternative proposals and as more information became available. ComEd’s final recommendation of a 15/85 split between the residential and non-residential classes for CE&A costs is based on actual spend and reflects the most recent data available. While BOMA/Chicago’s support of a 50/50 split and caution to wait until more data is available prior to making further changes to the CE&A allocation is reasonable, the Commission finds ComEd’s updated actual spend of 15/85 split persuasive and more in line with cost causation.

The Commission finds ComEd’s BE Plan cost allocation as updated throughout this proceeding and supported by Staff reasonable and it is adopted.

### E. HV Transformer Issues

#### 1. HV Transformer Charges

##### a. ComEd’s Position

ComEd explains that HV transformers are transformers and related equipment that bring power and energy to HV Delivery Class customers at high voltages (>69kV). _See_ ComEd 2.0 at 18; ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 25. ComEd allocates costs for HV transformers to HV Delivery Class customers through the HV Transformer Charge. ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 25. In recent years, the HV Transformer Charge has increased in price. _See id._ at 26.

ComEd argues that the Commission should adopt ComEd’s proposal regarding the HV Transformer Charge and reject REACT’s proposal. ComEd argues that its proposal preserves revenue neutrality by providing a mechanism to reallocate those costs – via adjusting the HV Delivery Class’s DFC – and is the best solution to this issue. ComEd argues that REACT’s proposal is inappropriate for consideration because it does not include a method of recovering the revenue requirement that would be transferred from the HV Transformer Charge and thus is not revenue neutral.

As an interim measure, ComEd proposes to change the 2026 and 2027 HV Transformer Charge back to the level of the 2024 HV Transformer Charge and reassign the unrecovered costs to the corresponding HV Delivery Class sub-class’s DFC, ensuring relief from escalating HV Transformer Charges and, importantly, maintaining revenue neutrality. ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 29. Under this illustrative proposal, the Over 10 MW HV Transformer Charge would change from $2.68/kW to $2.45/kW and their HV DFC would change from $0.52/kW to $0.68/kW, moving $1,485,480 of revenue requirement from the HV Transformer Charge to the HV DFC for this sub-class. The Up to 10 MW HV Transformer Charge would change from $5.02/kW to $4.56/kW and their HV DFC would change from $0.61/kW to $0.90/kW, moving $178,868 of revenue requirement from the HV Transformer Charge to HV DFC for this sub-class. _Id._ at 29-30. ComEd notes that IIEC supports this proposal. ComEd notes that, while IIEC supports ComEd’s proposal to reset charges for 2026 and 2027 to 2024 levels, it stated in a footnote that the Commission could initiate a proceeding on its own motion under Section 9-250 of the Act to modify ComEd’s MYRP and revise the 2025 HV Transformer Charge to 2024 levels as well. IIEC IB at 9 n.3. ComEd argues that this RDI is not the appropriate forum to request such a proceeding. _See_ 220 ILCS 5/9-250. Furthermore, ComEd argues there is no evidence in this docket to suggest that any of the conditions required to initiate a Section 9-250 proceeding (i.e., that ComEd’s rates are “unjust, unreasonable, discriminatory or preferential”) have been met, or that reopening the MYRP is justifiable on the grounds that IIEC suggests. _Id._ Moreover, even if such a drastic step were appropriate in this proceeding, ComEd argues there is no reason for the Commission to take it because IIEC participated as an intervenor in ComEd’s MYRP and could have raised this issue there, but did not.

ComEd argues that the Commission should reject REACT’s proposal, which would revert the HV Transformer Charge back to either 2011 or 2021 levels. _See_ REACT Ex. 6.0 CORR. at 19-20. ComEd argues that REACT’s proposal is not revenue neutral, because REACT witness Pruitt proposes no method of reallocating and recovering the revenue requirement that would be transferred from the HV Transformer Charge. ComEd Ex. 10.0 at 23.

ComEd explains that, in support of his proposal, REACT witness Pruitt theorizes that a potential reason for the increase in HV Transformer Charge is that “cross-subsidization” occurs with respect to the interconnection of new data center customers. REACT Ex. 6.0 CORR. at 16. ComEd notes that several of the data centers that witness Pruitt references are not even HV Delivery Class customers, making reliance on them even less relevant to the issue of HV Transformer Charge increases. _See_ ComEd Ex. 10.0 at 24. ComEd argues that witness Pruitt misunderstands how ComEd recovers costs associated with HV transformers and the distinction between standard and nonstandard service. _See_ ComEd Ex. 10.0 at 23. ComEd explains that the practice of functionalizing standard facilities costs to delivery classes and allocating them based on an appropriate methodology is in no sense a “cross-subsidization” or “cost shifting.” _Id._ If HV transformers are required as part of a customer’s standard service, those costs are appropriately allocated to the HV Transformer Charge and recovered from all customers receiving the service and subject to the charge. _Id._ ComEd notes that, in the specific example of data centers, the costs of HV transformers required by data centers as part of their standard service are shared among the approved class of customers, as are the revenues that the customers pay to use those and all other system facilities. _Id._ at 25. A corollary to the HV Transformer Charge is that if a customer requires nonstandard facilities or services to serve its electricity needs, such costs are attributed directly to and paid by that customer. _Id._ at 24.

##### b. Staff’s Position

Staff agrees with ComEd that this rate design proceeding must be revenue neutral. REACT’s proposal would change ComEd’s revenue requirement, which is not revenue neutral and thus outside the scope of this proceeding. ComEd’s proposed compromise, while not a perfect solution, would address REACT’s concern by reducing HV Transformer Charges in a way that is revenue neutral while anticipating a review of how costs are assigned to the HV Transformer Charge in ComEd’s next rate case and Staff recommends the Commission approve it. Staff Ex. 6.0 at 10.

Staff recommends that the Commission reject REACT’s recommendation that it should direct the Company to “roll back [T]ransformer [C]harge rates to 2011 levels (the last time the Commission had the full authority to review and adjust [T]ransformer [C]harge rates prior to formula ratemaking) until the causes of [T]ransformer [C]harge rates have been investigated and validated.” REACT IB at 32. This is a revenue neutral proceeding and any such changes that would change the revenue requirement are not revenue neutral.

##### c. REACT’s Position

REACT explains that ComEd’s HV Transformer Charge is imposed upon large energy users who require higher voltage electric service and elect to use transformers provided by ComEd rather than purchasing and maintaining their own transformers. If correctly calculated, the Transformer Charges should reflect the capital and maintenance costs associated with providing high voltage transformers and related facilities to these customers as well as the approved returns for ComEd.

REACT presented an analysis demonstrating that this single charge has been rapidly escalating in recent years – already increasing by 379% since 2013, it would increase by 500% over the 2013 level in 2027 under the rates proposed by ComEd in its MYRP case. _See_ REACT Ex. 1.0 at 10. REACT notes that ComEd confirmed the magnitude of these estimated cost increases but has been unable to explain why the increases have occurred. _See_ Tr. at 128. Despite repeated calls from REACT and Staff, ComEd has not been able to demonstrate how these costs are identified, characterized, or allocated in a manner that is consistent with the principle of cost causation. Thus, it appears that these massive increases have been imposed on these customers even when it appears they have done nothing to increase the transformation costs and ComEd has done nothing to improve the quality of the transformation service it provides to these customers. _See_ REACT Ex. 1.0 at 11-12.

REACT maintains that ComEd’s continued failure to provide any rationale for both the historical and proposed increases in the Transformer Charges may be indicative of cross-subsidization within the overall ComEd rate structure. _See_ REACT Ex. 6.0 at 15-16. ComEd’s vague theories concerning the potential causes of the increases in

transformer costs have been invalidated by ComEd’s own data, which shows that the number, voltage, and utilization of the portfolio of high voltage transformers that are supported by the Transformer Charge have not materially changed over the past decade. _See id._ at 14. REACT witness Pruitt explained that the three data centers that have been connected at the high voltage level in the ComEd service area since 2017 could be a cause for these increases if improper allocation of standard costs resulting from the data centers were applied to other high voltage customers. _See id._

Mr. Pruitt recommended that the Commission reset the transformer rate to the 2011 level, the last time the Commission heard a full rate case before the application of formula ratemaking under the Electric Infrastructure and Modernization Act, which is the point in time when ComEd’s Transformer Charge rates began their dramatic increases. _See id._ at 13, 19. Alternatively, Mr. Pruitt suggested it could be set at the 2021 level, which is when P.A. 102-0662 was passed. _See id._

REACT explains that this proposed adjustment of rates should be structured to achieve revenue neutrality by (1) resetting the Transformer Charge to the level it was set when a higher level of Commission review of Transformer Charges may have occurred; and then (2) characterizing any unrecovered revenues resulting from the adjustment in Transformer Charges as being general revenue requirements in nature. That is, since ComEd cannot demonstrate that they are specific to any particular class of customers they should be considered general revenue requirements to support ComEd’s operations and the costs should be allocated to all ComEd customers using an appropriate allocator.

REACT notes allocating unrecovered revenues resulting from the adjustment of Transformer Charges on a kWh basis to all ComEd customers would result in de minimis impacts for ratepayers of less than two hundred millionths of a cent per kilowatt hour. _See_ REACT RB at 28. This reallocation should continue until ComEd can discover why its ECOSS has generated the questionable cost allocations to HV Transformer Charge.

##### d. IIEC’s Position

IIEC points out that ComEd’s HV Transformer Charges have increased dramatically over the last several years, and with little explanation. IIEC notes further that these same costs are expected to rise even more in 2025 through 2027. IIEC points out that the HV transformers, for many of these customers, are long-lived assets and in most cases there have been no changes to the assets serving them which would warrant any increase, let alone large increases. _See_ IIEC Ex. 2.0 at 13 (citing to REACT testimony). IIEC notes that in response to arguments made, ComEd witness Leick proposed to change the 2026 (first year under rates resulting from this case) and 2027 HV Transformer Charge back to the level of the 2024 HV Transformer Charge. Mr Leick goes on to propose that costs that will not be recovered from the HV Transformer Charge, due to changing the 2026 and 2027 HV Transformer Charge back to the level of the 2024 HV Transformer Charge, will be assigned to the corresponding HV Delivery Class and that ComEd will review the charges in the context of its next rate case. ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 29.

IIEC supports ComEd’s approach as a step in the right direction, but notes that ComEd should have been reviewing the assignment of costs all along, i.e. before they got so high. IIEC Ex. 2.0 at 16-17.

##### e. Commission Analysis and Conclusion

REACT’s proposal is not revenue neutral as it would change ComEd’s revenue requirement and is therefore outside the scope of this proceeding. REACT’s proposal offers no method for recovery of costs that would no longer be recovered through the HV Transformer Charge. The Commission notes that Transformer Charges have been rapidly escalating in recent years. The Commission finds that ComEd’s revenue-neutral proposal in this proceeding will offer some relief from these escalating costs, and it is adopted.

#### 2. Proposed Requirement to Offer HV Transformer Customer Purchase Option

##### a. ComEd’s Position

ComEd argues that the Commission should reject REACT’s proposal (which is supported by IIEC) to require ComEd to sell its existing HV transformers to customers at net book value. _See_ REACT Ex. 2.0 at 26; IIEC Ex. 2.0 at 16. ComEd notes that it is not in the business of selling used HV transformer equipment – it is in the business of building and maintaining an electric grid for the safe, reliable, and affordable distribution of electricity. ComEd argues that REACT’s proposal would adversely impact ComEd’s performance of this mission and is contrary to the law and public policy. ComEd explains that, if adopted, REACT’s proposal would also create uncertainty around ComEd’s planning and maintenance of its grid facilities that it initially purchased with the understanding that it would own them over their useful life. ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 28. Furthermore, ComEd notes that customers have always been allowed to purchase their own transformers up-front, so REACT’s proposal is not necessary. _Id._ at 24. ComEd notes that Staff agreed with ComEd that it should not be forced to sell its HV transformers, acknowledging that ComEd’s purpose in acquiring the HV transformers was to provide a service, not resell them to its customers. _See_ Staff Ex. 6.0 at 9.

ComEd argues that REACT’s proposal to force ComEd to sell its HV transformers is contrary to the statutory requirements of this RDI. ComEd explains that REACT’s proposal is not revenue neutral because it would remove assets from rate base and therefore lower ComEd’s distribution revenue requirement. ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 24. ComEd argues that, based on the simple fact that REACT’s proposal falls outside the scope of this revenue neutral proceeding, it must be rejected. _See_ 220 ILCS 16-105.5(c)(1). ComEd notes that REACT claims that the Commission can simply set the purchase price at a level that would be revenue neutral from ComEd’s perspective. REACT IB at 27. ComEd argues, however, that it is not the Commission’s job to formulate a statutorily compliant plan for REACT and REACT has proposed none.

ComEd argues that REACT’s proposal would also violate Section 16-103(e) if implemented, as it would be an additional required tariffed service not otherwise required by Section 16-103. Section 16-103(e) prohibits the Commission from requiring “an electric utility to offer any tariffed service other than the services required by this Section, and shall not require an electric utility to offer any competitive service.” 220 ILCS 5/16-103(e). The services that utilities are required to offer are “delivery services in accordance with this Article, the power purchase options described in Section 16-110 and real-time pricing as provided in Section 16-107.” 220 ILCS 5/16-103(b). ComEd argues that the Act nowhere states that ComEd must offer customers the ability to purchase any kind of equipment from its rate base, let alone HV transformers. ComEd argues that forcing it to do so would constitute an entirely additional service that the Commission is not statutorily authorized to require.

ComEd argues that REACT’s proposal would unfairly deprive ComEd and its customers overall of the benefit of assets that it purchased with the intention of owning them for the duration of their useful lives. A transformer has an asset life over decades. If a transformer is still capable of safely and reliably being deployed after its net book value reaches $0, ComEd argues that its customers should be allowed to continue benefiting from that transformer at no additional cost. ComEd argues that a forced sale at $0 net book value would require ComEd (and its customers) to unnecessarily incur the cost for a replacement. Furthermore, ComEd claims that setting a single point in time at the current rate of return for the “sale point” could allow customers to arbitrage the point at which the transformer has a low enough “book life” value to incentivize the customer to move forward with the purchase of their transformers. In addition, ComEd notes that REACT has not proposed any methodology for determining the appropriate net book value for sale purposes, which may be different for example, than the net book value for tax purposes.

ComEd also argues that customers might face unanticipated risks if they were to take possession of ComEd’s used HV transformers (particularly if they were able to buy them at very low net book values), including unexpected purchase costs (which could be in the millions of dollars); the lead time on ordering additional parts or equipment (which could be several months or longer); potential difficulties in finding qualified workers to perform maintenance (especially on older transformers); and handling successor customer or service reconfiguration needs (i.e., successor customers requiring HV transformers due to the predecessor taking the customer-owned HV transformer off-site). ComEd Ex. 5.0. at 27. ComEd notes that REACT’s proposal could negatively impact ComEd’s Commission-approved Performance Metrics related to outage frequency and duration if customers are unprepared for the challenges associated with HV transformer ownership and are not able to maintain them in a way that ensures they do not adversely impact ComEd’s distribution system overall. _Id._ at 28. Staff likewise agreed that there may be reliability concerns with allowing any customer to purchase their used transformer, noting that “it is unclear from a policy perspective that the same level of assurance [in planning for risks by customers who purchase their transformers up-front] would exist if any customer could opt to buy a transformer.” Staff IB at 23. ComEd argues that the Commission should reject REACT’s proposal to deprive ComEd and its customers of the benefit of its assets by forcing ComEd to sell them.

ComEd notes that REACT and IIEC argue that, because HV Delivery Class customers are “highly sophisticated customers,” they would be able to make informed decisions on the purchase HV transformers and the risks involved. REACT IB at 28; IIEC IB at 10. ComEd argues that this assertion is belied by the very fact that REACT’s and IIEC’s members chose not to purchase their transformers when they were originally presented with the option and REACT now claims they have been subject to an unanticipated risk of cost increases. _Id._

##### b. Staff’s Position

Staff does not believe the Company should be forced to give its customers the option of buying the Company’s transformers and opposes REACT’s recommendation. Staff Ex. 6.0 at 9. Staff disagrees with the proposed requirement to give HV customers an option to purchase transformers because the purpose of acquiring the transformers was not to resell them to the Company’s customers but to provide a service to its customers. Staff Ex. 6.0 at 9-10. Staff believes the sale of transformers raises numerous questions, such as how long someone must be a ComEd customer to be regarded as an “existing customer” and how transformer sales would affect rates of customers in that customer class. _Id._ Additionally, Staff is concerned that there may be reliability concerns. ComEd testified that customers who own transformers know and plan for the risk associated with owning them. ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 28. However, it is unclear from a policy perspective that the same level of assurance would exist if any customer could opt to buy a transformer. Staff Ex. 6.0 at 9-10.

##### c. REACT’s Position

REACT notes that high voltage customers currently served by ComEd-owned transformers currently do not have any recourse to address these unjustified high and rising transformer rental charges. REACT requests that the Commission provide these large and sophisticated customers the option to escape ComEd’s continuous rate increases that represent millions of dollars of added costs to these businesses by allowing them to purchase the transformers from ComEd at a rate that ensures revenue neutrality for ComEd. _See_ REACT Ex. 2.0 at 25; REACT Ex. 6.0 at 20.

REACT explained why each of ComEd’s objections should not prevent the Commission from providing this self-help option to the aggrieved customers. First, ComEd incorrectly claimed that REACT’s proposal is not revenue neutral. _See_ ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 24. However, REACT has made it clear that the Commission should set the purchase price in a way that makes this revenue neutral from ComEd’s perspective. _See_ REACT Ex. 6.0 at 19.

ComEd next asserted that it is not “in the business of selling used HV transformer equipment – it is in the business of building and maintaining an electric grid for the safe, reliable, and affordable distribution of electricity.” ComEd IB at 28. REACT clarified that it does not intend to change ComEd’s business structure, but ComEd is not providing “affordable” service when it is increasing rates to individual customers by more than $1 million per year without explanation. REACT’s proposal is a reasonable method for addressing the unaffordability of this unjustified component of ComEd’s delivery services rates.

ComEd further asserted “customers have always been allowed to purchase their own transformers upfront, so REACT’s proposal is not necessary.” ComEd IB at 28. Thus, ComEd appeared to blame the very customers that are suffering under exponentially increasing rates for not anticipating that ComEd would impose unjustified increases, continuing to punish them for deciding decades ago not to purchase their own transformers. Such a viewpoint is a reminder why it is the role of the Commission to ensure that utility monopolies cannot simply dismiss legitimate concerns of captive customers.

ComEd also asserted that allowing these customers to purchase the transformers would expose them to new costs and risks. _See_ ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 28. However, REACT has explained that these are the same large, sophisticated customers that were given the option to purchase these exact transformers upfront; they are fully capable of making the cost-benefit analysis to determine whether exercising this option would be beneficial. _See_ REACT Ex. 5.0 at 8, REACT 6.0 at 19. Moreover, ComEd witness Leick admitted that ComEd’s system is designed to address any safety or reliability concerns. _See_ Tr. at 121. REACT clarified that any costs associated with system reconfiguration should be part of the purchase price to the customer if and when it exercises this option.

Finally, for the first time in this proceeding, ComEd asserts in its Reply Brief that this proposal is prohibited by Section 16-103(e) of the Act. This untimely attempt to restrict the Commission’s ability to grant relief likewise lacks merit. Section 16-103(e) states that the Commission “shall not require an electric utility to offer any competitive service.” 220 ILCS 5/16-103(e). Thus, ComEd would have to explain how selling the existing transformers that are serving the customers’ facilities is a “competitive service.” To be clear, REACT is requesting the Commission direct ComEd to offer to its captive customers the option to purchase the transformers that are currently servicing these facilities; no other company can provide that service. Moreover, as ComEd has made clear, ComEd already offers the option to purchase transformers. _See_ ComEd IB at 28 (“customers have always been allowed to purchase their own transformers upfront ….”) This proposal would simply change the rate design of that existing service by allowing existing customers an additional opportunity to exercise that option.

In sum, REACT notes that there is no legitimate reason to deny these customers the opportunity to purchase their transformers, empowering them to avoid an unjustified charge that seems destined to continue to rapidly increase.

##### d. IIEC’s Position

IIEC points out that REACT proposed that the Commission should direct ComEd to allow affected HV Transformer customers to terminate their obligations under the Transformer Charges by allowing current customers the option of purchasing their existing transformers at a price that reflects the depreciated value of those assets and then be excluded from future Transformer Charges. REACT Ex. 2.0, at 12. IIEC supports this proposal.

IIEC points out that ComEd responded to this proposal in the rebuttal testimony of Mr. Leick, where he concluded that that existing customers should not be given the option to purchase their existing HV transformers. ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 27-28. Mr. Leick testified that selling HV transformers will lower ComEd’s distribution rate base and claims that a one-time payment will not cover the financing costs nor afford the company any opportunity to earn a return on the asset. _Id._ at 5. Mr. Leick also expresses concerns that these customers may not realize the risks associated with owning high voltage equipment, such as: the costs, which might be in the millions of dollars; the lead time on ordering which could be several months or longer; the potential difficulty in finding qualified workers; and how to handle successor customer or service reconfiguration needs. _Id._ at 27.

Regarding Mr. Leick’s final point, IIEC respectfully suggests that customers contemplating such a purchase are sophisticated and will take these considerations into account before such a purchase. IIEC suggests that hypothetical concerns regarding the risks is not a reasonable justification for denying sophisticated customers the option. Regarding the point related to recovery of financing costs and earning a return on assets, IIEC witness Stephens pointed out that ComEd will have been earning a return of the cost of the asset, through depreciation expense, as well as a return on the asset for years. Also, IIEC points out that the sale of a transformer asset at net book value will both fully return ComEd’s undepreciated investment and provide a financial contribution that will allow ComEd to invest in other delivery service assets and earn a return on such new investment, should it choose to do so. IIEC Ex. 2.0 at 14-15. Thus, IIEC argues that unless ComEd knows its charges for HV Transformers are too high, it should be largely indifferent as to how it recovers its remaining investment, i.e., its net book value through rates or through a direct payment by customers. Finally, IIEC notes that because no ComEd investment or cost recovery would be disallowed, ComEd’s claims regarding upsetting revenue neutrality, e.g. ComEd Ex. 10 at 23, should not control. Notwithstanding, if there is a net impact on ComEd, then IIEC avers that the Commission should grant customers this option at the first available instance where ComEd’s revenues are subject to change, whether that occurs in the next MYRP case, in the annual adjustment process set forth in Section 16-108.18(f)(6) of the Act, or in the special circumstances prescribed in Section 16-108.18(d)(15) of the Act.

##### e. Commission Analysis and Conclusion

REACT requests that the Commission require ComEd to offer a mandatory customer purchase option for ComEd’s used HV transformers. Customers have an initial option to purchase their HV transformers. If not initially purchased by the customer, ComEd purchases and maintains HV transformers to service its customers, which comes with associated charges. The purpose of any purchase of HV transformers is to provide a service to customers.

The Commission is concerned REACT’s proposal would remove assets from rate base and therefore lower the distribution revenue requirement, rendering the proposal not revenue neutral and outside the scope of this proceeding. REACT asserts that the Commission should set the purchase price to make this proposal revenue neutral from ComEd’s perspective. It is not clear exactly how the Commission would accomplish this. An HV transformer could safely and reliably be deployed after its net book value reaches $0. The Commission agrees that ComEd customers should be able to continue benefiting from used HV transformers at no additional cost under those circumstances. The Commission further notes that there are questions as to the methodology in determining an appropriate net book value for resale purposes. Even if REACT’s proposal was within scope of this proceeding, the record does not provide the Commission the information necessary to establish an appropriate price methodology.

The Commission notes there are potential reliability concerns raised by ComEd and Staff associated with this proposal. For all the aforementioned reasons, the Commission rejects REACT’s proposal to mandate an optional purchase option for used HV transformers.

The Commission acknowledges REACT’s desire to purchase a previously rented piece of equipment at a depreciated price. An optional purchase of already installed and used equipment may be appealing should rental cost variances occur. Nonetheless, the Commission acknowledges this proposal could have an impact on rates. The Commission encourages the parties to work with one another to find a solution that appropriately balances the interest of all parties and ratepayers.

#### 3. Proposed Use of Rider NS in Lieu of Future Transformer Rental Charges

##### a. ComEd’s Position

ComEd argues that REACT’s proposal to recover costs for future HV transformer installations through Rider NS should also be rejected. Like REACT’s first proposal to force ComEd to sell its HV transformers, this proposal is not revenue neutral. This proposal removes costs from rate base and denies ComEd an opportunity to earn a rate of return over the life of the HV transformer. ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 24. ComEd also argues that such a proposal is discriminatory, as existing HV customers will have their transformer costs socialized, but new HV customers will not.

ComEd notes that, in its initial brief, REACT claimed that ComEd’s normal practice of functionalizing costs for standard facilities and service to delivery classes and allocating them based on an appropriate methodology – as opposed to using Rider NS – is “cross-subsidization” or “cost-shifting.” _See_ REACT IB at 29. In arguing for the necessity of its proposal to require ComEd to recover costs for future HV transformer installations through Rider NS, ComEd notes that REACT claimed that the “logical conclusion” of the HV Transformer Charge increase and ComEd’s current inability to pinpoint the exact cause is that the increases are the result of “potential cross-subsidization” or the inclusion of “non-transformer costs incurred elsewhere in the ComEd system.” REACT IB at 29. ComEd argues that there are various reasons that could explain why the HV Transformer Charge has been increasing (including some proposed by ComEd witness Leick in his rebuttal testimony), but there is no single “logical conclusion” that can be drawn without more information and study. _See_ ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 27, 29. ComEd argues that, contrary to REACT’s contention, ComEd’s practice of allocating costs for standard installations allows these costs to go into ComEd’s rate base and be recovered from all customers in the delivery class over the life of the asset, as those assets benefit all customers who use them. ComEd Ex. 10.0 at 25.

ComEd notes that Staff recommends that ComEd “identify costs that are caused by new HV customers which can be assigned to new HV customers … consistent with [cost] causation principles as part of any further discussion.” Staff IB at 24. ComEd argues that, while not directly advocating for cost recovery under Rider NS, Staff appears to be suggesting that ComEd assign costs incurred by new HV Delivery Class customers directly to those customers. ComEd argues that this proposal suffers from the same flaws discussed above, namely that this is not how ComEd allocates costs incurred by HV customers (or any other customers) for standard service – ComEd pools costs and allocates them on a delivery class-wide basis using an appropriate allocation factor. ComEd Ex. 2.0 at 12. ComEd argues that it would be inequitable to other customers if certain costs for standard service are applied on a delivery class-wide basis and other costs for standard service are applied directly to individual customers. ComEd requests that the Commission therefore reject both Staff and REACT’s proposals to utilize Rider NS to recover costs from HV transformers for customers going forward.

##### b. Staff’s Position

Staff recommended ComEd identify costs that are caused by new HV customers and assign such cost to the new customers responsible to be consistent with cause causation. Staff Ex. 6.0 at 11. In surrebuttal, ComEd responded that it would take the Company months to develop a new methodology for HV Transformer Charges. ComEd Ex. 10.0 at 22. However, the Company stated they are not opposed to addressing this issue and welcomes discussions on revising the methodology outside of this case. _Id._

Staff does not oppose to the Company collaborating with REACT and HV Class customers on this issue outside this docket to develop a billing system that is consistent with cost-causation principles, and which is agreeable to both the Company and customers. Staff Ex. 6.0 at 11. However, Staff continues to recommend the Company identify costs that are caused by new HV customers which can be assigned to new HV customers and assign such cost to the new customers consistent with cause causation principles as part of any further discussion. _Id._

##### c. REACT’s Position

Although ComEd has offered to study this issue to determine whether and to what extent there is improper cross-subsidization, REACT warns that an elongated study period or lack of resolution on the matter presents significant risks to those customers that currently rent transformers from ComEd. To provide more immediate relief, REACT has proposed that the Commission require ComEd to utilize Rider NS - Nonstandard Services and Facilities to recover high voltage transformer costs from any new customers who choose not to purchase their own facilities.

REACT notes that ComEd witness Leick identified that data center development could be a contributing factor to the increases in transformer rental rates. _See_ Tr. at 150. Multiple data centers with a combined peak demand of approximately 5,000 MWs are under some level of development in the ComEd region. REACT warns that, based on how ComEd has socialized prior data center service extension costs, these new data centers represent potentially billions of dollars of socialized costs to consumers. If there is even a slight possibility that socialized costs for data center development have been or could be inordinately applied to renters of transformers owned by ComEd, then it would be prudent for the Commission to adopt additional measures to ensure that the principles of cost causation are maintained.

REACT requests that Rider NS be used as a mechanism for recovering transformer costs for all future new customers that require ComEd to provide high voltage transformers as part of their service. _See_ REACT Ex. 6.0 at 20. Essentially, the Commission would “close” the over-10 MW high voltage transformer rental rate, and it would be unavailable to new customers after the date of the Commission’s Order in the instant proceeding. REACT clarifies that the pricing associated with this revision should result in this being revenue neutral to ComEd. REACT explains that this approach is consistent with Staff’s recommendation that “the Company identify costs that are caused by new HV customers which can be assigned to new HV customers and assign such cost to the new customers consistent with cause causation principles as part of any further discussion.” Staff IB at 24.

##### d. Commission Analysis and Conclusion

REACT proposes that costs for all new HV transformer installations be recovered through Rider NS. Adopting this proposal could deny ComEd an opportunity to earn a rate of return over the life of the HV transformer because “if the rate of return changes at a future point in time because Rider NS rentals are not periodically updated and do not always reflect recent O&M charges.” ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 24. Accordingly, the Commission finds that REACT’s proposal is not revenue neutral and therefore outside the scope of this proceeding. Moreover, the Commission notes that Rider NS is for nonstandard services for which customers generally pay for such services at the outset and HV transformer customers already have the option to initially purchase the transformer.

#### 4. HV Transformer Rate Study

##### a. ComEd’s Position

ComEd proposes to conduct an HV Transformer Rate Study. ComEd acknowledges REACT’s concern that HV Transformer Charges have increased in recent years. Therefore, ComEd proposes that in its next rate case filing, it will review the assignment of costs to HV transformers to see if unexpected costs are assigned to the HV Transformer Charge and what, if any, action should be taken to revise its methodology. ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 29. This additional data and analysis will require careful consideration of the current allocation methodology, and a review of how new customers impact the charge, including any HV transformer costs being recovered under Rider NS. ComEd Ex. 10.0 at 22. ComEd states that this process will take several months or longer. ComEd has committed to having further discussions with stakeholders regarding this issue outside of this proceeding. _Id._; _see also_ REACT Cross Ex. 7.0.

ComEd notes that REACT recommends that the Commission “[o]pen an investigation to oversee the high voltage rate study to allow parties to issue and respond to data requests and to ensure transparency and timeliness of results.” REACT IB at 31. ComEd argues that such a step is premature at this time and REACT has not presented evidence that the statutory standard for an investigation has been met. ComEd argues that the Commission can only open an investigation when there are “reasonable grounds to believe that the audit or investigation is necessary to assure that the utility is providing adequate, efficient, reliable, safe, and least-cost service and charging only just and reasonable rates therefor, or that the audit or investigation is likely to be cost-beneficial in enhancing the quality of service or the reasonableness of rates therefor.” _See_ ComEd IB at 39, 45 (quoting 220 ILCS 5/8-102). ComEd argues that REACT has identified no evidence that this standard has been met. ComEd requests that the Commission reject REACT’s recommendation to open an official investigation and instead allow the parties to informally work together to study this issue.

##### b. Staff’s Position [source labels this subsection “a. Staff’s Position”]

REACT recommends the Commission open an investigation to oversee a high voltage rate study. Staff does not oppose this recommendation and continues to recommend a collaborative engagement between the Company and stakeholders. ComEd proposed to review the assignment of costs to HV transformers in its next rate case to know if unexpected costs are being assigned and if anything could be done about the methodology. ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 29. Staff recommends the Company collaborate with REACT and customers in the HV class to develop a billing methodology that works for both parties and is consistent with the principal of cost causation. Staff Ex. 6.0 at 11.

##### c. REACT’s Position [source labels this subsection “b. REACT’s Position”]

REACT notes that ComEd has proposed working with Staff, REACT, as well as other large customers and interested stakeholders to identify the underlying causes of the enormous increases in transformer rental rates. ComEd witness Leick estimated that ComEd could begin its evaluation of transformer rental rates within a few months' time. _See_ Tr. at 140-141. As part of this process, ComEd would review its ECOSS, Rider NS charges to its existing Over-10 MW HV customers, and the loading on the transformers serving those customers to confirm that there are no distribution facilities costs improperly included in the transformer rental charge. _See_ Tr. at 139-140.

REACT explains that this proposed study is essential to the interests of large energy users served by ComEd and requests that the Commission enforce a high level of transparency in the process by ensuring that participating parties can obtain, test and confirm the appropriate data. One tool available to the Commission would be initiate an investigation regarding this charge, which would enable Commission oversight of the timing and production of the relevant data and provide a forum for parties to develop and propose solutions to address this issue. Further, REACT requests that the Commission make clear that the proposed study will investigate whether past ComEd rates for high voltage transformer rental have cross-subsidized other customer classes and also determine the veracity of the reasons for further rate increases.

##### d. Commission Analysis and Conclusion [source labels this subsection “c. Commission Analysis and Conclusion”]

The Commission adopts ComEd’s proposal to conduct an HV Transformer Rate Study. ComEd is directed to work with interested stakeholders to investigate its cost allocation methodology to the HV Transformer Charge. REACT’s proposal for an official Commission investigation into this issue is denied. The Commission is not persuaded that an investigation proceeding is warranted at this time and finds that a less formal collaborative effort among ComEd and interested stakeholders to investigate HV Transformer Charges is appropriate.

### F. Updating CP/NCP Allocators to Reflect Recent Weather Normalization

ComEd requests that the Commission approve its proposal to update the weather normal coincident peak/non-coincident peak (“CP/NCP”) cost allocators from 2018 to 2021. According to ComEd, this will minimize dramatic increases in costs to residential customers and eliminate anomalous weather events from the data set. _See_ ComEd Ex. 2.0 at 39-40. Staff supports ComEd’s proposal to update the weather normal CP/NCPs from 2018 to 2021, and no party opposes ComEd’s proposal. _See_ Staff Ex. 2.0 at 6; ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 7.

ComEd states that ComEd periodically updates its CP/NCP cost allocators to weather normalize them so that weather-sensitive customers will not be allocated a higher-than-normal amount of costs if the CP/NCP data set was captured during a year with an abnormally hot summer or cold winter. ComEd Ex. 2.0 at 39-40. ComEd analyzed data from 2019-2023 to determine which year would be the most suitable to use to update the weather normal CP/NCP cost allocators and concluded that 2021 would be the most appropriate because it was not affected by the COVID-19 pandemic (2020), it was not affected by abnormally hot temperature peaks compared to 10-year averages (2022 and 2023), and it is not outdated data (2019). _See id._ at 41-44.

This issue is uncontested. The Commission approves ComEd’s proposal to update the weather normal CP/NCP cost allocators from 2018 to 2021.
