## Introduction

The electric grid is entering a period of rapid transformation driven by evolving customer expectations, emerging technologies, and clean energy mandates. Utilities must plan for a system that ensures reliable and resilient service, supports the cost-effective integration of distributed energy resources, and maintains affordability for customers. This requires a forward-looking plan that not only addresses current system performance, but also advances the long-term outcomes related to equity, environmental sustainability, and the evolving role of electricity in customers' daily lives.

To address the rapid changes affecting the distribution grid, the Maryland Public Service Commission (the "Commission") issued Order No. 89865[^1] on June 23, 2021, to establish the Distribution System Planning Work Group ("DSPWG") and directed the DSPWG to conduct a comprehensive examination of distribution system planning in Maryland. During the 2022 legislative session, the Maryland General Assembly enacted the Climate Solutions Now Act ("CSNA").[^2] The CSNA, which became effective on June 1, 2022, among other things, adopted the language codified in the Annotated Code of Maryland, Public Utilities Article ("PUA"), § 7-801 et seq. Those sections directed the Commission to adopt regulations or issue orders to implement specific policies for electric distribution system planning ("DSP" or "ESP") and improvements. On February 6, 2023, the DSPWG filed the Phase I Report reflecting its comprehensive examination of distribution system planning in Maryland.[^3]

On August 24, 2023, the Commission issued Order No. 90777,[^4] directing the DSPWG to continue to implement CSNA requirements and other electric distribution system planning improvements in Phase II. During the 2024 legislative session, the Maryland General Assembly enacted House Bill 1393 ("HB 1393"), the Electric System Planning - Scope and Funding Act[^5] which required the Commission to adopt regulations or issue orders to implement specific policies for electric system planning on or before December 1, 2025. On May 1, 2025, the Work Group filed draft regulations culminating the Work Group's Phase II efforts. Subsequently, on May 2, 2025, the Commission docketed rulemaking proceeding RM 89.[^6] On July 28, 2025, the Commission issued Order No. 91751 launching Phase III and directing the Work Group to propose a schedule by November 1, 2025 for Electric Company Annual Plan Updates and Preliminary Electric System Plans ("ESPs"), including a recommendation regarding the scheduling of an annual Technical Conference and deadlines for related activities.[^7] The Commission also requested an electric company volunteer to file a Preliminary ESP in 2026 for consideration at an annual Technical Conference in August 2026.

Further, on August 29, 2025, the Commission issued Order No. 91799 directing DSPWG to make recommendations for integrating reliability and resilience planning into ESPs.[^8] The DSPWG issued the Report on November 3, 2025.[^9] Subsequently, the Commission issued Order No. 92052 directing electric companies participate in the August 2026 Technical Conference, and for Baltimore Gas and Electric Company ("BGE") to file its Preliminary ESP in June of 2027.[^10]

The 2026 Annual System Plan Update is BGE's preliminary filing of its ESP for consideration at the annual Technical Conference in August 2026. It represents the utility's plan for meeting the challenges of its evolving electric grid while advancing the State's clean energy goals and delivering customer value. This plan outlines BGE's current processes, planning criteria, and investment needs to deliver a clean, reliable, resilient, affordable, and equitable energy future for its customers.

## Distribution System Overview

BGE was founded in 1816 as the United States' first gas light utility by Rembrandt Peale, a scientist and entrepreneur who was interested in gas light to illuminate his art gallery.[^11] More than two hundred years later, the Company has evolved into an electric transmission and distribution and gas distribution company. The utility provides essential service to more than 1.3 million electric customers in a diverse, 2,300-square-mile area encompassing Baltimore City and all or part of 10 central Maryland counties through approximately 338 substations[^12] and 1,500 feeders. BGE's distribution system is the foundation of a safe, reliable, and increasingly dynamic energy future for over 3.1 million people in the Baltimore area.

BGE's parent company, Exelon (Nasdaq: EXC), is a Fortune 200 company and one of the nation's largest utility companies, serving almost 11 million customers through six fully regulated transmission and distribution utilities. Exelon serves a combined area of 25,550 square miles. Exelon is committed to improving reliability and operations, enhancing customer experience, and advancing clean and affordable energy choices in all its service territories.

## Overview

BGE's distribution planning process supports long-term system reliability, anticipated load growth, electrification, distributed energy resource ("DER") integration, and compliance with the Code of Maryland Regulations ("COMAR") Electric System Planning requirements. The planning process also supports development of the 10-Year System Plan, long-range planning activities, annual operational planning efforts, and coordination with financial, capital, and reliability planning processes.

The planning process supports analysis and planning across approximately **338 substations, 450 substation transformers, and 1,500 feeders** throughout the distribution system. As part of the planning process, BGE develops a **15-year forecast** for feeders, substation transformers, and substations supporting operational planning, long-term distribution planning activities and development of the 10-Year Distribution Capacity Plan.

By considering equipment age, condition, and any constraints (or limiting factors), planning activities also support reliability improvement programs associated with feeder reliability improvements, substation equipment replacement, underground cable replacement and distribution system asset replacement activities. If an asset is identified as needing a capacity upgrade but has a nearer-term need for replacement due to issues identified through inspection and maintenance activities, it is often most cost-effective to upgrade the asset's capacity at the time of the reliability replacement.

BGE's distribution planning process consists of four primary phases, including:

- Development of distribution load forecasts;
- Identification of system constraints;
- Evaluation of mitigation solutions; and
- Development of the prioritized system plan.

## Forecasting Methodology

BGE's forecasting process develops **coincident feeder, substation transformer, and substation level forecasts using weather-normalized hourly load profiles** derived from supervisory control and data acquisition ("SCADA") metered data, advanced metering infrastructure ("AMI") data, and historical weather data. Forecasting incorporates known customer load additions, economic development projects, DER interconnection information, planned load transfers, planned capital projects, electrification impacts, customer growth projections, planned system changes, and distributed generation impacts associated with photovoltaic ("PV") solar generation, and energy storage adoption.

The forecasting process includes normalization of observed system loading to weather conditions using historical localized weather data and seasonal loading behavior. Weather normalization methodologies incorporate approximately **thirty years of historical weather data from the National Weather Service BWI weather station**, including dry bulb temperature, humidity, and windspeed. The normalization produces a **synthetic year of hourly loads analogous to a typical meteorological year and represents the 90th percentile from the simulated distribution.** This forms the base profile on which additional elements of the forecast are added to, or subtracted from, to derive the expected hourly forecast by year.

In addition to weather normalization, BGE builds forecasts using **econometric regression and exponential smoothing models by customer class.** This process uses **top-down zonal and system modelling that is calibrated to revenue and rate classes.** Inputs to the econometric models include historical data from the year 2000 to 2025 for sales, customers, demand, economics, prices, energy efficiency, and solar. Inputs are used to estimate historical relationships between electricity consumption and its drivers. Sales are weather normalized using heating degree days ("HDD") and temperature humidity index degree days ("TDD") spline models with lag and seasonality effects.

Results from the 2025 to 2030 forecast show sales (MWh) and zonal peak (MW) forecasts grow about **1%.** This growth is mostly driven by data centers and other large loads, as well as EV adoption and customer growth that is partially offset by energy efficiency. Customer forecast shows about **0.4% growth**, which is primarily driven by employment and household projections.

## Recent and Planned Forecasting Enhancements

Historically, BGE utilized an **internally developed forecasting application based primarily on regression analysis of observed peak feeder and transformer loading** to develop baseline summer and winter peak forecasts. Prior to implementation of the LoadSEER platform, the legacy forecasting application primarily supported peak demand forecasting and incorporated known load transfers and approved new business load additions, but had limited capability to model hourly load behavior, DER impacts, or spatial allocation of forecasted growth.

Beginning with the 2026 planning cycle, BGE is implementing enhancements through the **Distributed Energy Resources and Load Forecasting ("DER & LF") System.** The DER & LF System will support expanded forecasting capabilities associated with increasing electrification, DER adoption, and hourly load analysis. Within the forecasting process, corporate forecast inputs are refined using known customer load additions and interconnection information, and the DER & LF system is used to spatially allocate forecasted load, DER growth, and managed and unmanaged EV charging impacts across the distribution system. The DER and LF system capabilities include **8760 hourly forecasting, spatial allocation of EV, PV, and load growth projections, and overlay of DER generation and EV charging profiles onto baseline hourly feeder load profiles** to evaluate impacts on feeder and transformer loading.

## Non-Wires Solutions Evaluation

BGE's distribution planning process includes evaluation of NWS for certain capacity-driven projects. Capacity-related projects above approximately **$500k** undergo NWS assessment activities as part of the project evaluation process. To date, BGE's NWS evaluations have primarily focused on BESS as potential alternatives to traditional infrastructure solutions. Existing evaluations compare the cost and operational impacts of BESS solutions against traditional distribution infrastructure investments. Current BESS evaluations primarily assess utility-owned and operated BESS configurations utilizing standardized **1 MW / 2 MWh and 1 MW / 4 MWh** deployment units.

NWS evaluations utilize feeder and station load profile analysis to determine the magnitude and timing of required load relief on constrained portions of the distribution system. NWS assessments evaluate **hourly feeder and station loading profiles, seasonal peak loading conditions, required MW and MWh load relief, overload duration, and recharge capability requirements** associated with constrained facilities. NWS evaluations also assess operational feasibility associated with charging/discharging duration, feeder load shape impacts, seasonal loading variability, and ability to defer or mitigate forecasted feeder and transformer overload conditions.

### Reliability and Capacity Planning Coordination

BGE utilizes a coordinated approach to align Reliability Planning and Capacity Planning functions. While each group has distinct objectives, both teams work on the same electric distribution system and coordinate to deliver cost-effective, high-performing solutions. The overarching goal is to proactively identify overlapping system needs and develop projects that improve reliability and meet current and future capacity demands.

The **Capacity Planning team** focuses on ensuring the system can safely and efficiently serve current load and accommodate future growth. The team leverages system load forecasts, growth projections, and equipment ratings to execute larger projects with a longer timeframe to complete. **Reliability Planning** focuses on minimizing outage frequency and duration and improving the overall customer experience. Together, these teams aim to deliver least-cost investments that maximize long-term value and customer benefit from a reliability and capacity planning perspective.

To achieve improved outcomes, the teams coordinate reliability and capacity work to identify opportunities to avoid redundant or sequential investments and maximize the value of the system investment by looking at the issue comprehensively. Early collaboration between teams ensures that both reliability risks and capacity constraints are considered for solutioning where overlap exists. A coordinated approach results in more efficient project execution, reduced costs, and in general improved system performance.

Collaboration between planning groups is typically triggered when overlapping system needs are identified. Common triggers include new development and load growth, electrification initiatives such as EV infrastructure, DER and battery storage interconnections, aging or underperforming infrastructure, and specific customer reliability concerns. Anytime projects add load to the system, opportunities for reliability improvement may exist. Major system upgrades, such as voltage conversions, also require coordinated evaluation, work planning, and early collaboration to achieve optimal results.

When reliability and capacity triggers are found to overlap, teams will coordinate on the solutioning. Depending on the trigger, the appropriate team assesses broader system conditions and engages the overall team to evaluate potential solutions. Alternatives are developed and compared based on cost, performance, and long-term value. Once a preferred solution is identified, the project moves through the approval and prioritization process, execution, and then performance tracking, with continuous feedback included in future planning cycles.

Projects are reviewed through a structured governance Project Authorization Process to ensure technical soundness and financial accountability. This review process also serves as another point of collaboration. The **Asset Investment Committee ("AIC")** evaluates technical solutions and alternatives, while the **Project Review Committee ("PRC")** validates financial assumptions and funding. Planning and Operating teams are standing members of these committees and involved in evaluations and approvals. These checkpoints reinforce cross-functional alignment and ensure that selected projects represent the most effective use of resources.

Reliability Planning and Capacity Planning, while separate teams, recognize the importance of a coordinated approach. By seeking opportunities to align early in the planning process, by sharing data, and by developing coordinated solutions where opportunities arise, BGE enhances system reliability, reduces cost, and delivers greater value to customers and stakeholders.

# Section 2: Relevant Planning Criteria

> COMAR 20.50.15.04.B(1)(b): Relevant planning criteria utilized to identify system constraints.

BGE's planning criteria establish the technical and operational thresholds used to design and assess its distribution system and identify system constraints. This is achieved through the evaluation of the Company's load forecast (see Section 1, above) against the criteria and the development of solutions for the identified constraints both in the long and short term. The following planning criteria are therefore used to guide system assessment and identify the constraints that inform the long-term system plan.

## Voltage Planning Criteria

BGE's distribution grid consists of approximately **12,300 circuit miles** of distribution lines that operate at either 34.5kV, 13.2kV, or 4.16kV. The 34.5kV and 13.2kV distribution lines serve the majority of BGE's customers. The Company also operates a 34.5kV sub-transmission system that forms a critical backbone of its delivery network. The Company aims to maintain nominal voltage levels across the system according to ANSI C84.1 American National Standard for Electric Power Systems and Equipment – Voltage Ratings (60 Hertz). The standard operating range runs from **0.95 to 1.05 per unit voltage.**

## Thermal Planning Criteria

BGE utilizes seasonal equipment ratings and load forecast data to perform a thermal capacity[^14] assessment of each major component of its distribution and sub-transmission systems. Each major component of the distribution system is assigned an established **normal rating** based on its operating characteristics, which takes into account daily load cycles and seasonal ambient temperature. The normal rating represents the maximum permitted loading when the system is operating under normal operating conditions. Loading at this level will result in minimal, if any, thermal-driven equipment degradation over the expected life of the equipment. BGE compares its load forecast to the normal ratings of its feeders and substation transformers to identify any potential overload. **Any feeder or substation transformer which is at or above 100% of either its summer or winter normal rating is flagged for further analysis to develop remediation.**

BGE also establishes **emergency ratings** for major components of its distribution system. The emergency rating represents the maximum permitted loading to be used for a 24-hour time interval without exceeding established limits for thermal aging, conductor clearance, or equipment damage. Loading more than the normal rating is to be used infrequently and only during contingency events such as the unplanned outage of another system component. The emergency rating is based on equipment loading prior to contingency and only intended for use during a limited number of times throughout the life of the equipment. Emergency ratings take into consideration the duration and frequency of the emergency conditions to limit the loss of equipment life to an acceptable level.

[^14]: Thermal capacity is the maximum amount of current that a piece of equipment, such as a conductor, can continuously carry without exceeding its temperature rating. It represents the safe, sustained current flow, preventing insulation failure due to overheating, and is determined by material, size, and ambient temperature. Exceeding system thermal limits adversely affects the lifespan of distribution equipment and can cause immediate equipment failure.

## Contingency Planning Philosophy

For feeders, BGE's contingency planning approach leverages emergency ratings to account for and withstand **N-1 contingency events**. BGE's preferred feeder design strategy sectionalizes a feeder into three equally loaded segments by strategically placed automated switches. During a contingency event on a feeder, this configuration relies on a neighboring feeder's emergency rating to accommodate one segment of its load, even if the neighboring feeder is loaded to its normal limit. **Current practice does not include system-wide assessments of this design practice; rather, analyses are carried out on a case-by-case basis depending on specific project or system conditions.**

Substation transformer contingency planning is segmented into three main categories: **Firm, Semi-Firm, and Non-Firm.**

- **Firm** substations can withstand the full N-1 loss of the largest substation transformer, meaning that in the event the largest substation transformer is no longer operational, other transformer(s) fully maintain service to all customers. As part of its load forecasting process, BGE conducts an analysis on Firm substations to ensure that they are able to operate as intended during N-1 contingency scenarios under future load conditions. Today, approximately **50% of the substations are classified as Firm.** It is BGE's long-term goal that any newly constructed substations will be Firm.
- **Semi-Firm** substations include a protection mechanism that restricts the load transfer between transformers if an overload would be created. Only **13 substations** on the BGE system are classified as Semi-Firm.
- **Non-Firm** substations lack either a transfer scheme or sufficient capacity to ensure full transfer of load during the loss of the largest substation transformer or are a single transformer substation. These represent the remainder of substations on the system.

## Current Imbalance Philosophy

During normal operation of the system, current on the three phases of the system should be balanced (i.e., the amount of current flowing through any of the three phases should be roughly equal in magnitude). The reality of the distribution system is that these currents are rarely, if ever, perfectly balanced. From the operational planning perspective, BGE allows acceptable current imbalances on the system limited to a **target maximum of 10% between any two phases.** Imbalanced feeders are flagged and investigated on a semi-annual basis to determine if the imbalance was a temporary occurrence while the system was not under normal operating conditions, or if it persisted under normal operating conditions and could be effectively remediated.

## Power Factor (VAR) Philosophy

Power factor describes the relationship between the total electricity supplied by the distribution system and the portion used to perform useful work. When power factor is below unity (i.e., zero reactive power), additional current is required to serve the same customer demand, which can increase system losses and reduce available capacity on utility equipment. As such, maintaining acceptable power factor levels supports efficient operation of the distribution system. BGE's power factor target for its distribution system is **unity (i.e. 1)** on the high-voltage side of its substation transformers. This is achieved through the installation of capacitors[^15] on the distribution lines to ensure a more desirable power factor on each feeder.

[^15]: Capacitors improve power factor by supplying reactive power locally, generally reducing system current, losses, and loading on utility equipment.

## Maryland Cost Allocation Method

The Maryland Cost Allocation Method ("MCAM") proposed an alternative strategy for funding system upgrades necessary to interconnect DER capacity. Under the current "causer pays" method, customers interconnecting large DERs that cause the need for system upgrades would pay the full cost of the upgrade in order to connect their system, even if the upgrade created more hosting capacity than the customer would use. Under MCAM, utilities would change to a "beneficiary pays" method where interconnecting customers pay a portion of the upgrade cost based on the amount of hosting capacity the interconnection will use.

In December 2025, Maryland electric utilities filed revised tariff proposals for MCAM to determine cost-sharing methods for DER customers interconnecting on the secondary less than 600 volts (MCAM-S) or primary voltage more than 600 volts (MCAM-P). Only the MCAM-S tariff proposal was approved by the Commission to proceed forward with an effective date of May 1, 2026. The Commission directed the Interconnection Work Group to review specific cost adjustments for the MCAM-P proposal and to refile the tariff for review. MCAM-P will be implemented at a later date. After Commission approval of the tariff, any Interconnection Applications submitted on or after May 1, 2026, BGE's tariff requires an application, admin, and hosting capacity flat fee which will be adjusted annually with the caveat that any upgrades that are non-shared, would be at 100% customer cost per FERC 2222.

## System Peak Shaving

System peak shaving refers to actions that reduce electricity consumption during the highest system-wide demand periods. Peak shaving reduces the maximum load seen by the grid and can be achieved through demand-side adjustments or by supplying energy from local sources such as BESS, rooftop solar, or onsite generation. It can provide grid stability during critical periods by reducing consumption peaks that can strain grid infrastructure and risk outages, thereby improving bulk system reliability. BGE's DRIVE pilot proposes to assess the impact that peak shaving will have on the grid. The lessons learned during the duration of the pilot will allow BGE to determine the overall peak-reducing impact of VPPs on its system and evaluate the potential of integrating VPPs as a consideration when alleviating system constraints.

### DRIVE Act Implementation Pilot

The DRIVE Act pilot is BGE's proposal for certain time-of-use rates, a VPP and V2G pilot, which was submitted to the Commission in accordance with the Maryland DRIVE Act[^21] on July 1, 2025[^22] and updated in accordance with Order No. 91917[^23] on January 20, 2026.[^24] The Maryland General Assembly and the Commission have made clear that the State's next phase of grid transformation must unlock the full potential of VPPs, bidirectional EV integration and customer-sited technologies deployed at meaningful scale. BGE's significantly expanded, portfolio-based pilot is purpose-built to align to the Commission's **2% peak-reduction target** while carefully balancing program costs, ratepayer impacts and long-term grid, customer and community value.

BGE's proposed Maryland DRIVE Act VPP pilot program integrates DERs to deliver electric distribution system support services ("EDSSS"). The pilot will provide defined participation pathways for all customer classes, enabling direct and indirect compensation for their grid services (EDSSS). It is designed to include and orchestrate residential behind-the-meter energy storage devices, residential bidirectional-enabled EVs, residential Heating, Ventilation, and Air Conditioning ("HVAC") loads, and residential and commercial demand response ("DR") capabilities to deliver measurable grid benefits, whether customers participate directly with BGE or through a licensed Aggregator.

The VPP pilot is structured around four programs through which customers may participate:

1. **Bring-Your-Own-Device ("BYOD") Program**: Targets existing, customer-owned, residential, BTM, dispatchable DERs as well as incremental new customer-owned DERs deployed in the BGE service territory by customers. These include stationary energy storage devices and bidirectional-enabled EVs coupled with requisite bidirectional EV supply equipment ("EVSE"). BGE will also focus on customers that have paired those DERs with rooftop, BTM solar PV systems to account for the DRIVE Act's language related to "Renewable On-Site Generating Systems."
2. **Utility-Owned Direct Load Control ("DLC")**: Utility-owned devices play a critical role in maintaining a diversified and reliable portfolio of load control resources. BGE plans to primarily focus on upgrading a portion of its existing fleet of aging one-way direct load control switches to two-way DLC ("2WDLC") switches as they provide increased transparency into device health and operability, increased granularity in event reporting, and improved reliability of HVAC system control. By utilizing this established customer base, significant improvements can be made quickly and easily during the Pilot term. BGE will also consider opportunities for new 2WDLC installations for customers, rather than replacements, to address a broader population for equitable access to the VPP.
3. **Medical BESS**: In an effort to address a critical need for equitable access to direct VPP benefits, BGE also proposed a medical needs battery energy storage system ("Medical BESS") program for customers with medical needs who are dependent on an uninterrupted supply of electricity. The program will provide BGE-owned batteries at no cost to customer-site hosts to serve medical needs customers at those locations. Customers would gain the benefits of resilience provided by the BESS during outages, and BGE's system would benefit from the feeder-level resource deployed and managed as part of the VPP.
4. **Aggregator Participation**: Pay-for-performance VPP structure that integrates Distributed Energy Resource Aggregators ("DERA") into BGE's grid management strategy and is intended for both residential and commercial and industrial customers. Aggregator participation targets of **135 MW** are derived from existing DERA participation in PJM markets.

### Conservation Voltage Reduction ("CVR") Program

CVR saves energy by lowering the voltages provided to customers within an acceptable voltage bandwidth, resulting in a reduction in energy demand and associated reductions in GHG emissions (PUA §7-802, (1)). This is possible because the voltage provided for normal 120-V service can be between 114 V and 126 V. Some loads like incandescent and compact fluorescent lamp ("CFL") lighting, refrigerators, heat pumps, and AC units run more efficiently at lower voltages, which results in energy savings to the grid and the consumer.

BGE uses switched capacitor banks and single-phase operation to lower service voltage dynamically at approximately **1,060 circuit feeders across 165 substations.** BGE's assets with CVR installed have a combined **summer peak load of 5,800 MW and a winter peak of 5,000 MW**, representing approximately 90% of the peak load for the BGE service territory, meaning CVR deployment is nearly territory-wide.

BGE CVR implementation reached saturation of available feeders for CVR implementation at the end of 2024, and no further opportunities for implementation have been identified on the BGE distribution system. However, on an annual basis, the exact number of feeders with CVR applied will vary due to system reconfigurations. BGE forecasts its CVR Program will reduce Lifecycle CO2e from 2024-2036 by 2.5M (metric tons).

## CSNA Goal (6)

### Smart Charge Management Program

BGE is meeting this goal of PUA §7-802, (6) - the efficient management of load variability - through its Smart Charge Management program described below, as well as demand response programs (see also EmPOWER Maryland Demand Response program described under CSNA Goal (3)).[^34]

To help minimize the impacts of EV charging, BGE implemented the Smart Charge Management ("SCM") pilot which ran from 2021 to 2024. Using virtual distribution assets, BGE was able to simulate how to manage residential EV charging load in one area and the potential impacts on a physical feeder. Charging was managed through prioritizing which EVs needed charged and when. Charging prioritization was based on the battery state-of-charge, which prioritized customers whose battery levels were below 20%, and declared departure time, which prioritized customers who required their vehicle early the next day. If an EV charging site did not meet prioritization requirements, the device could be overridden.

The results of the pilot showed a conservative estimate of $300 in value for each participating EV to the Distribution system and only cost $120 in bill credits a year per device enrolled to the customer. The program was officially adopted in 2024, with permission to scale up to 30,000 participants by the end of 2027. The program also expanded to allow more EVs manufacturers and charging devices, as well as increasing flexibility by removing auto-unenrollment after a certain number of overrides and changed incentives to be awarded per participating device rather than per account.

### Bidirectional Power Flows

Pursuant to PUA §7-802, (6) BGE has implemented bidirectional power flows on the majority of its system.

## E. Load and Demand Management

Short-term forecast accuracy is a means to quantify the accuracy of the feeder and substation transformer forecasts for one year in the future. The calculation identifies all feeders or substation transformers that are forecasted to be at 90% or more of their rating, or the weather normalized observed loads are at 90% or more of asset rating where the difference between the two values is +/-10%. The number of components that meet the criteria are then divided by the total amount of feeders and transformers to arrive at a percentage of highly loaded components that vary between the forecasted load and the observed load. **BGE's most recent short term forecasting accuracy metric shows deviation on 2.2% of impacted components.**

## H. NWS Incorporation

To date, two NWS have been successfully identified as being viable and cost effective to alleviate a constraint on BGE's system:

BGE's first non-wires solutions were the **Chesapeake and Fairhaven BESS**[^41] projects, which were developed in accordance with the Maryland Battery Storage Pilot Program.[^42] These projects mitigate a system constraint identified on a 34kV feeder in southern Anne Arundel County that could experience a **winter post-contingency overload of up to 3.5MW.** Specifically, if BGE were to lose two of its Marriott Hill sub-transmission circuits, both on a single right-of-way and shared pole line, customer load at three BGE substations would be transferred to an alternate Marriott Hill circuit. At peak load, this would result in the Marriott Hill alternate circuit exceeding its emergency rating. In February 2015, this contingency resulted in rotating customer load shed. To address this overload, BGE considered possibly undergrounding approximately ten miles of one of the Marriott Hill circuits to separate the 34kV circuits on the shared pole line. The following NWS BESS projects were chosen instead to mitigate the system constraint and went into service in 2023 and 2024 respectively:

- The Chesapeake BESS – 1MW/2MWh project owned and operated by Ameresco, Inc.
- The Fairhaven BESS – 2.5MW/9.74MWh project is owned and operated by BGE.

Both BESS projects also participate in PJM markets and all revenues to BGE are used to defray customer rates.

Further, under the **BGE DBESS Program** the Company plans to deploy 3MW of energy storage to meet predicted overloads by 2027 on two feeders associated with the **Hereford Substation** which were included in the Company's last Multi-Year Rate Plan ("MYP"). The Hereford Substation BESS will provide for deferral of construction of a new transmission-supplied 34kV low side substation, almost 5 miles of new 34kV feeders, a new 34-13kV substation, and several miles of new 13 kV feeder. In this instance, the unique combination of a system constraint along with a community solar project create the perfect opportunity for a BESS to provide capacity relief. The DBESS will function as non-wires solutions performing equipment-level peak shaving as an electric distribution grid support services ("EDSSS") and participate in PJM markets to offset costs.

The DBESS Program is planned to be leveraged for **22 other constraints** within the 10-year plan. These are currently conceptual solutions that are being further investigated and scoped.

# Section 7: Waiver of COMAR Metrics for good cause

BGE respectfully requests a waiver for good cause pursuant to COMAR 20.50.15.01E and Commission Order No. 92317 for any metric required by COMAR 20.50.15.05C that is not included in this report. These metrics remain under discussion and development within the DSPWG. Additionally, BGE requests waivers for good cause of the following metrics that weren't included in the updated short list or in the COMAR 20.50.15.05C metrics which are also still being discussed and evaluated in the DSPWG including:

### A. State Goals & Targets

**a.) Metric 1.1** — State-goal progress rollup (Multi-Criterion Decision Evaluation-mapping projects to §7-802 goals)

> Good Cause: Good cause exists for a waiver of this metric because a methodology for calculating and measuring progress toward CSNA goals has not yet been defined.

**b.) Metric 1.2** — Equity: Percentage of ESP investments in vulnerable communities

> Good Cause: Good cause exists for a waiver of this metric because a methodology has not been developed to determine how to map electric system infrastructure such as feeders and substations to overburdened and underserved communities. Feeders are generally linear and may traverse multiple census tracts with differing overburdened or underserved designations. Similarly, substations serve broad geographic areas that encompass a mix of census tracts with varying classifications.

### B. Reliability

**a.) Metric 2.1** — SAIDI and SAIFI (by reference to Case No. 9353 Annual Reports)

> Good Cause: Good Cause exists for a waiver of this metric because BGE does not have the necessary equity stratification available and for the same reasons described in Metric 1.2 above, including the absence of a defined methodology for mapping feeders and substations to overburdened and underserved communities.

**b.) Metric 2.2** — Segment reliability metrics by vulnerable/overburdened/underserved communities at the feeder level

> Good Cause: Good cause exists for a waiver of this metric for the same reasons described in Metric 1.2 above, including the absence of a defined methodology for mapping feeders and substations to designated vulnerable, overburdened, or underserved communities.

### C. Load and Demand Management

**a.) Metric 5.1** — Geo-targeted energy efficiency/demand-side management (EE/DSM) progress (by reference to Case No. 9705)

> Good Cause: Good cause exists for a waiver of this metric because the methodology required to perform the calculation has not been defined, and for the same reasons identified in Metric 1.2 regarding the lack of a framework for mapping infrastructure to overburdened and underserved communities.

BGE remains committed to working collaboratively with stakeholders through the ESP Work Group to develop and refine metrics that comply with COMAR 20.50.15.05C.
