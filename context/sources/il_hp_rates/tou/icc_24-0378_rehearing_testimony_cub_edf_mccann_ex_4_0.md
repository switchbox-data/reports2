## 1 INTRODUCTION

**Q.** Please state your name, business address, and current position.

**A.** Richard J. McCann, M.Cubed, 426 12th Street, Davis, California. My current position is Partner with M.Cubed.

**Q.** Are you the same Richard J. McCann who testified earlier in this proceeding?

**A.** Yes.[^1]

### 1.1 Purpose of This Testimony

**Q.** What is the purpose of your testimony?

**A.** I am testifying on Commonwealth Edison's (ComEd) proposed supply-based time-of-use (STOU) rate tariff. On behalf of the Citizens Utility Board and Environmental Defense Fund, I address how ComEd's proposal to include a per-kilowatt capacity charge and transmission charge does not follow cost causation and is unlikely to induce the desired customer response to reduce aggregate peak load. ComEd has shown that the Company itself is incapable [source says "has not shown that the Company itself is incapable"] of predicting when peak demand hours are occurring—its record reveals that its capacity alerts predict the days that determine these charges less accurately than would occur by random chance. It is entirely unreasonable to expect a typical residential or small commercial customer to predict the year's peak hours when even ComEd has repeatedly failed to see them coming. Instead ComEd should be ordered to implement a fully volumetric STOU energy-only rate that will cover all of the days in which peak demand might happen. I present an illustrative rate design consistent with those offered by other utilities.

**Q.** How is your testimony organized?

**A.** I first identify the issues in the utility's testimony that we are contending. I explain why a fixed-price TOU rate is preferable to the current real time price (RTP) rate now offered to customers. I then discuss why ComEd has not provided sufficient evidence to support its assertion that the Monthly Capacity Charge and Transmission and Miscellaneous Procurement Charge (TMC) is a necessary addition to the BEST rate tariff. I point out a series of implementation issues apparently overlooked by ComEd. Next, I explain how low-income customers will benefit from a TOU rate. I then present alternative illustrative rates. I conclude with my recommendations.

### 1.2 Summary of Recommendations

**Q.** What are your recommendations in this proceeding based on the analysis presented in your testimony?

**A.** I discuss the following recommendations in my testimony:

1. The Commission should authorize ComEd to offer an opt-in supply time-of-use (STOU) rate to residential and small commercial customers as a more effective alternative to the current real time price (RTP) rate.
2. The STOU rate should be a fully volumetric TOU energy charge.
3. ComEd's proposal to include a Monthly Capacity Charge and a Monthly TMC should be rejected for the many reasons I enumerate here.
4. I propose an illustrative set of rates that will achieve consistent customer response and align with the practices of other utilities that offer TOU rates to residential customers.

## 2 Summary of Commonwealth Edison's Proposals Addressed in this Testimony

**Q.** Which of the utilities' proposals are you addressing in your testimony?

**A.** Commonwealth Edison is proposing to implement a new STOU rate for supply service named Rate BEST using a four-period pricing algorithm.[^2] In addition to the four-period volumetric prices, ComEd proposes to add demand charges called the Monthly Capacity Charge, corresponding to the customer's usage during the previous year's five peak total usage hours in the PJM Interconnection systems,[^3] and a similarly per-kilowatt Transmission and Miscellaneous Procurement Charge (TMC) based on the five PJM system peak and five ComEd system peak hours.[^4] I contend that the more effective alternative is to follow the best practices of other utilities around the country and offer a volumetric energy-only TOU rate that fully incorporates the capacity cost, as ComEd already did with success in its recent four-year supply TOU rate pilot study (RTOUPP).

## 3 The Supply TOU Rate Is Preferable to the Current Real Time Price Rate Offered by ComEd

**Q.** Should Commonwealth Edison offer a supply TOU rate as an alternative to Rate BESH?

**A.** Yes. The fact is that Commonwealth Edison already offers a time-variant supply rate Real Time Pricing (RTP, or Rate BESH), a peak demand charge to non-residential customers based on time based Maximum Kilowatts Delivered (MKD),[^5] its Peak Time Savings program,[^6] and, until just last year, RTOUPP.[^7] The Company also will offer a distribution TOU (DTOU) rate as authorized by the Commission. Commonwealth Edison is offering these as both the default load serving entity and the distribution utility. Providing a supply rate that uses the same time period segmentation is an intuitive and sensible step.

**Q.** Why should Commonwealth Edison offer a supply TOU rate?

**A.** Given that the utility is arguing that matching supply and distribution time variant rates is required for customers to have simpler "customer friendly" choices, the lack of such a proposal is surprising and inconsistent with the utility's reasoning.[^8] As I pointed out previously, having one portion of a customer's bill determined on a real time hourly basis and another on a consistent time block schedule basis complicates a customer's decision rather than simplifies it. Often the real time prices in the PJM wholesale market diverge from the preset distribution TOU prices. For example, higher solar power production in the early afternoon in the spring can push PJM prices below nighttime prices while the distribution TOU price would be higher at the same time. This means that customers would have to track two separate, and at times conflicting, price signal streams. These confusing signals are likely to blunt the intended consumer response. A single unified overall price, such as one delivered through a combined TOU rate, avoids that problem. The differences in customer acceptance of RTP versus TOU rate also is readily apparent when looking at data for other utilities and states. Illinois is the only state with residential RTP; in contrast, out of 829 utilities reporting, 347 or 42% offer residential TOU rates.[^9] Only 1.1% of ComEd's residential customers have enrolled in the RTP tariff since its inception in 2007,[^10] while the average TOU tariff offered by U.S. utilities enrolled 3.4% of a utility's residential customers in 2019.[^11]

Further, at least two states have already adopted default residential TOU rates for investor-owned utilities (Missouri[^12] and California[^13]). California's rooftop solar customers have been required to be on TOU rates since 2017.[^14] Continuation of pilot programs that study TOU rates has been popular. In California, more than 90% of the 500,000 customers in each of the three utilities' pilots elected to remain in the TOU rate.[^15] Similarly, the TOU pilot study conducted by the Sacramento Municipal Utility District was extremely popular, and 98% of its customers have remained on the default TOU rate since 2018.[^16]

In regards to relative effectiveness, a 2022 study found that a standard TOU rate can accomplish 60% to 70% of the load shifting effects of an RTP, and the gap can be closed with the addition of either critical peak pricing[^17] or peak time rebates[^18] that are invoked a dozen times each year.[^19] ComEd offers a peak time rebate program, Peak Time Savings.[^20] With higher enrollment rates and little resistance to being implemented on an opt-out default basis compared to an RTP rate, a TOU rate has a much higher potential overall impact on reducing ComEd's peak loads.

### 3.1 Commonwealth Edison's Studies Show that TOU Rates Deliver Greater Bill Savings to Customers and Peak Load Reductions to the System than RTP Rates

**Q.** Please compare the studies that Commonwealth Edison prepared on its residential RTP program and TOU pilot program.

**A.** The utility recently published a study on these two programs. The first is on the RTP program which began operation in 1998.[^21] The second is on the residential TOU pilot (RTOUPP) started in 2020 and ending this past June.[^22] Although both tariffs vary only the supply portion of ComEd's bundled rate, the bill savings are not directly comparable because the rates are computed differently. Nonetheless, the peak load changes are informative about the relative effectiveness of each rate schedule.

The RTP rate has about 41,200 enrolled participants, and the study covered 38,500. The average peak load reduction was 0.013 kilowatts during the 11 am to 6 pm "peak" period.[^23] It took three day-ahead alerts to achieve an average reduction of 0.057 kilowatts. About 99% of participants achieved bill savings.

The RTOUPP averaged about 1,800 participants, which was roughly full enrollment compared to the Commission-mandated enrollment cap. Overall, Super Peak Period loads decreased 0.10 kilowatts or 6.7% while loads increased in Peak and Off Peak Periods as customers shifted usage.[^24] The peak reduction for the 1,250 customers who remained in the pilot from 2020 to 2023 was 3.4% from the base year. In contrast, the control group on the standard Basic Electric Service (BES) rate increased their peak load contribution by 7.0%, so the net total reduction was close to 10.4%.[^25] That translates to an average of 0.26 kilowatts per participant. More than 98% of customers had bill savings.[^26]

Customers in the TOU pilot appear to have reduced peak loads about eight times more than those on the RTP tariff and 75% more even for the most responsive subset. Differences in how the programs are administered could account for much of these changes, but given they are opt-in offerings, both programs likely have similar self-selection biases in recruiting those who are amenable to time variant rates. Given these outcomes, the TOU rate seems to offer at least the same level of load reduction benefits with much simpler rate structure for customers to follow.

## 4 ComEd Has Not Presented Sufficient Evidence Demonstrating that Demand Charges Are More Effective than Volumetric TOU Rates

**Q.** What is ComEd's stated purpose for proposing its Monthly Capacity Charge?

**A.** ComEd intends this charge to reflect the annual transmission charge imposed by PJM Interconnection (PJM), the independent system operator that serves ComEd.[^27] It would calculate the charge based on the customer's Peak Load Contribution (PLC) from the previous June to May period and incorporate that as a monthly fixed charge. The PLC calculation uses the five highest peak hours through the summer from June to August.[^28] Similarly, ComEd uses the same rationale for justifying the TMC.

**Q.** Does ComEd's rationale conform with its stated intent of providing incentives for customers to reduce their peak usage?

**A.** No. Kremer states, "[t]hough it is not known in advance which specific day or time will be the PJM or ComEd system peaks, we know that they are generally on the hottest days of the year, during the summer months, and between 3 p.m. to 5 p.m."[^29] In other words, a demand-based capacity charge requires customers to gamble all summer, every day, whether their usage will contribute to their Monthly Capacity Charge and TMC the following year.

Kremer argues that a demand-based charge sends a price signal to individual customers to reduce their individual PLC.[^30] This would be a strong argument if there were reason to believe Rate BEST customers will know which hours will count toward their PLC, with enough advance notice to adjust accordingly. Unfortunately, as Kremer herself admits, customers do not know this information in advance and, in fact, would not know until the following year, or at least until the Summer period is over. At this point, it is too late to do anything about their past usage. Kremer's analysis is more supportive of an energy-based critical peak period rate or a peak time rebate that is invoked a dozen times a year on a day ahead basis.[^31] The advice Kremer offers in testimony for predicting system peak hours does not evoke great confidence: "they are generally on the hottest days of the year, during the summer months, and between 3 p.m. and 5 p.m."[^32] Kremer prescribes that a customer "should see their individual Capacity Charge decrease the following year" if they "reliably reduc[e] the Mid-day Peak Period load" for 4 months.[^33] This is no more instructive than simply telling a customer to avoid Mid-Day Peak usage in the Summer, which is already communicated far more clearly by simply including capacity costs in the Mid-Day Peak volumetric rate in the Summer. If a customer fails to predict these peak hours, ComEd's proposal would irreversibly punish them with a hefty locked-in monthly fixed charge for a full year. All the customer can do to respond to this price signal is to leave the program for 12 months and hope they get luckier next year.

**Q.** Has ComEd been successful and accurate at predicting its system peak times for Real Time Pricing customers?

**A.** Absolutely not. ComEd's record of aligning capacity alerts for BESH customers with PJM or ComEd system peaks is abysmal—worse than random chance. ComEd has attempted for years to predict system peak hours and warn Real Time Pricing customers in advance, to little avail. In 2024, Kremer identifies the five days and hours with the highest PJM peaks[^34] and the five with the highest ComEd peaks,[^35] of which only one day and one hour overlapped. ComEd issued 33 capacity alerts in 2024, running from January to the end of September—eight months in total.[^36] ComEd successfully predicted any of the nine peak days, let alone hours, ONCE out of the 33 notices sent. That is a 3% success rate. If ComEd had instead sent out a capacity alert for 4 pm to 6 pm every day during those eight months, it would have been successful 3.75% of the days or 25% higher. If those alerts were issued only during the four summer months, the success rate would increase to 7.5% or 250% higher. ComEd would have been more successful guessing than what they achieved. This predictive failure illustrates why a constant time-based energy charge will be at least as effective as the proposed Monthly Capacity Charge and TMC in eliciting the appropriate customer response. Unless ComEd develops an unprecedented ability to forecast hourly system conditions well in advance, customers cannot reasonably be expected to see these demand-based charges as actionable price signals. Instead, I propose to recover the revenues ComEd proposes to allocate to the per-kilowatt Capacity Charge and Transmission Charge to the volumetric per-kilowatt-hour rate corresponding to the time period (Summer Mid-Day Peak) that captures every relevant system peak hour and does not require customers to monitor usage based on ComEd alerts that have only a 3% success rate.

### 4.1 The Monthly Capacity Charge and TMC Are Actually New Fixed Charges that Should Instead Be Incorporated into the Volumetric Charge Instead

_Section heading in body text (above) differs slightly from TOC entry 4.1, which omits "and TMC" / uses singular "Charge" / "Fixed Charge"._

**Q.** How does ComEd defend the Monthly Capacity Charge and TMC against alternatives such as a volumetric energy charge?

**A.** Kremer claims in defense of a demand-based Monthly Capacity Charge and TMC that it would "provide direct incentives to customers who reliably respond to pricing signals to avoid the Mid-Day Peak period."[^37] Further, she claims the capacity charge "is not diluted or averaged out in a usage-charge where the customer does not receive the direct reduction in costs for their performance in the prior summer."[^38]

I disagree. As noted above, customers cannot actually predict with sufficient accuracy when they will incur the Capacity Charge to be imposed the following year—they only know that it will likely fall between 3 pm and 5 pm within a three-month period. (Even so, the peak may well fall in September or in hours outside of the 3 pm to 5 pm window, there is no guarantee as to when it will occur.) As a result, customers will have to reduce their usage in those hours anyway. In other words, the price effect is diluted by the logical response of customers to the uncertainty created by ComEd's rate design. ComEd might as well just have a continual higher peak rate inclusive of those hours rather than turning customers into weather forecasters.

By leaving capacity costs out of the Mid-Day Peak period rate, ComEd would be decreasing the incentive for customers to avoid the Mid-Day Peak period and bifurcating the price signal between a usage-based price signal to avoid the Mid-Day Peak period this month to save this month and a demand-based price signal to attempt to avoid the 5 to 10 system peak hours that Kremer admits herself are "not known in advance" in hopes of saving next year.[^39] Including capacity costs in the Mid-Day Peak period rate would provide a stronger, more certain, and more immediate price signal to avoid the Mid-Day Peak period rather than waiting for nearly a year to see the impact on a bill.[^40]

Ironically, while Kremer claims that ComEd is not diluting the capacity cost by rolling it into the volumetric energy charge, ComEd precisely dilutes the capacity charge by taking costs incurred within a three-month period and spreading them over the entire 12 months. In other words, ComEd is reducing the summertime charge by 75% by diluting it with revenue from non-summer usage. Based on ComEd's workpapers, a Monthly Capacity Charge will collect only 40% of revenues during the summer months and 60% in the non-summer months outside of the peak period that Kremer claims is so important.[^41] And ComEd uses a cost basis provided PJM as a proxy for the current true economic capacity cost.[^42] By the time customers see this item on their bills, the basis of the PJM charge will be nearly two years old. Instead of sending an accurate peak period incentive, ComEd is creating a year-round fixed charge on false premises.

Instead, ComEd can just as easily spread the revenues the Company proposes to allocate to the Capacity Charge across the volumetric charge. I discuss that alternative further below in Section 7.

**Q.** Does ComEd's proposed Monthly Capacity Charge and TMC align with true cost causation by each customer?

**A.** No. Kremer confuses the utility's revenue requirements with efficient economic pricing. The revenue requirements are past sunk costs that are recorded by accountants.[^43] This is a retrospective view of financial requirements. Efficient economic prices reflect the opportunity cost for producers to supply generation. Those are based on the prospective costs of new resources needed to meet future demand.[^44] This form of pricing aligns utility planning and operations with customer price responsiveness.

In contrast to efficient pricing, ComEd's proposal would require customers to wait a year to find out whether they happened to avoid usage during the correct 5 to 10 hours to save in the coming year. In other words, ComEd is proposing to create a compulsory lottery for each Rate BEST customer in which they must gamble on whether they have guessed correctly as to when the actual ComEd peak will occur. That wait severs the cost causation from the responsive action.[^45] If their usage from the previous year coincided with peak hours too closely, their high fixed Monthly Capacity Charge locks in for a full year, and the only way to mitigate those losses is to drop out of the program. If the customer is considering re-enrolling the following year, they will have to shift load for a year, try to guess the right hours, and wait to be compensated for their efforts until the following year, if ever. I reiterate that it is far more appealing to customers to simply know what electricity costs each hour of the day and that 1 pm to 7 pm is the time range to avoid use in order to save.

**Q.** Does the fact that PJM bills ComEd a fixed monthly amount for capacity mean that ComEd should recover these costs from customers through a fixed monthly charge?

**A.** No. There is no basis in economic principles that calls for collecting "fixed costs" (too often misidentified) in a fixed charge. Kremer makes this misidentification error in defining capacity costs as fixed costs.[^46]

Using the logic that fixed costs should be collected through fixed charges, then the marketplace would be rife with all sorts of entry, access and connection fees at grocery stores, nail salons and other retail outlets as well as restaurants, car dealers, etc. to cover the costs of ownership and leases, operational overhead and other invariant costs. Simply put, that is not the case. All of those producers and providers price on a per unit basis, because that's how a competitive market works. In those markets, customers have the ability to choose and move among sellers, so the seller is forced to recover costs on a single unit price. Even cell providers, which appear to have monthly fixed charges, are actually charging monthly connection fees that represent the marginal cost of interconnecting to a network. And customers have the option of switching (and many do) to a provider with a lower monthly fee.

ComEd is exploiting its position as the monopoly utility to impose demand charges that cannot be charged in a competitive marketplace. Utility regulation is supposed to mimic the outcome of competitive markets, including pricing patterns. Alfred Kahn wrote, "the single most widely accepted rule for the governance of the regulated industries is regulate them in such a way as to produce the same result as would be produced by effective competition if it were feasible."[^47] Firms in a competitive market cannot price in this manner with a fixed or demand charge—they use volumetric pricing for their commodities, products and services.

That means that fixed cost recovery through a fixed charge must be limited to a customer-dedicated facility which cannot be used by another customer. That would be the service connection, which has a monthly investment recovery cost of about $10 to $15 per month. Everything else should be priced on a volumetric basis as would be in a competitive market.

**Q.** If a demand charge offers little to no benefit to customers, what benefit might it provide to ComEd?

**A.** ComEd would see a financial advantage to itself of this approach over volumetric TOU energy charges used by the vast majority of other utilities. In other words, this demand charge would serve as a backdoor means of imposing a new fixed charge on customers. A study by Hledik (2014) begins by extolling the increased revenue stability for a utility from implementing demand charges.[^48] While revenue stability may be attractive to the utility, it should not override the fundamental intent of TOU rates to change customer behaviors and choices that make the utility system more efficient both financially and in resource use.

### 4.2 Other Utilities and Even ComEd Have Incorporated Capacity Costs into Volumetric TOU Energy Charges Successfully

**Q.** Is Kremer's concern about incorporating capacity costs into a volumetric energy charge valid?

**A.** No. First, it is worth noting that ComEd already recovers capacity costs through a straightforward volumetric charge for Rate BES, the Company's default rate that applies to roughly 99% of its residential supply customers. ComEd is not proposing new demand-based charges for residential customers under Rate BES, nor should it.

Further, Kremer notes that including capacity costs in the Mid-Day Peak per-kWh rate would make this time block rate especially high, potentially dissuading customers from enrolling.[^49] Yet, ComEd's customers, other utilities around the country's customers, and the Commission all have demonstrated comfort with the highest peak per-kWh rate being relatively high compared to the other time block per-kWh rates. For example, Jim Lazar from the Regulatory Assistance Project has recommended a 3-2-1 ratio of peak-midpeak-off peak volumetric prices for years with widespread acceptance.[^50]

Through the successful RTOUPP pilot, ComEd customers showed their willingness to sign up for and ability to navigate a TOU rate with larger ratios between block rates than what ComEd is proposing here for Rate BEST. RTOUPP included over 90% of capacity costs in the Super Peak rate and the rest in the (shoulder) Peak rate.

As discussed previously, participation in RTOUPP was limited to 1,900 customers at a time, but TOU rates with much larger enrollments have succeeded with no separate per-kW charge and even larger ratios between their highest per-kWh rate and their other time blocks' per-kWh rates. The vast majority of 347 utilities offering residential TOU rates do not include a demand or capacity charge, instead rolling all of those costs into the volumetric charges.[^51] This situation clearly shows that this approach is quite feasible and even standard practice. These real-world examples disprove Kremer's claim that including capacity costs in the Mid-Day Peak per-kWh rate would have a chilling effect on enrollment.

The DTOU rate the ICC approved for ComEd in this docket has far larger ratios between the Mid-Day Peak per-kWh rate and the other time block rates than what ComEd is proposing for Rate BEST. ComEd calculated the Mid-Day Peak per-kWh charge for its approved DTOU rate to be 10.712 cents per kWh, which is 167% higher than the Morning rate, 186% higher than the Evening rate, and 259% higher than the Overnight rate. These ratios are already close to the 3-1.5-1 ratio that I am proposing for ease of administration. Yet ComEd's proposed Rate BEST features a Summer Period Mid-Day Peak rate of only 4.787 cents per kWh, which is only 55% greater than the next-highest (Summer Period Evening) time block rate.[^52]

Further, the capacity costs reflected in this rate are unavoidable. The capacity cost increase is a pass through from the tremendous increased capacity clearing price from PJM Interconnection's wholesale auction, which goes into effect this coming June. ComEd customers on any of the Company's supply rate offerings will have to pay these increased capacity costs one way or another. ComEd Hourly Pricing (Rate BESH) customers pay the same Capacity Charge Kremer proposes for Rate BEST. Currently, this Capacity Charge is $0.91048 per kW-month.[^53] ComEd proposes an updated Capacity Charge of $8.37511 per kW-month, an 820% increase.[^54] Nearly all ComEd residential retail supply customers take service on Rate BES. These customers will pay this same increased capacity cost, entirely through the per-kWh supply rate they pay. When deciding whether to enroll in Rate BEST, Rate BES, which fully includes capacity costs, is the Price to Compare. Kremer proposes that customers instead see a deflated Mid-Day Peak rate that does not include capacity costs, making every time block per-kWh rate in Rate BEST lower than the Price to Compare that customers see. It is not reasonable to expect every residential customer considering this option to determine their fixed Capacity Charge amount and their historic kWh usage for a typical billing period, divide their Capacity Charge by their usage (if they know it), and then add that to the per-kWh time block rates to determine and compare their total supply cost per kWh. This mathematics is likely far too challenging for the vast majority of customers. It is far better to simply transparently communicate through the per-kWh rates that customers need to minimize usage between 1 pm and 7 pm to save. Recovering capacity costs via the Mid-Day Peak per-kWh rate also allows customers a far more direct load-shifting incentive by rewarding load-shifting away from peak hours with reduced charges in the same billing period.

**Q.** Has the Commission already addressed this issue about how to allocate capacity costs to volumetric energy charges?

**A.** In the proceeding in which the Commission approved RTOUPP, there was rehearing on how to allocate capacity costs between the summer and non-summer periods. In that case, the Commission rejected the same Capacity Charge mechanism ComEd proposes here for Rate BEST and ordered the Company to instead recover capacity costs through the volumetric rates corresponding to the hours during which system peaks historically had occurred. My proposal follows the methodology the Commission approved for RTOUPP. While all system peaks in 2024 occurred during the Mid-Day Peak hours,[^55] I propose that a portion of those costs be allocated to other months and periods but with the largest portion in the summer Mid-Day period. This will mitigate any rate shock during the summer Mid-Day period, but more importantly, it is likely that a significant portion of the PJM Capacity Charge is covering the capital costs of generation built primarily to serve off peak and around the clock energy needs such as nuclear, wind and solar plants. Customers who heed the price signal from a high Mid-Day Peak block rate and limit usage between 1 pm and 7 pm, especially in the summer, can expect to also reduce their Peak Load Contribution, without the added uncertainty of attempting to guess the exact relevant 5 to 10 hours each year.[^56] I discuss this proposal further below in Section 7.

**Q.** What is a better solution than a demand-charge-based rate structure?

**A.** ComEd should be ordered to prepare a rate structure much more like the one recently adopted for its distribution TOU rate and aligned with its RTOUPP rate.[^57] ComEd's DTOU rate relies solely on a time-dependent energy-based rate. The advantage is that customers know that any usage within a specific period (1 pm to 7 pm) will be charged a higher rate. They can plan their usage to avoid those periods on a daily basis and do not need to guess which hours will determine their fixed monthly charge for the following year.

**Q.** Can you explain the issues with ComEd's proposed Monthly Transmission Charge?

**A.** Yes. The critiques I raise above about the Monthly Capacity Charge apply equally to the proposed TMC and for the same reasons.

## 5 ComEd Has Not Adequately Considered Implementation Issues for Its TOU Proposal

**Q.** What are the potential implementation flaws in the Monthly Capacity Charge and TMC?

**A.** ComEd fails to account for the fact that because the proposed demand-based charges do not account for one summer's usage until the following summer, a customer can avoid paying for their own individual Peak Load Contribution by leaving Rate BEST before the following June. In contrast, my proposal holds every participant accountable for their own usage during peak hours for that month immediately. A customer on Rate BEST that has high usage during the Mid-Day Peak period necessarily will have to pay for that usage, including their full corresponding share of capacity costs, in that billing period. Further, ComEd is imposing the Monthly Capacity Charge and TMC obligations incurred by the previous resident that has been in the BEST rate on a new customer who moves into a premise. ComEd's proposal does not address how to address capacity charges for tenants who may be residing for a short period and even moving annually (e.g. college students). And ComEd's proposal does not explain how it plans to make up for the collection shortfall from the customers on this rate, whether those costs will be subsidized by other customers or will shareholders bear the risks of those customers migrating among rates and abodes? These issues do not arise in a purely volumetric STOU.

**Q.** Does ComEd make reasonable assumptions about how customers can manage their loads in response to this new rate proposal?

**A.** ComEd's proposal makes a fundamental error in assuming that residential customers will be able to make the effort to manage energy use to respond to this type of rate. ComEd assumes that residential customers understand how much electricity each of their appliances use in a short period. In my experience, residential customers do not have this level of understanding,[^58] and ComEd presents no evidence to support its implied supposition.

Customers are left to themselves to discern from a whole house metric how much electricity each individual appliance uses. This makes two unreasonable demands on customers:

1. that customers greatly increase their attention on a daily basis to their electricity consumption, and
2. that customers become analytic experts in quantifying the usage of each appliance.

As it stands now, many (could even be most) customers have difficulty deciphering the intricacies of their bills and understanding why those bills change month to month.[^59] It has been the experience of Staff at CUB that customers routinely struggle with the concepts and math necessary to interpret their bills under Rate BES. Each compounded layer of abstraction and math risks alienating customers. Even the utilities themselves typically hire outside consultants[^60] using complex models and software to determine the usage patterns for individual appliances—tools not available to customers.

**Q.** How should ComEd address true-up of cost recovery when usage differs from projected sales?

**A.** Kremer identifies the issue of aligning revenue recovery and cost occurrence.[^61] To prevent persistent overreliance on the Purchased Energy Adjustment, ComEd should calculate per-kWh charges for each Rate BEST time block based on billing determinants that are adjusted to assume a reasonable level of load-shifting by participants. The Company can use the data it collected from RTOUPP to determine expected load shifting. This adjustment is necessary because the very purpose of Rate BEST is to induce load-shifting from higher-cost hours to lower-cost hours. If ComEd were to assume that Rate BEST customers will not change their load profile in response to enrolling in the program, the Company can expect to consistently overestimate usage during higher-cost hours and underestimate usage during lower-cost hours, leading to a predictable pattern of under-recovery and reliance on the PEA to make up the difference. This arrangement would blunt price signals and reduce cost-causation because the PEA applies equally across time blocks. It is, of course, impossible to perfectly predict future billing determinants, but ComEd should make a reasonable effort to make its margin for error smaller and more evenly distributed between over- and under-collection.[^62]

**Q.** What issues exist with regard to ComEd's proposed Monthly Transmission Charge?

**A.** ComEd's proposed Monthly Transmission Charge has the same implementation challenges as the Monthly Capacity Charge and for the same reasons explained above.

## 6 Low-income Customers Benefit More from TOU Rates

**Q.** Are the benefits to low-income customers to be considered in designing TOU rate programs?

**A.** Yes. In establishing the Multi-Year Integrated Grid Plan requirement, CEJA states the plan shall be designed to "support efforts to bring at least 40% of the benefits of those benefits to Equity Investment Eligible Communities."[^63] ComEd includes its peak load reduction programs, including its current time variant rate offering (Rate RTP) in this plan.[^64] To this end, the new TOU program needs to deploy a rate structure that best achieves this objective.

**Q.** What are the results of studies done on Commonwealth Edison data?

**A.** To make this assessment, Kremer reviews the results from the studies of ComEd's Rate RTP and RTOUPP in her testimony.[^65] One analysis assesses the number of low-income customers who are on the RTP rate who would receive bill savings with the addition of the proposed distribution TOU rate.[^66] That analysis showed that 58% to 76% of those customers would see decreased bills of 0.8% to 4.0%. That is equivalent to $0.17 to $1.40 per month.

The second analysis used the results from the supply-oriented RTOUPP.[^67] That study showed average monthly savings of $9.87 per month for low-income ratepayers. Again, these savings from the supply TOU are a multiple of the RTP savings, indicating that adopting a supply TOU rate schedule may be advantageous over the current RTP rate.

**Q.** Is there another step that should be taken to improve the benefits to low-income customers?

**A.** A study conducted in 2019 using data from 100,000 Commonwealth Edison customers found that imposing a fixed customer charge disproportionately disadvantages low-income customers.[^68] Those customers benefit from the shift of time variant volumetric charges, but the fixed charges are proportionately a large share of their overall bill, so the net effect is adverse to the interests of low income households.

The Low Income Discount Rate in ICC Docket 24-0163 likely will address this concern while preserving the differential price effects necessary for an effective TOU rate.[^69] The discounts would be applied to the total bill, but importantly, are calculated for each customer based on what they would have paid under the default rates. For example, applying the 77% discount for single family home nonheating customers to the distribution and supply charges of a TOU customer would reduce the rate for each time period by 77% of the amount Rate BES customers would pay. Suppose the sum of the default Distribution Facilities Charge and Rate BES volumetric supply rate is 10 cents per kWh under default rates for this customer class. The rate for each TOU period would be 7.7 cents per kWh less than what it would be without the discount. This maintains the same cents per kWh difference between each time period's rates, and by reducing the amount of each rate, increases the ratio between the higher and lower rates. Thus, depending on whether you compare rates by cents or ratios, the incentive to shift load is either the same or greater for low-income customers. Combining a TOU rate with the Low Income Discount Rate ComEd Ordered in Docket 24-0163, or any discount rate designed similarly enhances the pricing effect across periods and increases benefits for low-income customers.

## 7 Proposed Volumetric TOU Rates

**Q.** What type of STOU rate should ComEd implement instead of the Company's proposed Rate BEST?

**A.** The Commission should order ComEd to implement a fully volumetric STOU energy rate. The Capacity, Transmission Service and Miscellaneous Charges should all be rolled up into this charge so that customers can readily see a single price as they do with most other market commodities and services.

**Q.** Is it appropriate to impose the highest peak rate year-round during Mid-Day hours?

**A.** No. Commonwealth Edison has two distinctive load patterns or profiles between the "summer" for June to September and "non-summer" for October to May. Figure CUB-EDF-1 compares the two seasonal load profiles. The most obvious contrast is that the highest average load in the non-summer evening is in the bottom third of summer loads. That would place that load in the lower half of the Peak hours using the feeder peaking data. No hours during the October to May season approach the Summer Mid-Day loads. Charging customers Mid-Day Peak rates during non-summer would not reflect cost causation, and the benefits of load-shifting (and thus the need for a time-variant price signal) are far stronger in the summer than non-summer. For these reasons, the Super Peak period should be confined to the June to September summer season.

**Q.** What principles should that STOU rate use?

**A.** ComEd can design this rate using the data that it has already gathered and many of the design principles it used in developing the Monthly Capacity Charge. The difference is that the costs should be allocated across kilowatt-hours of usage based on these principles:

1. The same seasons and time periods can be used as proposed by ComEd for the volumetric segment.
2. Because the Overnight hour prices from PJM are approximately the same across both seasons, those rates should be consistent year round. Similarly, the PJM prices for the Evening and Morning for both seasons should be same. In addition, the Non Summer Mid-Day rate should be the same as the Evening and Morning rates. All PJM prices are within a range of reasonable uncertainty and a significant number overlap among those periods.
3. The ratio of Summer Mid-Day / Evening, Morning & Non Summer Mid Day / Overnight prices should be set at 3.0 / 1.5 / 1.0. This heuristic approach approximates cost causation and is easy to convey and understand for ratepayers. This method has been used by Burbank Water and Power[^70] and the Sacramento Municipal Utility District uses a ratio of about 2.5 / 1.5 / 1.0 for its rates.[^71] Jim Lazar of The Regulatory Assistance Project and coauthor of _Smart Rate Design for a Smart Future_[^72] recommended this type of simple rate design approach to the Hawai'i Public Utility Commission over more complex, precise methods.[^73]

## 8 Recommendations

**Q.** What are your recommendations in this proceeding based on the analysis presented in your testimony?

**A.** I make the following recommendations:

1. The Commission should authorize ComEd to offer an opt-in STOU rate to residential and small commercial customers as a more effective alternative to the current RTP rate.
2. The STOU rate should be a fully volumetric TOU energy charge.
3. ComEd's proposal to include a Monthly Capacity Charge and TMC should be rejected for the many reasons I enumerate here.
