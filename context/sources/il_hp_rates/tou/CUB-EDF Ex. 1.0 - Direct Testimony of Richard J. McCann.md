M.Cubed specializes in economic and public policy consulting to public and private sector clients. My resume with further details is attached to this testimony.[^1]

### 1.1 Purpose of This Testimony

**Q.** What is the purpose of your testimony?

**A.** I am testifying on Commonwealth Edison’s (ComEd) proposed distribution-based time-of-use (TOU) rate tariff. On behalf of the Citizens Utility Board and Environmental Defense Fund, I address the need to add a companion supply-based TOU rate tariff, to adjust the pricing periods to match those of the supply TOU pricing pilot to simplify rates for customers, and to create separate seasonal pricing periods. I highlight that low-income customers are most likely to benefit with discounted or no customer charges. And finally, that revenue neutrality in rate design should be determined by including the cost savings created by customers responding to price signals.

**Q.** How is your testimony organized?

**A.** I first identify the issues in the utility’s testimony that we are contending. I then discuss how Commonwealth Edison should align its TOU periods between a distribution and supply TOU rate to simplify the programs for customers. I describe how the utility should use transparent quantitative criteria to identify the pricing periods and seasons. I review analyses of net benefits to low-income customers, and I consider the rationale for adjusting the revenue neutrality benchmark to reflect cost savings. I conclude with a set of proposed illustrative rates using the methodology discussed here.

### 1.2 Summary of Recommendations

**Q.** What are your recommendations in this proceeding based on the analysis presented in your testimony?

**A.** I discuss the following recommendations in my testimony:

1. The Commission should order ComEd to offer a companion supply TOU rate schedule based on its Residential TOU Pilot Program (RTOUPP). Customers who enroll in the supply TOU rate should be automatically enrolled in the distribution TOU rate as well.
2. Customers on supply-side time variant rates such as real time pricing (RTP), RTOUPP or third-party offerings should not be required to enroll in the distribution TOU (DTOU) rate schedule. Non-residential customers on time variant rates are not currently required to do so.
3. ComEd should adjust the hours for its DTOU rate to match those used in the RTOUPP to simplify the rates for customers and avoid confusion.
4. Both the distribution and supply TOU schedules should have separate summer and non-summer seasons.
5. When calculating the benchmark for allocating revenue requirements to both the distribution and supply TOU schedules, the projected system costs savings from the reduced peak period use should be deducted.
6. The Commission should order ComEd to adopt the TOU rates calculation methodology used to develop the illustrative rates presented here.
7. The enrollment incentive payment for electric vehicle (EV) owners in Rider EVEN should be based on the projected resource savings from their participation. This would be either a two-year payment of $463 or a monthly payment of $9 for as long as the customer participates. Further, the combined (distribution and supply) TOU should be added to the list of time variant rates that qualify a customer to receive ComEd’s rebate for installing a Level 2 charger.

## 2 Summary of Commonwealth Edison's Proposals Addressed in this Testimony

**Q.** Which of the utilities’ proposals are you addressing in your testimony?

**A.** Commonwealth Edison is proposing to implement a new time-of-use (TOU) rate schedule (DTOU) for distribution service (excluding its default supply service) using a four-period pricing algorithm.[^2] The customers who are on a supply-side time variant rate such as real time pricing (RTP) or a third-party TOU rate offering would be required to also use the distribution TOU rate. An RTP rate differs from TOU in that the price varies hourly based on changes in the PJM Interconnection’s wholesale electricity market, which are not known in advance, whereas a TOU rate is preset and constant across a predesignated stretch of hours each day. For example, a TOU rate might cost 15 cents every day from 2 pm to 7pm, 5 cents from 10 pm to 7am, and 10 cents for all hours between those two periods. I also address the lack of a proposal for a supply-side TOU rate that is paired with the distribution TOU rate so as to expand the acceptability of TOU rates among customers.[^3] Further, I confirm Commonwealth Edison’s finding that low-income customers are at least no worse off on a TOU rate and may be better off and I comment on ComEd’s proposal to offer an enrollment incentive to EV owners.

## 3 Commonwealth Edison Should Align Its Supply and Distribution Time Variant Rates Tariffs

### 3.1 Commonwealth Edison Is Not Precluded from Offering a Companion Supply TOU Rate Tariff

**Q.** Is Commonwealth Edison requiring customers who are on time variant rates, which include RTP and TOU versions, to also enroll in the distribution TOU rate?

**A.** Yes. Commonwealth Edison witness Kathleen Kremer first asserts that pairing time variant supply and distribution rates are the only sensible approach to maximize the effect of the rate.[^4] She also states this combination is needed for ease of administration by the utility.[^5] However, Commonwealth Edison is not proposing a comparable supply side TOU.[^6]

**Q.** Do you agree with ComEd witness Kremer that administering both a time variant supply rate and a time variant distribution rate is simpler than if one of those two rates were not time variant?

**A.** Not necessarily, and not under the rates Kremer proposes ComEd offer. Kremer proposes to pair Rate BES-H, a RTP rate, with Rate DTOU, a TOU rate. This is especially complex to administer, as it would require calculation of rates to include a Distribution Facilities Charge calculated based on applying each of three volumetric rates to the customer’s kWh usage during the hours corresponding to each rate, a volumetric supply charge calculated by applying the corresponding PJM Interconnection hourly rate for each kilowatt hour used, and a fixed Capacity Charge calculated based on usage during system peak hours the previous year. ComEd’s RTP rate is already complex to administer by itself. Adding a TOU distribution component may be attractive to customers who choose it and should be available as an option, but pairing the two cannot reasonably be said to be simpler to administer than current rate offerings. However, it is simpler to administer a TOU rate that incorporates both distribution and supply charges, according to one shared set of rate period hours. In practice, administering this rate would only require adding the supply and distribution rates together.

**Q.** Should Commonwealth Edison offer a companion supply TOU rate?

**A.** Yes. The fact is that Commonwealth Edison already offers several time variant rates that include supply costs, including the residential and commercial RTP rate, a peak demand charge to non-residential customers based on time based Maximum Kilowatts Delivered (MKD),[^7] its Peak Time Savings rate,[^8] and the recently closed residential TOU pilot program (RTOUPP).[^9] Commonwealth Edison is offering these as both the default load serving entity and the distribution utility. Providing a supply rate that uses the same time period segmentation is an intuitive and sensible step. While I am not an attorney, a plain reading of the language of the Climate and Equitable Jobs Act (CEJA) demonstrates that this proceeding is to address ComEd’s rate design generally. Thus, I see no reason why Commonwealth Edison should be precluded from offering a new supply TOU rate alongside its new distribution TOU rate. As Kremer references in her testimony, “[w]hether or not the TOU rate as proposed in the future rate design investigation proceeding is appropriate and should be adopted will be determined in that proceeding. However, the Commission directs ComEd to work with Staff and stakeholders in the development of the new proposed distribution TOU rate.”[^10] As stated, I am not an attorney, but one need not have legal training to discern that a requirement that the utility propose a distribution TOU does not thereby bar the Commission from considering a corresponding supply TOU as well. Other than an incorrect assertion that this is exclusively a “delivery proceeding,” Kremer offers no other support for her claim to the contrary,[^11] and no other witness offers a rationale.

### 3.2 Time Variant Rate Schedules Must Be Simple for Consumers to Understand and Use to Be Widely Adopted

**Q.** Why should Commonwealth Edison offer a companion supply TOU rate?

**A.** Given that the utility is arguing that matching supply and distribution time variant rates is required for customers to have simpler “customer friendly” choices, the lack of such a proposal is surprising and inconsistent with the utility’s reasoning.[^12] ComEd’s proposal to automatically enroll Rate RTP customers in its DTOU while ignoring the TOU periods it developed for its TOU pilot program, without an option to take service on aligning TOUs for both distribution and supply, defies common sense. As I pointed out previously, having one portion of a customer’s bill determined on an hourly basis and another on a multipart time period basis complicates a customer’s decision rather than simplifies it. Often the real time prices in the PJM generation market diverge from the preset distribution TOU prices. For example, higher solar power production in the early afternoon in the spring can push PJM prices below nighttime prices while the distribution TOU price would be higher at the same time. This means that customers would have to track two separate, and at times conflicting, price signal streams. These confusing signals are likely to blunt the intended consumer response. A single unified overall price, such as one delivered through a combined TOU rate, avoids that problem. The differences in customer acceptance of RTP versus TOU rate also is readily apparent when looking at data for other utilities and states. Illinois is the only state with residential RTP; in contrast, out of 829 utilities reporting, 347 or 42% offer residential TOU rates.[^13] Only 1.1% of residential customers have enrolled in ComEd’s RTP tariff since its inception in 2007,[^14] while the average TOU tariff offered by U.S. utilities enrolled 3.4% of a utility’s residential customers in 2019.[^15] Further, at least two states have already adopted default residential TOU rates for investor-owned utilities (Missouri[^16] and California[^17]). California’s rooftop solar customers have been required to be on TOU rates since 2017.[^18] Continuation of pilot programs that study TOU rates has been popular. In California, more than 90% of the 500,000 customers in each of the three utilities’ pilots elected to remain in the TOU rate.[^19] Similarly, the TOU pilot study conducted by the Sacramento Municipal Utility District was extremely popular, and 98% of its customers have remained on the default TOU rate since 2018.[^20] In regards to relative effectiveness, a 2022 study found that a standard TOU rate can accomplish 60% to 70% of the load shifting effects of an RTP, and the gap can be closed with the addition of either critical peak pricing[^21] or peak time rebates[^22] that are invoked a dozen times each year.[^23] ComEd offers a peak time rebate program, Peak Time Savings.[^24] With higher enrollment rates and little resistance to being implemented on an opt-out default basis than an RTP rate, a TOU rate has a much higher potential overall impact on reducing ComEd’s Super Peak loads.

**Q.** Should residential customers on supply-side time variant rates be compelled to also enroll in the DTOU tariff?

**A.** No. Non-residential ComEd customers on time variant rates are not compelled to do so, and neither should residential customers. Traditionally TOU and RTP rates have focused on supply side prices because the time differentiated source of those costs is more transparent through wholesale markets such as PJM. Forcing many of the existing RTP and other time variant rate customers onto a new rate schedule could be disruptive for them. Instead, ComEd should focus on offering a new companion supply TOU rate that brings new customers over to these offerings.

### 3.3 Commonwealth Edison’s Studies Show that TOU Rates Deliver Greater Bill Savings to Customers and Peak Load Reductions to the System than RTP Rates

**Q.** Please compare the studies that Commonwealth Edison prepared on its residential RTP program and TOU pilot program.

**A.** The utility recently published a study on these two programs. The first is on the RRTP program which began operation in 1998.[^25] The second is on the residential TOU pilot (RTOUPP) started in 2020 and ending this June.[^26] Although both tariffs vary only the supply portion of ComEd’s bundled rate, the bill savings are not directly comparable because the rates are computed differently. Nonetheless, the peak load changes are informative about the relative effectiveness of each rate schedule. The RTP rate has about 41,200 enrolled participants, and the study covered 38,500. The average peak load reduction was 0.013 kilowatts during the 11 am to 6 pm “peak” period.[^27] It took three day-ahead alerts to achieve an average reduction of 0.057 kilowatts. About 99% of participants achieved bill savings. The RTOUPP averaged about 1,800 participants. Overall, Super Peak Period loads decreased 0.10 kilowatts or 6.7% while loads increased in Peak and Off Peak Periods as customers shifted usage.[^28] The peak reduction for the 1,250 customers in the pilot from 2020 to 2023 was 3.4% from the base year. In contrast, the control group on the standard Basic Electric Service (BES) rate increased their peak load contribution by 7.0%, so the net total reduction was close to 10.4%.[^29] That translates to an average of 0.26 kilowatts per participant. More than 98% of customers had bill savings.[^30] Customers in the TOU pilot appear to have reduced peak loads about eight times more than those on the RTP tariff and 75% more even for the most responsive subset. Differences in how the programs are administered could account for much of these changes, but given they are opt-in offerings, both programs likely have similar self-selection biases in recruiting those who are amenable to time variant rates. Given these outcomes, the TOU rate seems to offer at least the same level of load reduction benefits with much simpler rate structure for customers to follow. For this reason, the Commission should encourage Commonwealth Edison to offer a companion supply TOU rate and shift its marketing efforts to promote combined supply and distribution TOU service.

### 4.2 The TOU Periods Should Reflect Seasonal Differences in Cost Causation

**Q.** Is it appropriate to impose a Super Peak period year-round?

**A.** No. Commonwealth Edison has two distinctive load patterns or profiles between the “summer” for June to September and “non-summer” for October to May. Figure CUB-EDF-1 compares the two seasonal load profiles. The most obvious contrast is that the highest average load in the non-summer evening is in the bottom third of summer loads. That would place that load in the lower half of the Peak hours using the feeder peaking data. No hours during the October to May season approach the Super Peak loads. Charging customers Super Peak rates during non-summer would not reflect cost causation, and the benefits of load-shifting (and thus the need for a time-variant price signal) are far stronger in the summer than non-summer. For these reasons, the Super Peak period should be confined to the June to September summer season.

## 5 Low-income Customers Benefit More from TOU Rates

**Q.** Are the benefits to low-income customers to be considered in designing TOU rate programs?

**A.** Yes. In establishing the Multi-Year Integrated Grid Plan requirement, CEJA states the plan shall be designed to “support efforts to bring at least 40% of the benefits of those benefits to Equity Investment Eligible Communities.”[^33] ComEd includes its peak load reduction programs, including its current time variant rate offering (Rate RTP) in this plan.[^34] To this end, the new TOU program needs to deploy a rate structure that best achieves this objective.

**Q.** What are the results of studies done on Commonwealth Edison data?

**A.** To make this assessment, Kremer reviews the results from the studies of ComEd’s Rate RTP and RTOUPP in her testimony.[^35] One analysis assesses the number of low-income customers who are on the RRTP rate who would receive bill savings with the addition of the proposed distribution TOU rate.[^36] That analysis showed that 58% to 76% of those customers would see decreased bills of 0.8% to 4.0%. That is equivalent to $0.17 to $1.40 per month. The second analysis used the results from the supply-oriented RTOUPP.[^37] That study showed average monthly savings of $9.87 per month. Again, these savings from the supply TOU are a multiple of the RTP savings, indicating that adopting a supply TOU rate schedule may be advantageous over the current RTP rate.

**Q.** Is there another step that should be taken to improve the benefits to low-income customers?

**A.** A study conducted in 2019 using data from 100,000 Commonwealth Edison customers found that imposing a fixed customer charge disproportionately disadvantages lowincome customers.[^38] Those customers benefit from the shift of time variant volumetric charges, but the fixed charges are proportionately a large share of their overall bill, so the net effect is adverse to the interests of low-income households. The Low Income Discount Rate being considered in ICC Docket 24-0163 likely will address this concern while preserving the differential price effects necessary for an effective TOU rate.[^39] The discounts would be applied to the total bill, but importantly, are calculated for each customer based on what they would have paid under default ComEd rates. For example, applying the 77% discount for single family home nonheating customers to the distribution and supply charges of a TOU customer would reduce the rate for each time period by 77% of the amount of the fixed default charges that would otherwise apply. Suppose the sum of the Distribution Facilities Charge and volumetric supply rate is 10 cents per kWh under default rates for this customer class. The rate for each TOU period would be 7.7 cents per kWh less than what it would be without the discount. This maintains the same cents per kWh difference between each time period’s rates, and by reducing the amount of each rate, increases the ratio between the higher and lower rates. Thus, depending on whether you compare rates by cents or ratios, the incentive to shift load is either the same or greater for low-income customers. Combining a TOU rate with the Low Income Discount Rate ComEd proposes in Docket 24-0163, or any discount rate designed similarly enhances the pricing effect across periods and increases benefits for low-income customers.

## 6 Determination of Revenue Neutrality for TOU Migration Must Include Cost Savings from Customer Participation

**Q.** How should Commonwealth Edison assess revenue neutrality for these TOU programs?

**A.** The point of adopting TOU rates is to create system-wide savings for all customers—if Commonwealth Edison assumes there are no such savings, then why is the utility bothering to implement these rates? Those savings should arrive in both deferred capital investments in generation, transmission and distribution and lower energy purchase costs during high demand hours. The distribution savings should be identified in a similar manner to those for generation and transmission already included in the Multi-Year Grid Plan. Simply taking the costs created by customers who are on the standard BES rate and assuming that they will impose the same costs ignores the purpose of TOU rates. For this reason, an appropriate cost of service study must first account for either forecasted or realized savings. Including costs that are unlikely to materialize inflates the purported revenue requirements which means the utility will overcollect relative to true costs and TOU rates will be too high. To do otherwise is likely to create windfall excess revenues for the utility and to undercompensate the customers who are taking on a more price responsive burden on behalf of all customers. Customers on TOU rates must take actions to change their consumption choices and they risk using electricity during higher cost periods; in return overall system demand is reduced during the most stressful periods. Commonwealth Edison has not undertaken this required task of resetting the revenue requirement baseline in designing the rates presented here. Second, any adjustments to utility revenue requirements should be made ex-post using actual data on sales, revenues, and costs rather than relying on speculation on what customers might do. The burden of uncertainty should be on the utility that possesses all the necessary data and information to conduct this analysis rather than on outside parties that might not even know what data is available or are blocked from this data due to customer confidentiality.

## 7 The Rider EVEN Should Reflect the System Cost Savings Created by Time Variant Charging

**Q.** Is ComEd’s proposed Rider EVEN enrollment incentive a reasonable means of increasing participation in time variant rates by electric vehicle owners?

**A.** A $2 per month incentive payment, capped at 2 years and $48, is unlikely to induce additional enrollment given that an EV buyer already spent several thousand dollars more than for an internal combustion engine vehicle and in turn may have received up to $11,500 in state rebates[^40] and federal tax credits.[^41] They also likely will have paid a significant amount, and received a ComEd rebate of up to $3,750, to purchase and install a home charger.[^42] ComEd’s incentive is less than 0.5% of these other incentives; this amount will be lost in the taxes and fees in purchasing the car. ComEd’s administrative costs might exceed this incentive. Without supporting analysis, any belief that an incentive of $48 over two years is sufficient to change a customer’s choice is simply baseless. The much stronger and more obvious incentive is to make ComEd’s Level 2 EV charger rebate available to TOU customers. A more efficient incentive payment for EV drivers to enroll in the TOU rate should be based on system savings created by shifting charging away from the Super Peak period. The RTOUPP report shows that EV charging increased 1.03 kilowatts during Off Peak overnight period.[^43] The report goes on to speculate that these customers may be charging more at home and, implicitly, less during Super Peak hours because of their enrollment in the TOU rate. If we assume that the Super Peak reduction is equivalent to the Off Peak increase, the incentive should be based on this one kilowatt reduction. That reduction can be valued in part based on the PJM Interconnection’s reliability capacity benchmark, which is the cost for a new combustion turbine.[^44] The upfront installation cost is $927 per kilowatt and the annualized cost is $108 per kilowatt-year. If ComEd offers a two-year incentive, the payment would be $463 per year. Or if ComEd offers an annual incentive, the payment would be $9 per month for an indefinite period. Even this amount understates the expected value of an EV owner taking service on a combined supply and distribution TOU because it reflects a supply-only TOU rate. If ComEd is unwilling to base its incentive on the system value, ComEd should withdraw this proposal as it will be meaningless and a distraction from other aspects of the DTOU tariff. I am not convinced allocating $2 a month in bill credits to these customers yields any added system benefits at all, let alone in an amount exceeding administrative costs.

## 9 Recommendations

**Q.** What are your recommendations in this proceeding based on the analysis presented in your testimony?

**A.** I make the following recommendations:

1. The Commission should order ComEd to offer a companion supply TOU rate schedule based on its RTOUPP. Customers who enroll in the supply TOU rate should be enrolled automatically in the distribution TOU as well.
2. Customers on existing supply-side time variant rates such as RTP or third-party offerings should not be required to enroll in the DTOU rate schedule, though they should retain the option to do so on an opt-in basis.
3. ComEd should adjust the hours for its DTOU rate to match those used in the RTOUPP to simplify the rates for customers and avoid confusion.
4. Both the distribution and supply TOU schedules should have separate summer and non-summer seasons.
5. When calculating the benchmark for allocating revenue requirements to both the distribution and supply TOU schedules, the projected system costs savings from the reduced peak period use should be deducted.
6. The Commission should order ComEd to adopt the TOU rates calculation methodology used to develop the illustrative rates presented here.
7. The enrollment incentive payment for EV owners in the Rider EVEN should be based on the projected resource savings from their participation. This would be either a two-year payment of $463 or a monthly payment of $9 for as long as the customer participates. Further, receipt of ComEd’s rebate for installing a Level 2 charger should be contingent on enrolling in the combined TOU tariff, Rate BES-H, or an ARES time-variant rate.

**Q.** Does this conclude your testimony?

**A.** Yes. Thank you for the opportunity to present my findings.
