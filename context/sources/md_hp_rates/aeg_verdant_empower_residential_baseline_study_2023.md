## 1 Introduction

Under the directive of the Maryland Public Service Commission, the Future Programming Work Group considered proposals on a new, broader goal structure that included energy reduction, greenhouse gas reduction, electrification, and distributed energy resources (DERs), among other topics. There was consensus among most stakeholders that conducting a statewide survey to identify potential achievable cost-effective GHG abatement is the most appropriate investment to inform future program designs. Therefore, the EmPOWER Maryland Utilities (Baltimore Gas & Electric (BGE), Potomac Electric Power Company (Pepco), Delmarva, Southern Maryland Electric Cooperative (SMECO), Potomac Edison, and Washington Gas) supported a Request for Proposal for the development of a study that provides technical analysis for assessing cost-effective GHG abatement opportunities. The AEG Team (Applied Energy Group, Verdant Associates, Cadeo Group, and The Brattle Group), were selected to perform the study.

This report presents the results of the saturation survey that was completed as part of the EmPOWER Maryland Greenhouse Gas Abatement Potential Study, originally designed to establish a baseline to inform future opportunities for GHG abatement. The AEG Team conducted residential surveys for each of the participating utilities: BGE, Delmarva, Pepco, Potomac Edison, SMECO, and Washington Gas. The surveys focus on appliance and technology saturations within homes throughout the state and collected information on the following end-uses:

## 2 Sample Design and Weighting Methodology

The residential baseline study data collection effort utilized a web-based survey. The data collection approach allowed the team to leverage the large residential populations, information available to the utilities, and relatively low-cost web-survey techniques to obtain large, robust samples of residential responses across multiple strata of interest.

For the residential baseline study's large primary data collection effort, the success of the study is highly dependent on the development of the population frame, the determination of the variables available for sampling, the sample design, the survey development, and the survey process and number of completes.

The residential population frame was developed using data from each utility. To develop the frame, the AEG Team requested the following data from each utility:

- Residential customer information system data (CIS)
- Residential customer billing data
- Residential customer participation in low income (LI) customer assistance programs

These three data sources were combined using utility customer identification variables to develop each utility's residential customer frame. The CIS provided information on the number of residential customers per utility, their address, and information on whether the utility had both email and street addresses or only street addresses for individual customers. The customer billing data was used to characterize customer usage. Customers were characterized as high users if their annual energy consumption was in the top 34% of their utility's residential customer energy usage. For BGE, the customer billing data was also used to verify information on fuel types delivered, whether the customer received only electricity, only natural gas, or both natural gas and electricity from BGE. The residential customer LI assistance program participation data was used to help identify LI customers, though customers were also asked income questions during the residential survey to determine their LI status.

### 2.1 Sample Design

The AEG Team set a utility specific sample size of 18,000 customers and the preliminary baseline survey quota was set at 450 per utility and fuel, where applicable (see Table 2-1). This design was established to support saturation estimates at the 90/10 confidence and relative precision level for each utility.

The utility population frames were reviewed to identify variables to be used in building type and LI/non-LI stratification. The utility population frames did not consistently include variables identifying building type or customer income. In addition to any building type details the utility may have provided, the AEG Team used the customer site addresses, parsing the address components and using apartment numbers to identify those customers living in MF versus SF homes.[^3] The utility sample and the survey quota were divided evenly between MF and SF customers to ensure that the survey responses could be used to accurately represent the equipment saturations by building type.

While it is not standard for utility population frames to include information on customer income, it is often possible to use information on customer participation in LI or rate assistance programs to identify a sub-set of the customer frame that is identified as LI. The AEG Team used these data to help identify LI customers. The number of customers identified as participating in these programs, however, is usually insufficient to ensure an adequate number of LI respondents for the levels of confidence and precision desired for this study.

The team decided to identify customer eligibility for the LI survey sample through a combination of customers who had participated in LI assistance programs and survey responses. An initial baseline study screening question asked customers if their household income was above or below $57,500.[^4] If the survey respondent stated that their household income was below this level, they were identified as part of the LI sample and households with incomes above this level were placed into the non-LI sample. Using this approach, the number of LI sample points in the sample population is unknown, though Table 2-2 below uses the LI program participants as the identified LI sample population. The screening question increased the number of sample points in the LI strata (during the survey process), increasing the likelihood that LI survey strata, with small initial sample sizes, could approach their survey quota. The survey quota for LI customers was 225 per utility and the quota for non-LI customers was also 225. Using the building type and economic flags, the quota for a given building type and economic level was 112.5. For example, the quota for SF/LI responses was 112.5.

The sample was further stratified by customer energy consumption. If a customer's energy consumption placed them in the top 34 percent of the utility's energy consumption, they were identified as a high usage customer. All other customers were identified as normal usage customers. It is common practice in a baseline study to stratify sample and survey quota by usage due to interest in insuring that the survey adequately represents both types of customer usage and the equipment in their homes.

The baseline survey quotas were evenly divided by building type, economics, and usage (56 to 57 respondents per strata). ### 2.2 Web-Survey Design

The residential baseline survey was developed in collaboration with the EmPOWER Maryland Utilities and was designed to provide input data for the greenhouse gas abatement potential study. The survey was designed to take between 10 and 20 minutes to complete and included many questions regarding the saturation of appliances and technologies, the customer's knowledge of existing energy efficiency programs, and if they currently owned solar, storage, or an electric vehicle.

The survey included screening questions to determine the customer's eligibility for the survey. Individuals were eligible if they lived at the sample survey respondent address, were a customer of a participating utility but did not work for a utility, if they made or contributed to the energy decisions for the household and paid for their utility bills, and they were at last 18 years old. Customers who met these criterial and who chose to complete the survey were asked questions covering the following area:

- Home characteristics including type of residence, age of home, square footage, rent or own, number of bathrooms, number of stories, and if the home was in an urban, suburban or rural area.
- Description of the space heating equipment including the type and fuel, age, and high or base efficiency level.
- Description of the space cooling equipment including the type, age, and high or base efficiency level.
- Description of the home's thermostat.
- Description of the water heating equipment including the type and fuel, and age.

[PDF p. 11 | printed p. 10]

- Number and configuration of refrigerators and freezers, the equipment's age and high or base efficiency level.
- Description of major appliances including dishwashers, clothes washers, clothes dryers, and swimming pool or spa equipment.
- Types and number of various types of home office and entertainment equipment. Customers were asked if they currently work from a home office and if they anticipate that a household member would continue to work from home in the next year.
- Description of lighting equipment including the type and number of bulbs and recent bulb purchase type.
- Description of the number of electric or plug-in hybrid electric vehicles owned or leased by members of the household. Customers with electric or plug-in hybrid electric vehicles were asked if they charged their vehicles at home and/or away from home. Customers were also asked if they anticipated purchasing an electric vehicle within the next three years.
- Presence of solar photovoltaic (PV) system, year of installation and capacity of the system. Customers without PV were asked if they anticipated installing PV within the next three years.
- Presence of a battery storage system, year of installation, and the energy capacity of the system. Customers without battery storage were asked if they anticipated purchasing a system within the next three years.
- Knowledge of utility program.
- Household demographics including the age of household members, their education level and employment status, and the household's total annual income.

Prior to full implementation of the survey, the survey was reviewed by the EmPOWER Maryland Utilities and other stakeholders. Comments were provided, reviewed, and incorporated. The survey was tested on team members for length and logic consistency. Following internal testing the survey was field tested on a small sample of the sample population. Following review of the small field test results, the full survey process began.

### 2.3 Survey Process

The residential baseline survey was a web-based survey. The utilities, however, did not have email addresses for all their customers.[^5] For sample customers with an email address in the utility CIS, an email was sent inviting the customer to participate in the survey. The email explained that the research was supported by the EmPOWER Maryland Utilities and that the research was conducted as part of the statewide EmPOWER Maryland Program in efforts to:

- Help Maryland residents save energy and money,
- Meet statewide energy efficiency goals, and
- Reduce Greenhouse gas emissions.

Customers who chose to participate in the survey were instructed to click on a link in the email. They were informed that they would receive a $20 gift card as a "thank you" for completing the survey. The survey was available in English and Spanish.

The sample customers whose email was known to their utility were divided into three groups. The email invitations to the three groups were staged across different weeks to reduce potential impacts to the utility call centers. Sample customers were given approximately one week to respond to the survey's email invitation. For those customers who did not respond to the initial invitation, an additional invitation was sent reminding them of the survey opportunity. Following the second email, a final reminder email was sent to encourage the customers to participate in the survey. Approximately one week after the last groups' final email invitation, the survey was closed to further participation to enable the analysis of the responses.

Sample customers whose email address was not included in their utility's CIS were divided into two groups and one of the two groups was initially sent postcards inviting them to participate in the survey. The second group was also sent a postcard invitation, though at a later date to reduce potential impacts to the utility call centers. The front of the postcard included the utility and EmPOWER Maryland logo and

[^5]: While the share of customers with email addresses differed by utility, over two-thirds of customers had email addresses with the study sample population.

[PDF p. 13 | printed p. 12] informed the individual that they were chosen to participate in the Home Energy Use Survey and they could receive a $20 gift card for their response.

The back of the postcard provided the customer with the information similar to what email customers received. They could directly connect with the survey using the QR code or copy the survey link into their web browser. Postcard recipients were provided with a five-digit customer code to enable the customer's responses to be uniquely identified and aligned with their utility data. ### 2.4 Weighting Methodology

Weights were developed to mirror the population of the six different utilities (and seven utility groups). The weighting was complicated by two attributes of the sample. First, we do not have a direct count of the number of LI households in the EmPOWER Maryland Utilities. Second, nearly all the WGL respondents identified their electric utility as one of the five electric utilities participating in the baseline study. The approach to these complications is described below.

To estimate the number of LI households in each utility's territory, the team attached information from the 2020 census at the census track level to each utility's population of customers. Using these data, it was possible to develop an estimate of the share of LI customers by utility, building type (SF/MF), usage (high/regular), and survey delivery type (email or postcards). First the share of customers with income below $50,000 and the share of customers with income above $75,000 was identified.[^9] The baseline survey screening question used households whose income was below $57,500 as LI, therefore, the calculation of weights used linear interpolation of the $50,000 and $75,000 shares to develop an estimate of the share of households with income below $57,500. For example, using this approach, approximately 38% of BGE all-electric, regular usage MF households where the utility knows their email were identified as LI while only 26% of their SF households were classified as LI. These LI shares were then applied to population counts to identify the utility population within each of the 16 groups within a utility and 112 groups across the seven utility groups. Using a standard set of expansion weights, the

[^9]: The sample of customers was randomly drawn from each utility enabling us to assume that the distribution within our sample population was consistent with the utility's population of customers.

[PDF p. 15 | printed p. 14] survey responses were weighted to represent the number of customers within each utility.[^10] These weights are used for all utility specific analyses and representation of findings.

It is not possible to simply sum the utility specific counts to develop statewide estimates of technology saturations because nearly all WGL households receive their electricity from other utilities in the study.[^11] Using the utility specific weights would double count the WGL households, once as WGL customers and once as their electric counterpart. To develop statewide weights, the team reallocated the WGL responses to their appropriate electric utility (survey respondents were asked to identify their electric utility). The statewide weights were then developed in an approach that was similar to the utility specific weight, but with the WGL responses counted as part of their electric utility.

[^10]: For some customer groups where the utility did not know the customers' emails, the low survey responses led to large expansion weights. For groups of customers with individual customer weights exceeding 5,000, their weights were capped at 5,000. The remaining customer count was reallocated to the similar utility customer group where the utility did know the customers' emails.

While the figure above presents these saturations at the statewide level across all housing and income types, there are some interesting differences in equipment saturations across utilities and housing/income types. Some of these differences are anticipated. For example, when comparing the saturations of air source heat pumps across utilities, the share of homes with heat pumps is generally not statistically significantly different[^15] across the electric-only utilities (for example, the share of homes with air source heat pumps in Delmarva and Potomac Edison territories are not statistically different). There is a statistically significant difference in the share of homes with heat pumps, however, when comparing the share of homes with heat pumps in Washington Gas or BGE territories to the shares for homes in the electric-only utility territories, households in the gas or dual-fuel utility territories have a significantly lower share of air-source heat pumps than their all-electric utility counterparts. When gas heating is available, Maryland households appear to prefer a non-heat pump form of heating.

The analysis of baseline data also found a statistically significant difference in the number of second refrigerators reported by SMECO respondents compared with those in Pepco or BGE territories. The difference in the share of homes with second refrigerators may be explained by the higher share of single-family homes within SMECO's territory (83 percent) relative to Pepco (70 percent) or BGE (74 percent) and the finding that second refrigerators are more statistically significantly more common in single family homes (see Figure 3-16) than multifamily households. Analysis of the share of homes with second refrigerators also found that second refrigerators are significantly more likely to be found in SF non-LI (35 percent) than SF LI homes (17 percent), they are also more common in SF LI homes than MF non-LI (2.7 percent) and in MF non-LI than MF LI (0.1 percent).

The analysis of baseline data also found that single family homes are significantly more likely to have stand-alone freezers than multifamily houses. There is no statistical difference, however, in the share of homes with freezers by income type; the share of SF LI homes with stand-alone freezers is not statistically different from the share of SF non-LI homes with stand-alone freezers.
