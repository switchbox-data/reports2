## Abstract

### Background and Purpose

Residential Appliance Saturation Surveys (RASS) are conducted periodically to provide basic information characterizing the buildings, appliances and equipment, and to some degree, occupants of homes throughout the State of Connecticut. The results provide valuable information for program planning, identifying measure gaps and opportunities, existing age of equipment, and other data. The previous studies were conducted in 2012 and updated in 2018.

The goal of the study was to inventory and characterize residential end uses including household appliances, electronics, HVAC equipment, and water heaters. Beyond the scope of a typical RASS, this study also explored topics related to potential electrification efforts and barriers, including data collection on electric panels to assess readiness for electrifying measures, as well as an assessment of homes for their suitability to install heat pumps and heat pump water heaters.

This study used a combination of web survey (1,030 responses), and follow-up of virtual audits at 140 of these homes. The survey also included an option for respondents to take and upload photos of their appliances and mechanical equipment, including nameplates, which substantially increased sample to draw conclusions about various equipment types. The study products include this report addressing key findings for all equipment types, a separate document containing extensive look-up tables split by various strata of interest (e.g. income status, home type, EJ community), as well as a database supporting additional tabulations.

### Key Findings

The study found that while heat pump penetration has increased since the 2018 RASS (4% to 18% overall, and 2% to 8% as primary systems), electric resistance heating has also increased in prevalence, particularly in multifamily homes. Households are frequently adding cooling to previously uncooled space, room air conditioners (46%) being installed more often than heat pumps (36%). Electric resistance as primary heating is significantly more common in environmental justice communities (18% vs. 7%), among low income households (20% vs. 5%), and among renters (28% vs. 6%). Households in Connecticut displayed high levels of suitability for heat pumps and heat pump water heaters, and two-thirds (67%) of homes could accommodate electrifying their fuel-fired end uses without the need for a full service electrical upgrade. Findings on other “special topics” are included, along with tables of numerous tabulations in the Appendix.

### Inventory of Recommendations

The study offers the following recommendations based on the findings:

- Continue to focus heat pump recommendations on homes heating with delivered fuels and electric resistance, paying particular attention to the level of weatherization of homes receiving recommendations to ensure they will see benefits from their use.

[PDF p. 3 | printed p. 3]

- Considering the rate at which households are adding cooling to previously uncooled space, target these customers for heat pump installation.
- Continue to focus program efforts to install heat pumps and weatherize homes in environmental justice communities and among low income households and renters, where a large concentration of electric resistance heating and less efficient cooling was found.
- Target electrification in homes with favorable conditions such as 200-amp service and the use of delivered fuels or electric resistance heating. Consider offering incentives for electric service upgrades in cases where this is a barrier to electrification.
- Consider offering additional program support to moderate income households, where appliances were found to be less efficient.
- Encourage solar PV and battery storage installation alongside heat pump rebate programs.
- Focus efforts to remove barriers to EV ownership among residents of multifamily buildings.

## Executive Summary

NMR Group conducted a residential appliance saturation survey (RASS) of homes to inventory and characterize end uses such as heating and cooling equipment, water heating, and appliances in residential buildings in Connecticut. The study surveyed 1,030 single-family and multifamily households from May to July 2024, with a nested sample of 140 virtual audits conducted from June to September 2024. In addition to this main report which focuses on key findings, the study also provides a separate document of detailed tables by various strata of interest (home type income, etc.) as well as a database for further tabulation. This report provides added value beyond a typical RASS equipment inventory with a particular focus on heating and cooling and the potential for homes to electrify. To that end, the team estimated the suitability of existing housing stock for heat pump and heat pump water heater installations, and included analysis of homes’ electric panels for other potential electrification measures.

### KEY FINDINGS AND RECOMMENDATIONS

#### HEATING

**The number of single family homes with a heat pump has increased from 4% to 18% since 2018: 72% of heat pumps across the state were installed in the past five years. 8% of single-family households use heat pumps as their primary heating source, an increase from 2% in 2018.** The increase in heat pumps as primary and supplementary systems since 2018 are both statistically significant. Electricity as a primary heating fuel has also increased since 2018, from 11% to 14% in single-family homes. While the increase in heat pump penetration likely contributes to this increase, the study also noted an increase in electric resistance (baseboard or space heaters) used as primary heating (4% to 6% in single-family homes, and 19% to 41% in multifamily homes[^1], both statistically significant differences[^2]). Half (50%) of single family homes primarily heat with delivered fuels: 44% with oil and 6% with propane. One-third (32%) primarily heat with natural gas. The increase in electricity as a primary heating fuel seems to be related to a decrease in natural gas (from 35% to 32%) as opposed to delivered fuels, which remained consistent since the 2018 RASS.

#### COOLING

**Households in Connecticut are frequently adding or replacing cooling systems, often in previously uncooled spaces. Although heat pumps are increasing in prevalence, they still face competition with less efficient equipment such as room air conditioners:**

- While very few homes (2%) reported having no cooling system present, nearly half of homes (48%) reported having uncooled space.
- One in three households (31%) installed a new cooling system within the last three years; nearly half of those systems (44%) were installed in spaces not previously cooled.
- Room air conditioners are the most common type of system installed in previously uncooled spaces.

Heat pump penetration for cooling has increased from 4% to 12% since the 2018 RASS (a statistically significant difference), although room air conditioners (47% penetration) and central air conditioners (40%) are still the most common form of cooling. While more homes installed heat pumps for cooling, the penetration of central air conditioning systems in single family homes increased since 2018 (36% to 40%), while the study observed a slight decrease in room air conditioner penetration (49% to 47%).

Of households reporting installing new cooling systems in the last three years, 63% who purchased heat pumps did so to add cooling to a previously uncooled area, as did 57% who purchased room air conditioners. New installations (2021 or later) in previously uncooled spaces were typically room air conditioners (46%) and heat pumps (36%, primarily MSHP); however, 18% of households installed a new central air conditioner for additional cooling. > **➢ Recommendation 2a:** Households purchasing room air conditioners are likely good candidates for ductless MSHPs or window heat pump units[^4], although the study notes the difference in price point between these technologies. Attempt intervention in room air conditioner purchases at retail locations (in person or online) with program advertising highlighting the benefits of MSHPs and window heat pumps and the available incentives.

> **➢ Recommendation 2b:** Households purchasing central air conditioners are great candidates for ducted ASHPs. As shown above, most of these installations are replacing an existing system, and ducted ASHPs are nearly a like-for-like replacement from an installation standpoint. Target intervention in these purchases by leveraging relationships with HVAC contractors and providing them with training and education materials to convert CAC sales into ducted ASHPs where appropriate.

#### ENVIRONMENTAL JUSTICE COMMUNITIES

**Renters, low-income residents, and those living in environmental justice (EJ) communities are significantly more likely to primarily heat with electric resistance than their counterparts.** Electric resistance as primary heating is more common among renters (28%) than owners (6%), low-income residents (20%) than non-low- and moderate-income (LMI) residents (5%), and residents of EJ communities (18%) than not (7%). All of these are statistically significant differences. Heat pump penetration is lower amongst these groups, and they are also more likely to cool with room air conditioners or portable fans.

The highest-earning households among non-low- or moderate-income households drive heat pump penetration. More than one-quarter of households making over $150,000 annually (27%) have heat pumps, compared to 18% of households making less than $150,000 annually but more than 80% of the state median income for their household size (moderate-income). Continue to focus program efforts to install heat pumps and weatherize homes in these communities, taking into account the economics of the particular household (future electric bill costs), the costs to the program, and maintaining the affordability of heat pumps for all customers.

#### ELECTRIFICATION

**Based on the heat pump suitability analysis conducted in this study, most single family homes display desirable characteristics for heat pump installation, including favorable economics (currently heating with electric resistance or delivered fuels), uncooled rooms, and 200-amp service (no electric service upgrades required).** Of the 140 single family homes that received a virtual audit, nearly two-thirds (63%) of ducted homes and over four-fifths (84%) of non-ducted homes were good or excellent candidates for heat pump installation. The study also assessed homes for their suitability for heat pump water heater (HPWH) installation based on space and temperature criteria. Over three-quarters of single-family homes (76%) could easily accommodate a HPWH, meeting all key space and temperature constraints. Just under half (42%) of single-family homes meet these same space and temperature constraints for a HPWH and also have a drain present near the existing water heater, simplifying the handling of condensate.

> **➢ Recommendation 4:** Target electrification in homes with favorable conditions such as 200-amp service and the use of delivered fuels or electric resistance. Consider offering incentives for electric service upgrades in cases where this is a barrier to electrification. Create parameters around recommendations to ensure heat pumps or heat pump water heaters are not installed in suboptimal scenarios.

#### APPLIANCES

**Renters and low- or moderate-income residents have less efficient appliances.** The portion of ENERGY STAR® appliances is lower among renters for many common appliances such as refrigerators (38% compared to 54% for owners), clothes washers (53% to 68%), and clothes dryers (13% to 23%). Note that the study also found that most renters (92%) were responsible for their own utility bills (electric or gas), which complicates program intervention in upgrading appliances. A similar pattern is seen when comparing low- or moderate-income respondents to non-LMI respondents. Moderate income residents in particular have lower-efficiency appliances such as refrigerators (39% ENERGY STAR® compared to 54% among non-LMI), freezers (7% to 30%), and clothes dryers (10% to 28%); although the study also notes this group was found to be a relatively small portion (6%) of the overall population.

#### SOLAR AND ELECTRIC VEHICLES

**Solar photovoltaics (PV) and electric vehicle (EV) penetration have both increased since the 2018 RASS study, mostly benefitting single family, non-LMI homeowners. Fewer than one-quarter of homes with heat pumps have solar panels (22%), suggesting an opportunity to target these homes for further opportunities to reduce their energy consumption.** The study estimates that solar PV system penetration has increased from 2% to 17% since the 2018 RASS, a statistically significant difference. Most of the solar PV systems reported by survey respondents are installed on non-LMI homes (78%) and 97% are on owner-occupied homes, although almost half (42%) of PV systems were in EJ communities[^5].

Five percent of survey respondents own an electric vehicle or plug-in hybrid vehicle, an increase from 2% in the 2018 RASS. Ten percent of homeowners surveyed reported owning an electric or plug-in hybrid vehicle, compared to only 2% of renters. Only 1% of low- and moderate-income households reported owning an electric vehicle, compared to 13% of non-LMI households. Charging an electric vehicle is an obstacle to ownership. Only 1% of multifamily residents reported having a Level 2 EV charger at their home, compared to 5% of single-family residents. All of these comparisons represent statistically significant differences.

> **➢ Recommendation 6:** Encourage solar PV installation alongside heat pump rebate programs to maximize energy savings and reduce demand on the grid.

> **➢ Recommendation 7:** Focus efforts to remove barriers to EV ownership among residents of multifamily buildings and low-income residents, especially cost and the availability of charging infrastructure.

# Section 1 Background & Methods

## 1.1 OBJECTIVE

This report presents the results of the R2202 Residential Appliance Saturation Survey (RASS) study. Commissioned by the Connecticut Energy Efficiency Board (EEB) and overseen by the Connecticut Evaluation Administrator (EA) Team, the study conducted a comprehensive multimedia survey of Connecticut residences. The objective of the survey was to characterize the existing building stock of single-family and multifamily homes in Connecticut through a representative sample. To provide additional value beyond a typical equipment inventory often produced by a RASS type study, particular focus was directed towards heating and cooling equipment and electrification potential, including assessing the suitability of homes for space heating heat pumps and heat pump water heaters.

To that end, this report presents saturation, efficiency levels, and usage behaviors associated with the following types of building equipment and features:

- Appliances
- Heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) equipment and controls
- Domestic hot water (DHW) systems
- Renewables and other electrification measures
- Program participation and awareness
- Heat pump knowledge and attitudes

In addition to this report which focuses on overall findings, the study also produced a separate document containing extensive lookup tables split out by various strata of interest (e.g. income status, home type, EJ communities), as well as a database for additional tabulation. The report regularly calls out statistical significance when comparing data between groups or to the previous study, but it also includes universal confidence interval bands for both the survey and virtual audit sample which can be found in Appendix A.4 along with a brief explanation on their use.

## 1.2 BACKGROUND

Prior to this study, the last RASS study in Connecticut was conducted in 2018.[^7] The team designed the 2024 RASS study to provide updated, comprehensive data to inform energy-efficiency program planning, potential estimation, and evaluation baselines, with a special emphasis on obtaining statistically meaningful data for environmental justice (EJ) communities and collecting data related to electrification efforts and barriers.

## 1.5 STUDY LIMITATIONS AND SOURCES OF UNCERTAINTY

The study sought every opportunity to minimize uncertainty and produce specific findings and recommendations. Nonetheless, it is important to acknowledge that it is not possible to eliminate all sources of uncertainty and/or bias. Using utility customer data naturally biases the survey sample towards individually metered homes and units. This study reached current occupants of a home (homeowners and renters) who could describe the characteristics of their household and submit photographs of key equipment and appliances. Due to the difficulty of using utility customer data to reach multifamily households in master-metered buildings, coupled with lower response rates from multifamily homes in general, the 2024 RASS study likely under-represented multifamily customers. Despite applying weights to mitigate this and better represent Connecticut’s housing stock, the statewide estimates are skewed towards single-family homes, and the study presents single-family and multifamily results separately where appropriate.

### 5.1.2 Heat Pump Suitability

During virtual audits, technicians collected data at homes to assess the suitability of installing heat pumps. One of the first considerations in assessing a home’s suitability for heat pumps was the

[PDF p. 39 | printed p. 39]

**Among ducted homes visited during virtual audits, 25% were excellent candidates for ASHPs.** Nearly two-thirds (64%) of the 140 homes visited during virtual audits had central duct systems that covered all or a majority of the home[^16]. Another 38% have 200 amp service but currently heat with natural gas, which does not make as compelling of a case to switch to ASHP since the home may not see savings on their utility bills. ## 5.2 HEAT PUMP WATER HEATER SUITABILITY

During virtual audits, technicians collected data in the area where the current water heater was installed to assess the suitability of installing a HPWH. The assessment focused on three key criteria: the area remaining roughly above 40°F year-round, an adequate amount of space for the heat pump to work effectively, and sufficient ceiling height to accommodate the equipment. In addition, the presence of a drain in the space was also assessed but was not considered one of the key criteria since it does not pose as significant of a physical barrier as the other criteria[^19]. This methodology is consistent with the same analysis done in the 2018 RASS study.

**The majority of single family homes statewide meet key criteria for installing a HPWH.** Among the 140 homes at which virtual audits were conducted, four (2%) already had heat pump water heaters installed and are therefore excluded from the HPWH suitability analysis below. Table 34 shows the results of the remaining homes evaluated by the criteria for installing a HPWH. Almost all homes had sufficient ceiling height — the most difficult barrier to overcome unless the water heater is moved to a different location. The majority (91%) were in locations that stayed above a temperature required for HPWH to work effectively, and 79% had sufficient space around

[^19]: The extent to which access to a drain is a barrier varies case to case. In most cases, a condensate pump can be installed with relative ease. However, in some cases there may not be a nearby outlet to install the pump, which would require an electrician and increase costs.

[PDF p. 48 | printed p. 48]

the water heater.[^20] If the current water heater location was not suitable, the survey found that most single-family homes have basements (91%), a common location for HPWH installations. While not a significant barrier to installing a HPWH, the presence of a drain in the basement or utility room can help mitigate condensate produced by the HPWH. ## 7.2 COOLING - NEWLY INSTALLED EQUIPMENT

**Connecticut households are upgrading cooling systems frequently.** One-third of households (32%) reported installing new cooling equipment in the past four years. ### A.2.4 Heat Pump Suitability

During virtual audits, technicians collected data at the room level to assess the suitability of installing heat pumps in those spaces. One of the first considerations in assessing a home’s suitability for heat pumps was the presence of a forced air duct system. In cases where ducts were present, the assessment focused more on the characteristics of the whole home as opposed to the individual room level as described below. A ducted ASHP would be the more logical choice for HP installation in these cases, which would serve the whole home. For homes without duct systems, room level data was used to make the assessment. For each room in the home, the technician recorded the room type, approximate square footage,[^37] and the heating and/or cooling equipment that served the room.

Technicians also asked qualitative questions of the homeowner regarding areas or rooms within the home that were less comfortable than others (i.e., too cold in the winter or too hot in the summer) or if the room was heated by delivered fuels (oil or propane). Based on the data collected on each room, the team used a formula to assign a heat pump suitability tier ranking ranging from 1 to 5, with 1 indicating a heat pump is already installed in that room and a 5 indicating the room is an excellent candidate for a heat pump installation. These tiers emulate a 1-to-5-star rating system to determine how compelling a case each room presents in its suitability for a heat pump in non-ducted homes. Table 71 describes the factors input into the ranking formula and what effect they have on a room’s tier ranking.

### A.2.5 Heat Pump Water Heater Suitability

During the virtual audits, technicians assessed the suitability of installing a heat pump water heater. This assessment was based on the space in which the current water heater was located, typically a basement, utility room, or closet. Factors included in the assessment were:

- **The presence of a drain nearby:** The heat pump component naturally dehumidifies air and collects condensation that needs to be drained. The presence of a drain was assessed visually over the virtual audit, and further confirmed by asking the homeowner whether they had a sump pump or washing machine in the same room as the water heater.
- **Room stays warm in the winter:** Heat pump water heaters work most effectively and efficiently when the surrounding air is a reasonable temperature in all seasons. A minimum temperature of 40°F is typically recommended. This was assessed by asking the homeowner during virtual audits and confirmed by observing the characteristics of the room (actively conditioned, passively heated through uninsulated pipes, presence of insulation on walls or frame floor, etc.).
- **Sufficient space:** Heat pump water heaters need a certain volume of air to intake and exhaust to pull in heat; 750 cubic feet is typically recommended. This was assessed through estimates on dimensions given by the homeowner similar to the room level heat pump assessment. Where possible, dimensions were confirmed through assessor database floor layouts.
- **Sufficient ceiling height:** Due to the heat pump component typically being mounted on top of the storage tank, these units are often taller than typical storage water heaters. Ceiling height was estimated by the homeowner and confirmed for reasonableness

## A.5 ADJUSTMENT FACTORS

The multi-modal survey guides respondents through self-reporting appliances and equipment, but it can still be difficult for some respondents to distinguish between types of equipment (e.g., furnaces and boilers). For the fraction of measures where we do not have photo evidence confirming the equipment type, we applied adjustment factors to those self-reported responses to correct for presumed mischaracterization.

The following approach was developed to create adjustment factors:

1. Calculate a net error rate using validated information from the virtual audits as well as self-audit photos for key equipment types.
2. Develop adjustment factors based on the net error rate.
3. Apply the adjustment factor to unverified self-reports of the same equipment on the customer survey to correct for self-report errors.

Definitions:

- A false positive for an equipment type occurred when a respondent incorrectly reported the presence of that equipment in their home – i.e., they reported on the survey that they

[PDF p. 82 | printed p. 82]

have this equipment in their homes but the virtual audit data or verified photos indicate something else.

- A false negative for an equipment type occurred when a respondent incorrectly reported the absence of that equipment in their home – i.e., they reported on the survey that they have some other type of equipment in their homes but the virtual audit data or verified photos indicate that they actually do have this equipment.
- A true positive for a given type of equipment type occurred when a respondent correctly reported the presence of that equipment in their home – i.e., they reported on the survey that they have this equipment in their homes and the virtual audit data or verified photos confirm it.
- Verified responses are the sum of false positive responses, false negative responses, and true positive responses.

Calculations:

$$\%\ \text{False Positive} = \frac{\text{Number of false positive responses}}{\text{Number of all verified responses}} \times 100\%$$

$$\%\ \text{False Negative} = \frac{\text{Number of false negative responses}}{\text{Number of all verified responses}} \times 100\%$$

$$\text{Adjustment factor} = 100\% - \%\ \text{False Positive} + \%\ \text{False Negative}$$

Using this approach, adjustment factors were developed for measures with photo verification collected through the survey or virtual audits. As shown in Table 75, some heating and cooling measures have adjustment factors for penetration (the presence of the measure in the household was incorrect or equipment type or fuel was mischaracterized). An adjustment factor less than 1.0 indicates that false positives exceed false negatives for that equipment, i.e., saturation and penetration figures among unverified survey responses is overstated due to self-report errors for that equipment.

To ensure precision-related robustness, these adjustment factors were applied only when there are at least ten photo/virtual audit cases, and an absolute precision criterion of 90/25 is met for the estimate from verified cases.

This section defines terms and systems used throughout the study.

- **Single-family** refers to single-family detached or standalone houses, including mobile homes, attached homes that share a wall with another home (e.g., townhomes), and small multifamily homes containing two to four units.
- **Multifamily** refers to homes in buildings with five or more units.
- **Low-income** includes respondents eligible for LIHEAP (60% of state median income) based on their reported household occupancy and 2023 income.
- **Moderate-income** includes respondents whose household incomes are greater than 60% of state median income (LIHEAP-eligible) but less than or equal to 80% state median income based on their household occupancy.
