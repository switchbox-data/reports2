## {5} IV. COST OF SERVICE STUDY

{6} **Q** **What is the purpose of a COSS?**
{7} A A COSS estimates how much of the utility’s revenue requirement each customer class
{8} causes. It assigns that revenue requirement to rate classes based on the costs each class
{9} imposes on the system.
{10} **Q** **How is a COSS performed?**
{11} A An allocated cost of service (“ACOS”) study typically follows three steps. First costs are
{12} functionalized, which means that the utility plant and expenses are separated according to
{13} the primary functions served. Second, the functionalized rate base and operating costs are
{14} classified by their main cost driver, primarily demand, energy, or customer. Finally, the
{15} costs are either directly assigned to customers or allocated among customer classes using
{16} allocation factors based on their classification. Allocation factors primarily consist of
{17} various measures of peak demand, energy usage, or the number of customers.
{18} **Q** **How do analysts determine the appropriate approaches to cost classification and**
{19} **allocation?**
{20} A When selecting classification factors or allocators, the goal is to fairly allocate costs
{21} among different customer classes based on cost causation. Cost causation reflects the
{22} notion that the customer or set of customers that caused a cost should pay for the cost. To

[PDF p. 10 | printed p. 8]

{1} determine cost causation, analysts often rely on economic theory and power system
{2} engineering considerations. These judgments are rarely clear-cut. Reasonable analysts
{3} can and do reach different conclusions about what drives a given cost.
{4} **Q** **How should the Authority use a COSS in this proceeding?**
{5} A The Authority should exercise judgement when using a COSS because it is an imprecise
{6} tool. The analyst makes many subjective choices about functionalization, classification,
{7} and allocation, and each one can change the results substantially. A COSS should
{8} therefore be one of several things that guide revenue allocation and rate design, not the
{9} deciding factor.
{10} **Q** **In your view, has the Company selected appropriate ACOS methods?**
{11} A No. I have three primary concerns with the Company’s ACOS methods:
{12} 1) The Company’s meter classification does not reflect AMI cost causation;
{13} 2) The Company classifies portions of the distribution system as partially “customer-
{14} related” based on a flawed minimum system methodology; and
{15} 3) The Company’s demand allocation method for distribution system infrastructure
{16} does not reflect localized variance in cost causation.

{4} **Q** **Why do you propose time-differentiating supply and transmission costs as well as**

{7} and better reflect cost causation. Customers respond to the price signals presented by the
{8} total rates, rather than to any specific rate component. Since distribution only represents a
{9} portion of customers’ bills, the price signal provided by time-differentiated distribution
{10} rates will be diluted if transmission and supply charges continue to be flat. Additionally,
{11} distribution, transmission, and supply costs are all disproportionately driven by peak
{12} demand. Following cost causation principles, therefore, requires that all rate components
{13} be differentiated.

## {1} VI. ECONOMIC DEVELOPMENT RATE PROPOSAL

{2} **Q** **Please summarize Eversource’s proposed Economic Development Rate.**
{3} A Eversource proposes an EDR rider that would establish discounted rates for incremental
{4} load growth of at least 1 MW from new and existing large commercial and industrial
{5} (Rate 57 and Rate 58) customers.[^69] The EDR is structured as a percentage discount that
{6} applies to the customer’s incremental distribution demand and energy charges and that
{7} declines over a 5-year term, with the specific discount level determined through direct
{8} negotiation with the customer.[^70] Additionally, the discount is anchored to the customer’s
{9} marginal cost of service and subject to a cap tied to the incremental distribution revenues
{10} from the new load, and the customer is still responsible for interconnection, system
{11} upgrade, and make-ready costs.[^71]
{12} **Q** **Do you have concerns with the Company’s proposed EDR?**
{13} A Yes. While a well-designed EDR with strong safeguards around ratepayer impacts can
{14} help incentivize incremental load that helps both support economic development and
{15} system utilization, I have three concerns with the Company’s proposal. First, the proposal
{16} lacks sufficient detail on safeguards against the potential impacts of hyperscale data
{17} center load. Second, the rate does not sufficiently encourage customers on the EDR to
{18} utilize the system in an efficient manner. Third, the Company’s proposed approach to
{19} determining the discount level would add administrative complexity and regulatory
{20} burden, and it is not transparent. For these reasons, I recommend that the Authority reject
{21} Eversource’s proposed EDR and instead approve a modified EDR that I describe below.

### {4} _incremental load to support more efficient system utilization_

{5} **Q** **Why is it important for EDR customers to receive price signals that encourage load**
{6} **flexibility?**
{7} A New load being added to the system can only improve system efficiency if the revenues
{8} the utility receives from that new load are greater than its marginal cost to serve. This
{9} depends on the customer’s load shape: new load added primarily to peak hours may
{10} necessitate new investments in distribution capacity to accommodate higher peak loads as
{11} well as increase the utility’s cost responsibility for transmission and generation. While
{12} the Company proposes to ensure that revenues collected from the customer are greater
{13} than their marginal cost to serve, this determination is only based on the Company’s
{14} marginal distribution cost of service study, which does not account for impacts on
{15} transmission and supply costs.[^79] These costs impact all ratepayers and could diminish the
{16} benefits from the increased sales. On the other hand, the benefits of new load to the
{17} system and existing ratepayers are maximized when that new load is added primarily to
{18} off-peak hours, when the grid can absorb additional load with existing system capacity
{19} and when wholesale market prices are low. Therefore, to ensure that new EDR load can
{20} improve system efficiency, EDR customers should receive price signals that encourage
{21} off-peak usage and discourage on-peak usage. Time-varying rates can provide these price
{22} signals.
