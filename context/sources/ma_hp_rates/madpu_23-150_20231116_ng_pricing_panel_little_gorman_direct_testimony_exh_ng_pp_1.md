## III. Allocated Cost of Service Study

**Q. What is an ACOSS, and how is it prepared?**

A. The Company’s filing is based on its investments and costs incurred to provide distribution service to its customers. An ACOSS directly assigns or allocates each element of the revenue requirement, including plant and other investments, operating expenses,

<!-- PDF page 10 (printed page 6 of 51) -->

depreciation expense, and taxes, among the rate classes, to determine the costs of providing distribution service to each rate class. Each element of the total revenue requirement is analyzed and assigned to or allocated among the rate classes, so the utility can establish rates that – subject to assumptions such as kWh delivery volume, peak kilowatt (“kW”) demands, and the number of customers – provide it with a fair opportunity to recover its costs and to earn an appropriate return on its investments.

A three-step process is traditionally used to analyze each element of the revenue requirement. The first step is functionalization of each element. For the Company, these functions are Primary Distribution, Secondary Distribution, and Billing. The second step is classification of each functionalized cost element as Demand, Energy, or Customer. The final step, class allocation, is the allocation of each functionalized, classified cost element among the rate classes. The results of the ACOSS – that is, the revenue needed from each rate class to recover the costs incurred to serve that class and to provide an appropriate return on the assets needed to serve it – were compared to the revenue at current rates, and this information was used for guidance in designing the distribution rates proposed in this proceeding.

**Q. Did the Company use the same methodology in preparing the ACOSS as in its prior rate cases, D.P.U. 09-39, D.P.U. 15-155, and D.P.U. 18-150?**

A. Yes. The same three-step methodology was used to prepare the ACOSS in this case as in D.P.U. 09-39, D.P.U. 15-155, and D.P.U. 18-150. This three-step ACOSS methodology has been accepted by the Department, and all other regulators with which Mr. Gorman is

**Q. What are “external allocators” and “internal allocators”?**

A. Two types of allocation bases, or “allocators,” are used in performing an allocated cost of service study: external allocators and internal allocators. “External allocators” are developed in special studies derived from the utility’s accounting, operating, and other records. For example, the allocator “12 CP” measures each class’s contribution to the

<!-- PDF page 17 (printed page 13 of 51) -->

monthly Coincident Peak demand on the system for 12 months; the value of the allocator for each class is the class’s average monthly contribution applied to monthly average peaks. Other examples of external allocators are the number of customers in each rate class, class Non-Coincident Peak demands, the annual peak demand for each class, installed cost of a typical meter, and historical bad debt experience for each rate class.

Internal allocators are developed based on some combination of external allocators and other internal allocators. For example, the internal allocator for property insurance costs is based on plant investment; therefore, it is necessary to allocate plant investment before property insurance costs can be allocated. Both external and internal allocators are used in each of the functionalization, classification, and allocation steps.

**Q. How did you determine the appropriate allocators for functionalizing, classifying, and allocating the components of the revenue requirement?**

A. Selection of the appropriate allocator for functionalizing, classifying, and allocating each component of the revenue requirement was based on careful consideration of cost causality, as well as prior Company methodology, Department precedent, and utility practice as stated in the NARUC Manual. Cost causality means the cause-and-effect relationships between customer requirements and load profiles on one hand, and the costs incurred to serve those requirements on the other hand.

**Q. What do the Demand allocators represent?**

<!-- PDF page 18 (printed page 14 of 51) -->

“NCAP_at_Sec;” and “NCP_at_Step.” These Demand allocators are developed on Exhibit NG-PP-3O, Exhibit NG-PP-3P, and Exhibit NG-PP-3Q, respectively. These allocators are calculated in the same manner as in cases D.P.U. 09-39, D.P.U. 15-155, and D.P.U. 18-150, with one modification. In D.P.U. 09-39, D.P.U. 15-155, and D.P.U. 18-150 the Demand allocators in each case were calculated using data from a single year, namely, the 12 months ending December 2008, June 2015, and December 2017, respectively. In this case, the Company examined data for each of the years ending March 2015 through March 2023, and the demand allocators in this case reflect the averages over that period. The 12CP (“Monthly Annual Peaks”) allocator reflects each class’s average contribution to the average monthly system peaks over that period. For example, the average contribution to April system peak multiplied by the average April peak. The NCP (“Class Non-Coincident Peaks”) allocator reflects each class’s average contribution to the annual sum of non-coincident peaks over that period.

The Company believes that using averages developed over time provides better information for use in the ACOSS than using a single year. A single year can have isolated events that cause cost shifts. In allocating costs incurred over decades and in developing rates that may be in place for years, a broad sample of data is more appropriate. The use of data that reflects a longer period promotes rate stability and mitigates abrupt changes. Of course, it is necessary to examine the results each time to determine if new usage patterns are emerging.

<!-- PDF page 19 (printed page 15 of 51) -->

For example, usage patterns were affected by the COVID-19 pandemic. Some of the changes may be permanent and some may be transient, so over the next several rate cases, the data should be examined to determine the appropriate usage patterns for use in allocation.

**Q. What is the functionalization step of an ACOSS?**

A. In the functionalization step, costs are separated by the utility’s basic service functions. For the Company, these are Primary distribution, Secondary distribution, and Billing. There are separate functions for Primary distribution and Secondary distribution to enable appropriate allocation between customers taking service at primary voltages and customers taking service at secondary voltages. The Billing function includes measuring, billing, and collecting for the services the Company provides, including customer support.

**Q. How were assets and costs allocated among the functions?**

A. In general, functionalization follows costs as recorded in the FERC Uniform System of Accounts. However, some accounts are functionalized to more than one function. For example, Overhead Conductors and Devices (Account 365) and Underground Conductors and Devices (Account 367) were functionalized among primary distribution and secondary distribution based on a special study. In this study, miles of conductors were separated by voltage level, between primary (including sub-transmission) and secondary conductors, and costs were allocated based on the voltage level splits. The study is presented in Exhibit NG-PP-3S, Functional Splits.

**Q. What is the classification step of an ACOSS?**

A. In the classification step, the previously functionalized accounts are separated into Customer, Energy, or Demand, according to the system design or operating characteristics that cause them to be incurred.

<!-- PDF page 21 (printed page 17 of 51) -->

Customer-related costs are incurred to attach a customer to the distribution system, to meter usage, and to maintain both these distribution assets and the customer’s account. These costs are primarily a function of the number of customers served and will be incurred regardless of the customer’s peak demand or total usage. These costs typically do not vary with usage or load profile. These costs include capital costs associated with the customer portion of the distribution system, services, and meters; and operating costs such as customer service, field service, billing, and accounting.

Energy-related costs vary with the electricity sold to or delivered to customers.

Demand- or capacity-related costs are associated with plant that is designed, constructed, and operated to meet customer maximum demand (e.g., system peak demands, class peak demands, local peak demands).

**Q. How were assets and costs classified as Customer, Energy, and Demand?**

A. All assets and costs in the Primary distribution function are classified as Demand-related, and all assets and costs in the Billing function are classified as Customer-related. The Secondary distribution function includes Demand-related elements, Customer-related elements, and elements that are allocated between Demand and Customer. The classification step is shown in Exhibit NG-PP-2E.

Services are classified as Customer-related because there is a direct relationship between Services and the number of customers. Assets that are upstream of Services, such as those

<!-- PDF page 22 (printed page 18 of 51) -->

whose purpose is to deliver energy to the service drop, are related to system peak demand or to local-area peak demand and are classified as Demand-related. Costs are classified in the same manner as the assets to which they relate.

**Q. Are any other methods sometimes used for classification?**

A. Yes. A minimum system study is sometimes used to classify the following distribution assets: Poles, Towers, and Fixtures; Overhead Conductors and Devices; Underground Conduits; Underground Conductors and Devices; and Line Transformers. The minimum system study recognizes that these assets have a dual purpose – both to connect customers to the system and to meet peak demands – and that a utility’s investment in these assets is affected by both purposes. However, the Company has not performed a minimum system study in its last four distribution rate cases, or more, and they are not routinely performed in Massachusetts. Therefore, the Company did not perform a minimum system study for this ACOSS.

**Q. What is the class allocation step of the ACOSS?**

A. In the class allocation step, the functionalized, classified costs are allocated among the rate classes based on causal relationships. These relationships are determined by analyzing the Company’s system design and operations, its accounting records, and its system and customer load data. Based on those analyses, direct assignments of costs, as well as cost allocators, can be chosen for each asset and cost.

## IV. Proposed Rate Design

**Q. What were the guiding principles in designing the rates for each rate class?**

A. The Company’s guiding principles for rate design are listed below. These are based on the concepts put forth in _Principles of Public Utility Rates_, cited above:

- To produce the target revenue for each rate class as determined in the revenue allocation process;

## VII. Timing of Proposed Rate Changes

**Q. When is the Company proposing the changes to its base distribution rates, BSACFs, and SFRFs will take effect?**

A. Based on the date of the Company’s filing in this case, the Department’s statutory suspension period will run from December 1, 2023 through September 30, 2024, with the effective date of new rates occurring on October 1, 2024. The Company is requesting that the Department allow the rate change to be legally effective as of October 1, 2024, upon the issuance of the final decision in this case but allow the Company to implement/bill those rates as of November 1, 2024.

The Company would have to hold bills as of October 1, 2024 to complete the compliance phase of the rate implementation. The Department’s compliance phase typically consumes

<!-- PDF page 53 (printed page 49 of 51) -->

at least three weeks after the issuance of the rate case order. As companies have unbundled their rates, proposals become more comprehensive and can affect more than just base distribution rates. The time it takes for the changes to the exhibits and tariffs required by the Department’s order to be approved – including the utility reviewing the Department’s order and requisite changes, preparing and submitting the initial compliance filing by the utility, review of the compliance filing by the Department and possible revision by the utility, and final approval by the Department – can be extensive. During this time the Company would be compelled to hold several billing cycles so that new rates are billed effective as of the first of the month. In routine circumstances, holding customer bills for a three-week period creates a material level of customer disruption, which the Company experienced in the months following the issuance of the Department’s order in D.P.U. 15-155. In this case, the holding of bills in October to implement new rates consistent with the Department’s decision would mean that customers will be receiving two bills at virtually the same time. This would be certain to create substantial customer confusion and concern that is unreasonable to impose on customers.

The Department has allowed National Grid to delay a change in base distribution rates in D.P.U. 17-170, D.P.U. 18-150, and D.P.U. 20-120. As a result, National Grid did not have to hold bills and was able release November bills on schedule, avoiding significant efforts by Company personnel and what could have been extreme customer confusion and dissatisfaction as customers with meter read dates in billing cycles early in the billing month would receive their October bill followed almost immediately by their November

<!-- PDF page 54 (printed page 50 of 51) -->

bill. As a result, the rate changes resulting from D.P.U. 17-170, D.P.U. 18-150, and D.P.U. 20-120 were seamless to customers. Therefore, to avoid this situation for customers, the Company is proposing to delay the implementation of new rates to November 1, 2024, while accounting for the fact that the new rates approved by the Department will be legally effective October 1, 2024.

**Q. How is the Company proposing to implement the new base distribution rates as of November 1, 2024, while accounting for the fact that the rates will be legally effective on October 1, 2024?**

A. In its first RDM filing subsequent to an approved base distribution rate increase in this proceeding (January 2025), the Company will reconcile actual base distribution revenue billed against its approved Annual Target Revenues (“ATR”) for calendar year 2024. In its RDM reconciliation, the Company would propose to reflect an increase in the monthly ATR amount effective October 2024, such that any loss of billed revenue due to the delayed implementation and billing of new base distribution rates on November 1, 2024 would be recovered from all customers through the Company’s RDM factors effective March 1, 2025.
