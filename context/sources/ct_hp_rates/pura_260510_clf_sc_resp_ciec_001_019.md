**Question CIEC-2:**
Please refer to Velez Direct Testimony at page 95, lines 1-16, and page 98, lines 10-18.

a. Please confirm that Rate HP is not designed to be revenue neutral with respect to the residential
class as a whole.

b. Please identify who Mr. Velez contends should bear any reduction in distribution and
transmission revenue resulting from Rate HP (e.g., other residential customers, non-residential
customers, or the Company’s shareholders), and explain the basis for that position.

c. Does Mr. Velez recommend that any portion of the revenue reduction resulting from Rate HP be
recovered from customers outside the residential class? If so, identify each class and the amount.
If not, explain how his recommendations would prevent that result.

d. In developing his recommendations, did Mr. Velez consider how any revenue reduction resulting
from Rate HP would be recovered between rate cases through the Company’s reconciling
mechanisms? If so, describe that consideration and provide any analysis. If not, explain why not.

**Response:**

a. The term “revenue neutral” is used in many different ways, so I define it precisely here to clarify what my
Rate HP proposal does and does not do.
Rate HP is not designed to be revenue neutral with respect to the residential class as a whole, in the
following sense: if all customers currently on other residential rate schedules were switched to Rate HP
— whether or not they had a heat pump — the Company would experience a revenue shortfall.
Rate HP is also not designed to be revenue neutral with respect to residential heat pump customers as a
whole, relative to what they would pay under Rate 1: under Rate 1, a heat pump customer overpays by
$1,040 a year, on average, and Rate HP is designed specifically to remove this overcharge, while still
charging these customers for any additional T&D costs caused by their heat pump.

b. From other residential customers.
My complete proposal is designed to be revenue neutral with respect to the residential class as a whole, in
the sense that any revenue reduction that occurs when existing heat pump customers who are currently on
Rate 1 enroll in Rate HP would be recouped from the residential customers left on Rate 1, by slightly
increasing Rate 1’s charges. (As shown in CIEC-1(a), any new heat pump customers enrolled in Rate HP
at install time would produce more revenue after the switch, not less.)
In other words, the basis for my position that other residential customers should bear any revenue
reduction resulting from existing heat pump customers joining Rate HP is this: the Company should have
been collecting this revenue from non-heat pump customers in the first place, not from heat pump
customers. Rate 1 unfairly shifts costs from non-heat pump customers to heat pump customers; Rate HP

[PDF p. 8 | printed p. 2]

unwinds this significant and growing cross-subsidy within the residential class to improve intra-class
equity.1

c. No. I recommend that no portion be recovered from customers outside the residential class, because Rate
HP addresses an intra-class equity problem.
My recommendations prevent that result by proposing Rate HP as a residential rate schedule, and
changing how the residential class’s allocated costs are split between Rate 1 and Rate HP, not the cost
allocation across classes. So when rates are set, any revenue Rate HP gives up is recovered by slightly
raising Rate 1’s charges. Nothing moves to other classes. (Reconciling mechanisms work slightly
differently; see the following question.)

d. I considered this but did not address it in my testimony. I do so here. The two reconciling mechanisms
relevant to Rate HP are the Revenue Decoupling Mechanism (RDM) for distribution and the
Transmission Adjustment Clause (TAC) for transmission. Rate HP does not change any other riders —
customers on Rate HP pay the same SBC, CTA, FMCC, and SCRAF rates as Rate 1 — so the remaining
reconciling mechanisms are unaffected.2
How the RDM and TAC work. Between rate cases, the RDM reconciles the Company’s actual
distribution revenue against the fixed revenue target approved in this proceeding.3 If actual revenue falls
short of the target — because customers used less electricity than forecast, or, in this case, because
customers switched to a rate schedule that lowered the per-kWh distribution charges applied to their same
usage — the RDM adds a surcharge in the following year to recover the difference. If actual revenue
exceeds the target, the RDM credits the difference back. The surcharge or credit is set as a flat ¢/kWh and
applied to every rate class.4 The TAC works similarly for transmission. Each year, it recovers the
Company's projected transmission costs (external charges from ISO New England (ISO-NE) that the
Company passes through) plus the prior year's under- or over-recovery, and allocates the total among
classes by each class's demand at the Company's twelve monthly system peaks rather than by kWh.5
New heat pump customers. When a customer installs a heat pump and enrolls in Rate HP, their
electricity consumption rises, with winter kWh roughly tripling. By design, even at Rate HP’s lower
winter distribution and transmission rates, the volume increase means the Company collects more total
