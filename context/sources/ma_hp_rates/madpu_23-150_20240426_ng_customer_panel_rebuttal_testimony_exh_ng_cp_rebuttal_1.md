## III. LOW-INCOME CUSTOMER PROPOSALS

**Q. Please summarize your understanding of Mr. Nelson and Ms. Palmer’s recommendations to the Department regarding the Company’s Low-Income Customer Proposals.**

A. Mr. Nelson and Ms. Palmer recommend that the Company remove the minimum 32 percent discount for Rate R-2 customers with the highest qualifying incomes, to align the energy burden outcomes for customers in the highest-income tier with the energy burden outcomes for the other income tiers. They state that under the Company’s proposal, some customers may still face energy burdens above the target, while others with the highest qualifying incomes may receive a discount rate that will result in an energy burden lower than the Company’s target level (Exh. AG-RNCP-1, at 49). Mr. Nelson and Ms. Palmer argue that the Company should divide the income tier between 200 percent of the Federal Poverty Level (“FPL”) to 60 percent of the State Median Income (“SMI”) into two separate tiers, each with an energy burden range aligned with the other tiers (id. at 51). Additionally, they state that (1) it is problematic to assume all participants receive Low-Income Home Energy Assistance Program (“LIHEAP”) payments when significant numbers of households do not receive such payments; and (2) it is problematic to calculate the discount based on 600 kilowatt-hour (“kWh”) monthly usage when significant numbers of eligible ratepayers’ usage do not align with that assumed usage amount (id. at 54). On the assumption of 600 kWh monthly consumption, Mr. Nelson and Ms. Palmer include circumstances in which

<!-- PDF page 7 (printed page 5 of 60) -->

they argued customers would consume much more or less than the average on which the Company based its discounts and, as a result, they believed the Company would fail to achieve the stated goal of its proposed discount (id. at 59-60). In their opinion, the Company could scale the discount levels based on a few average monthly usage levels so that Rate R-2 customers who consume significantly above or below 600 kWh per month could come closer to the target energy burden (id. at 60).

**Q. Do you agree with the suggestions of Mr. Nelson and Ms. Palmer?**

A. No. The Company designed its proposal to implement a multi-tiered low-income bill discount for Rate R-2 customers (“Low-Income Discount Rate proposal”) using a combination of factors that appropriately balances competing policy and customer interests and can be reasonably implemented by the Company. The Company informed this analysis by stakeholder engagement prior to the rate case filing, to ensure the Low-Income Discount Rate proposal best reflects the diverse needs of a range of stakeholders, including Rate R-2 customers, low-income advocate groups, and other customers.

The Company’s Low-Income Discount Rate proposal represents a more complex, yet equitable, approach to reducing energy burden and advancing equity than the flat discount level that is currently in place. As the Company’s testimony, exhibits, and responses to information requests indicate, the Company designed the proposed Low-Income Discount Rate tiers and discount levels to help achieve a target level of energy burden for representative customers, based on average monthly usage.

<!-- PDF page 8 (printed page 6 of 60) -->

Further, the Company considered the overall level of program cost for the tiered system, recognizing that all customers pay for the assistance. The Company believes that increasing assistance and lowering the energy burden for eligible customers should not significantly increase the energy burden for all other customers – including for residential customers just slightly above the eligibility threshold. This led to the following design features for the Low-Income Discount Rate proposal:

(1) The proposal to maintain a minimum 32 percent discount rate for Rate R-2 customers was made after significant discussion with and feedback from low-income stakeholders, who strongly encouraged the Company to avoid reducing any currently-eligible Rate R-2 customer’s level of benefits. These stakeholders indicated that customers in the income bracket in question (200 percent of FPL up to 60 percent of SMI) often are ineligible for other types of assistance, making the R-2 rate an important source of assistance to maintain for these customers. While this design feature comes at a higher cost (as shown in Attachment AG-19-4 to the Company’s response to Information Request AG-19-4), preserving the 32 percent minimum would avoid increasing the level of energy burden currently experienced by any customers, and also facilitate smoother implementation of the new tiered discount with the Company’s customer base. In addition, preserving the 32 percent minimum discount removes the need to divide the income tier between 200 percent of the Federal Poverty Level (“FPL”) to 60 percent of the

<!-- PDF page 9 (printed page 7 of 60) -->

State Median Income (“SMI”) into two separate tiers, as both tiers will just receive the 32 percent minimum discount. (2) The proposal to exclude LIHEAP for discount rate calculations helps reduce cost of the Low-Income Discount Rate program, making it more affordable to more customers. This proposal was made based on the following premises:

i. The inclusion of LIHEAP was based on the Company’s affiliate in New York, which was instructed by the New York Public Service Commission to include the New York version of LIHEAP payments in the Discount Payment calculations; and ii. The Company’s broader program for increased education and outreach is designed to help enroll as many customers as possible onto the LIHEAP program. The Company does not support offering additional, different levels of R2 assistance based on differing levels of monthly electricity consumption, , as recommended by Mr. Nelson and Ms. Palmer, due to: (i) the complexity of constructing and implementing such a system, including complexity and cost for the Company’s billing system; (ii) the reduced incentive it would provide to lower electric usage; and (iii) the complexity of communicating such a system to customers. For example, if the discount rate tiers were different for 500 to 599 kWh per month customers and 600 to 699 kWh per month customers, the percentage discount rate system likely would lead to a customer using 599 kWh per month paying more than a customer using 600 kWh in a month. The Company’s Low-Income Discount Rate proposal in this proceeding reflects design choices meant to achieve a balancing of factors.

**Q. What other recommendations did Mr. Nelson and Ms. Palmer make to the Department?**

A. Mr. Nelson and Ms. Palmer recommend that the Company discuss the feasibility of verifying actual LIHEAP receipt amounts. They encourage the Department and the Company to consider solutions in D.P.U. 24-15 for addressing imprecision in the Company’s LIHEAP assumption (Exh. AG-RNCP-1, at 58). They state it is problematic to assume all participants receive LIHEAP payments when significant numbers of households do not receive such payments (id.).

**Q. Do you agree with this suggestion of Mr. Nelson and Ms. Palmer?**

A. The Company addressed the issue of including LIHEAP payments in the above response to Mr. Nelson and Ms. Palmer’s first recommendation. The Company believes that it is appropriate to reflect the value of the LIHEAP payment in its estimation of low-income residential customers’ energy burdens, rather than excluding LIHEAP and setting the level of the R2 discount sufficiently high to achieve a target level of energy burden for every customer when considered in isolation. The R2 rate is an important tool but not the only tool available to the Commonwealth’s policymakers to help reduce energy burden and improve equity, and the Company maintains that LIHEAP payments should be incorporated in the design of the tiered R2 structure. The Company designed its LIHEAP payment estimates as one of many different data points in the design process, intended to provide a balancing of factors, including balancing the needs of Rate R-2 customers with

<!-- PDF page 11 (printed page 9 of 60) -->

the cost borne by all other customers who do not receive the discount (including commercial customers).

**Q. Please summarize your understanding of Ms. Henderson’s recommendations to the Department regarding the income verification procedures for the Company’s Low- Income Discount Rate proposal.**

A. Ms. Henderson supports the Company’s three proposed Low-Income Discount Rate income verification procedures, with additional recommendations. Ms. Henderson supports the Company’s proposal to maintain an income verification pathway in which household level gross annual income is collected and verified (Exh. AG-CEH-1, at 13). Ms. Henderson also recommends a reduction of paperwork and that the Company should, to the extent possible, link verification procedures for its various income-eligible programs and services to enable eligible income-qualified customers to use one verification procedure to participate in multiple offerings (id. at 14). Ms. Henderson believes the Company should expand data sharing agreements beyond DTA and Community Action Agencies (“CAAs”) (id. at 15), and that the Department should consider the use of customer self-attestation of income, with spot-checks on a portion of customers each year (id. at 16). Ms. Henderson states that the Company has not described the re-certification process or requirements, and she recommends that customers should receive at least two notices via different channels when it is time to re-certify (id. at 18). Energy efficiency programs, including weatherization, air sealing, and energy audits, should continue to prioritize low-income customers (id. at 19). Further, income requirements should be aligned across programs as consistently as possible, and a single-verification process

<!-- PDF page 12 (printed page 10 of 60) -->

should be used to qualify customers for participation in the Company’s various income-based programs (id. at 20).

**Q. Do you agree with Ms. Henderson’s recommendations?**

A. The Company agrees with some, but not all, of Ms. Henderson’s recommendations. The Company agrees with aligning income requirements as consistently as possible – and will continue to use Rate R-2 eligibility for as many other programs as possible (i.e., Arrearage Management Program (“AMP”), Income-Eligible programs, Energy Efficiency (“EE”) programs). Additionally, the Company already has a recertification process in place, as described in its response to Information Request LI-NG-2-18. The Company also intends to establish new data-sharing agreements with as many additional agencies as possible, consistent with Ms. Henderson’s suggestion.

The company has considered but does not recommend the use of customer self-attestation of income because of the potential for erroneous or false attestations resulting in the provision of assistance to customers outside of the statutory eligibility.

**Q. Did Ms. Henderson have any other comments on the Low-Income Discount Rate?**

A. Yes. Ms. Henderson expressed an expectation that the Low-Income Discount Rate maximum income eligibility threshold will be addressed in D.P.U. 24-15, as well as the issue of using Area Median Income for eligibility (Exh. AG-CEH-1, at 21).

**Q. What is your response to these comments on the Low-Income Discount Rate?**

A. What the Department approves in D.P.U. 24-15 and in this rate case should be consistent with each other. The Company is open to exploring the concept of Area Median Income, especially as a tool for determining allocation of marketing, education, and outreach funding. However, the Company does not support using Area Median Income as a means of establishing eligibility thresholds for the following reasons: (1) added complexity and associated administrative burden is associated with a more geographically-differentiated eligibility structure; and (2) Area Median Income is not an appropriate measure of whether a household requires assistance in certain cases. For example, a household earning below 60 percent SMI needs support regardless of whether their area has a higher or lower Area Median Income.

**Q. What comments did Ms. Henderson make regarding the Company’s Marketing, Education and Outreach plan?**

A. Overall, Ms. Henderson is agreeable with the Marketing, Education and Outreach (“MEO”) plan; however, Ms. Henderson has many suggestions to add to the MEO program. First, she suggests aspects of the Company’s proposed MEO plan could be improved, including developing local workforce and pursing equitable community engagement (Exh. AG-CEH-1, at 29-30). Specifically, Ms. Henderson suggests the Company could hire local community members, ambassadors, or organization representatives to support its MEO efforts (id. at 30).

<!-- PDF page 14 (printed page 12 of 60) -->

Second, Ms. Henderson states the Company uses overly complex language in its MEO efforts, such as her opinion that the 2023 discount rate bill insert uses legalese or complex language (id. at 30-31). Third, Ms. Henderson states that the Company should use MEO targeting tactics to direct communications at the target audience (id. at 32). Fourth, she suggests that the Company: (a) test three messages in message testing; (b) use quantitative message testing in addition to qualitative message testing; and (c) get input from Community Based Organizations (“CBO”) (id. at 36-37). Fifth, Ms. Henderson states that the Company should target Low-Income Discount Rate-related MEO to ratepayers in census tracts with the lowest average incomes, and where participation rates in the Low-Income Discount Rate are lower than expected based upon average income in the area (id. at 37-38). Sixth, she suggests that any customer whose disconnection is imminent should be provided with a minimum of two contacts via two different channels and the Company should communicate with any ratepayer who is facing a disconnection about Low-Income Discount Rate enrollment information and arrearage management plans (id. at 39). Seventh, she suggests that the Company does not use the term “low-income” and instead uses “income-eligible” or “income-qualified” (id. at 39-40). Eight, Ms. Henderson suggests that the frequency of reporting on MEO should, at minimum, provide pre- and post-campaign reports on relevant MEO metrics (id. at 42).

**Q. Do you agree with Ms. Henderson’s recommendations on the MEO?**

A. The Company appreciates Ms. Henderson’s recommendations and agrees with some, but not all, of Ms. Henderson’s recommendations regarding the Company’s MEO program.

<!-- PDF page 15 (printed page 13 of 60) -->

The Company commits to strategically targeting income-eligible, low-income customers in the Company’s service territory. Further, as part of established regulatory requirements, the Company issues three notices of disconnection to a customer via the bill (first notice) and mailed letters (second/third notice) before taking further action towards disconnecting a customer. Additionally, the Company provides awareness of imminent disconnection through direct calls, text messages, or emails, depending on the customer’s contact details or channel preference. Prior to issuing disconnection notices, the Company conducts an in-person field visit at the service address. Each method of communication includes program information, such as the low-income discount rate, deferred payment agreements, and the AMP, to help customers avoid disconnection.

Further, the Company plans to partner with community organizations. This effort will include soliciting CBO members to participate in focus groups. However, the Company is not proposing to pay CBO staff as “ambassadors.” Rather, when the Company hires new advocates, in many cases candidates are likely to be individuals from underrepresented communities. The Company agrees with the suggestion for language simplification and agrees with the use of “income-eligible” or “income-qualified” as an alternative to “low-income.” The Company also plans to use MEO targeting tactics to direct communications towards the target audience. For message testing, the Company generally uses A/B testing, and will continue to do so.

**Q. What comments did Ms. Henderson make regarding the Company’s MEO budget?**

A. Ms. Henderson suggests that the $3 million budget for MEO annually is too high. However, Ms. Henderson does not provide adequate support for the amount per new enrolled Low-Income Discount Rate participant (Exh. AG-CEH-1, at 42). Instead, she makes a comparison to a 2019 California program where investor-owned utilities appear to have spent less to implement two utility discount programs (id.).

**Q. Do you agree with Ms. Henderson’s comments on the MEO budget?**

A. No. $3 million is the right amount for the Company’s MEO plan number because the Company has proposed an extensive multi-part MEO strategy, As described throughout this proceeding, focused not only on implementation of a new tiered discount rate structure, but on narrowing the enrollment gaps that exist for all of the Company’s programs available to support low-income customers.

In addition, the Company disagrees with Ms. Henderson’s assertion that its MEO plan is too costly based on her calculation of a cost per net new customer enrolled in the R2 (see Exh. AG-CEH-1, at 42). First, these MEO plans seek to help achieve not only enrollment of net new customers, but also re-enrollment of customers in low-income programs. Many customers struggle with the recertification process, and additional support provided by additional consumer advocates and supplemental communications would help to reduce that churn. Ms. Henderson’s analysis fails to account for these customers in her calculation. More broadly, however, the Company did not put forward an “R2 customer

<!-- PDF page 17 (printed page 15 of 60) -->

acquisition cost” metric for its proposal, for two reasons: 1) because such a metric risks too narrowly interpreting the purpose and intended results of the Company’s proposal; and 2) because a cost-based metric cannot be viewed without also quantifying the significant benefits the Company’s proposal is intended to create, and the Company considers such a quantification of benefits beyond the reasonable scope of this rate proceeding.

These concerns notwithstanding, the Company suggests that the MEO portion of its proposal could be better assessed using a “lifetime value” concept for each customer acquired (e.g., the value of services provided to a new customer once enrolled in Rate R-2). For example, if a customer were to enroll in the R2 discount rate and remain enrolled for a total of 3 years, the "lifetime” customer savings (i.e. for the duration of that customers’ enrollment) could be estimated as follows:

|                               |             |
| ----------------------------- | ----------: |
| R-2 Customer Savings per Year | **$960.00** |
| Years on R-2                  |       **3** |
| Lifetime R-2 Customer Savings |   $2,880.00 |

However, even this methodology is likely to understate the broader value created by the Company’s efforts, because its outreach proposal is aimed at the full suite of income-eligible programs, many of which become accessible to customers only upon enrollment in the Rate R-2. Therefore, the value to the customer from these programs would also need to be accounted for in an actual estimation of “lifetime value.” Of course, such an

<!-- PDF page 18 (printed page 16 of 60) -->

estimation would be very difficult and complex to estimate; for this reason, as stated above the Company does not believe it appropriate at this time to consider Ms. Henderson’s customer acquisition cost as a reasonable method for evaluating its level of spending for its proposed MEO plan.

**Q. Does Ms. Henderson make any other recommendations regarding the Company’s LIDR?**

A. Yes. Ms. Henderson recommends that the Company propose increasing year-over-year Low-Income Discount Rate enrollment targets, set at the census tract level. Ms. Henderson believes that the Company’s proposed enrollment targets are too low, based on the Company’s proposed MEO expenditures of $3 million annually for MEO and $1.25 million for the hiring of 10 incremental full-time equivalent (“FTE”) employees, and enrollment targets in other jurisdictions (Exh. AG-CEH-1, at 44). In 2019, California investor-owned utilities appear to have spent less to implement two utility discount programs (id.). Therefore, Ms. Henderson believes the Company should design Low-Income Discount Rate enrollment targets that increase year-over-year (id. at 47). Further, after Advanced Metering Infrastructure (“AMI”) is deployed, Ms. Henderson believes the Department should direct utilities to collect and use data to identify ratepayers who are likely eligible for Low-Income Discount Rate services and could benefit from participation based upon their consumption (id. at 48).

<!-- PDF page 19 (printed page 17 of 60) -->

**Q. Do you agree with Ms. Henderson’s recommendations on increasing LIDR enrollment targets?**

A. No. Ms. Henderson does not offer any alternative enrollment target levels specifically for the Company. Furthermore, the comparisons to other utilities in California are irrelevant, because Southern California Edison (“SCE”) and Pacific Gas & Electric (“PG&E”) can enroll customers in the statewide CARE program without any eligibility verification, which is significantly different than the Company’s verification requirement. Additionally, those investor-owned utilities have very different rates, customer bases, geographies, and media markets compared to the Company. It is not helpful to an analysis to cite only a single year of data (2019) for SCE and PGE discount rate penetration. A single data point does not demonstrate a trend. Upon further research by the Company, it found that the penetration rate of the California low-income discount rate known as CARE was in the range of 66 percent to 78 percent among California investor-owned utilities in 2006.[^1] The higher penetration rates cited by Ms. Henderson were achieved some 13 years later, and only after California Public Utilities Commission ordered both funding for MEO and a target penetration rate of 90 percent of eligible accounts. The high levels of enrollment of the estimated eligible population among California investor-owned utilities was achieved only after many years of approved funding for MEO, which the Company has never had.

<!-- PDF page 20 (printed page 18 of 60) -->

Further, Ms. Henderson shows no evidence for why LADWP’s metric of having enrolled 9 percent of its entire residential customer base to its low-income discount rate (see Exh. AG-CEH-1, at 44-45) has any relevance to the Company’s proposed Low-Income Discount Rate program, performance incentive mechanism (“PIM”), or enrollment targets. The Company is not a municipal utility comparable to LADWP. The Company’s geography and customer demographics are different from those of LADWP. Further, the Company does not have access to the NREL model mentioned. In short, Ms. Henderson provides no analysis that shows NREL or LADWP is a benchmark with any applicability or relevance outside of LADWP.

The Company is already proposing to significantly increase net new annual enrollment under the proposed plan and funding. Each additional customer enrolled may be harder to reach, and more expensive to acquire, than the previous customer. Thus, each incremental customer enrollment over and above the target is increasingly difficult. Because the target is set to be a measure of net new enrollments year-over-year, it is increasingly difficult to acquire the target number of customers in the later years of the rate plan. Under the PIM proposal, the Company would deliver benefits to customers at the target enrollment level without asking for an any incentive. Even at levels of performance that could result in a penalty, the Company would still deliver benefits to customers.

**Q. What recommendations does Ms. Henderson make regarding the evaluation MEO efforts?**

A. Ms. Henderson recommends that the Company develop a detailed MEO evaluation plan and framework, which should include the campaign goals, aligned with objectives that follow “SMART” metrics. Specifically, Ms. Henderson believes the Company should report on its MEO efforts and Low-Income Discount Rate enrollment rates at the census tract levels, layering these metrics over those census tracts with the lowest average incomes and amongst those where participation rates in the Low-Income Discount Rate are lower than expected based upon average income in the stated geographic area (Exh. AG-CEH-1, at 49). Additionally, Ms. Henderson recommends the Company should retain and compensate the CBOs it proposes to leverage for its Low-Income Discount Rate MEO efforts, and that metrics of success for this workforce development and capacity-building should also be developed. According to Ms. Henderson, the metrics should focus on training or hiring a certain number or percentage of individuals from under-served communities (id.).

**Q. What is your response to Ms. Henderson’s recommendations on evaluating MEO efforts?**

A. The Company already plans to establish Key Performance Indicators (“KPIs”) to monitor and communicate progress towards achieving specific Rate R-2 discount rate enrollment goals. In particular, these new employees will coordinate, facilitate, and oversee all the customer’s interactions with the various departments within the Company that are relevant to the customer’s project or question, such as Customer Connections, Distributed Planning and Asset Management, Complex Project Management, Distributed Generation, Electric Operations, Billing, Energy Efficiency, Clean Transportation, among other potential areas (Company Response to Information Requests DPU-20-2, DPU-20-3, DPU-20-6, DPU-20-7, DPU-20-9).

## V. PROPOSED ELECTRIFICATION PRICING

**Q. Please summarize your understanding of the intervenors’ recommendation with respect to the Company’s Electrification Pricing proposal.**

A. Several intervenors recommend the rejection of the Company’s Electrification Pricing proposal, including the Attorney General in Mr. Nelson and Ms. Palmer’s testimony;

<!-- PDF page 44 (printed page 42 of 60) -->

DOER in Ms. Whited’s testimony; EDF/CLF in Mr. Castigliego’s testimony; and TEC in Mr. Bride and Ms. Nutter’s testimony.

**Q. What is your response to the recommendation to outright reject the Company’s Electrification Pricing proposal?**

A. The Company asks the Department to carefully consider the Company's intent and logic behind the opt-in Electrification Pricing proposal. The Company made the Electrification Pricing proposal in an effort to begin supporting electrification and providing benefits to customers while Advanced Metering Infrastructure (“AMI”) deployment is still underway and while options for innovative rate design remain limited by current metering technology. Notwithstanding, the Company recognizes the benefits of immediate action on rate design may not outweigh the inherent limitations. If the Department does not approve the Company’s Electrification Pricing rate proposal, the Company would fully support a Department decision to hold off taking action on rate design in this rate case and until advanced rate design has been discussed in a separate proceeding and can be more precisely implemented with AMI.

**Q. How did the Company develop the proposed Electrification Pricing option?**

A. The Company has long recognized the cross subsidies created by volumetric delivery rates and the associated negative implications for widespread electrification and achievement of the Commonwealth’s goal of 93 percent in GHG reduction in the buildings sector by 2050. To date, lack of interval metering capability has prevented the Company from proposing advanced rate designs to address these issues. Though this barrier persists, the Company

<!-- PDF page 45 (printed page 43 of 60) -->

decided to propose Electrification Pricing in this rate case in response to increased stakeholder interest in rate design reform and, more specifically, rate design reform as a critical foundation to support widespread electrification.

The Company assessed a range of options that could feasibly be implemented prior to system-wide AMI deployment that would serve as a first step toward movement away from volumetric delivery charges to begin to correct overpayment by customers with higher volumetric usage and lower demands. While the Company believes that delivery rates comprising customer maximum and system-coincident peak demand charges are more cost-reflective, send the most efficient price signals to customers, and would allow customers who electrify to efficiently manage their bills, lack of interval metering prevented the Company from proposing demand-based charges in this rate case. The Company determined that a proposed solution could rely only on a mix of fixed and volumetric delivery charges. The Company also determined that changes to transmission charges and policy-related surcharges and adjustment factors are outside the scope of this rate case. Any pricing option would thus be limited to a fixed base distribution charge.

Accordingly, the Company considered four initial options: (i) propose a pricing option offered only to customers with heat pumps; (ii) propose a rate-adjacent pricing discount only to customers with heat pumps; (iii) propose a pricing option available to all residential customers; and (iv) do nothing in this rate case. The Company quickly ruled out a heat pump-specific pricing option as antithetical to the principles of rate design and the

<!-- PDF page 46 (printed page 44 of 60) -->

Company’s long term rate design strategy, because distribution system costs are not driven by the specific technologies customers use. The Company determined that while reducing operating costs for customers with heat pumps would be a key factor driving action in this rate case, and while reduced eligibility would help limit bill impacts on non-participating customers, it would not help other customers who also overpay relative to their contribution to distribution system costs under volumetric delivery rates. Also, technology-specific rates can be confusing to customers, especially over the long-term.

The Company then considered a rate-adjacent pricing discount for customers with heat pumps. The Company determined that under such a program, heat pump ownership would need to be verified. The Company also questioned which heat pump technologies and configurations would be eligible for the discount. Further, determining the appropriate discount as well as whether the discount should vary based on heat pump technology and configuration, would be contentious and possibly confusing. The Company was concerned about proposing an arbitrary discount that may interact adversely with existing heat pump incentives offered through existing energy efficiency programs. The Company is open to continuing to discuss programmatic solutions to support electrification outside of rates.

The Company was then left with the options of proposing a pricing option open to all residential customers, or doing nothing in this rate case and instead progressing rate design issues through the Department's anticipated proceeding on rate design to prepare for system-wide AMI deployment and interval metering capabilities. The Company ultimately

<!-- PDF page 47 (printed page 45 of 60) -->

decided to propose a rate design that it believes serves as a first step toward movement away from volumetric delivery charges to begin to align revenue from customers with higher volumetric usage, such as heat electrification technologies, with costs. The Company believes that the proposed Electrification Pricing option is a viable and justifiable short-term solution to improve residential rate design and provide customers with options.

The Company agrees with the intervenors that the proposed Electrification Pricing option is not perfect and may pose risks the Department determines to be unacceptable. The Company further agrees that any rate design must be carefully considered with respect to its customer bill impacts. The Company maintains, however, that the proposed Electrification Pricing option reasonably represents a movement toward more cost-reflective and fair rate design compared to volumetric delivery charges.

**Q. Please summarize your understanding of the intervenors’ recommendation to replace the Company’s Electrification Pricing proposal with another electrification offering.**

A. Several intervenors recommend replacing the Company’s Electrification Pricing proposal with another electrification rate offering, including the Attorney General in Mr. Nelson and Ms. Palmer’s testimony; DOER in Ms. Whited’s testimony; and EDF/CLF in Mr. Castigliego’s testimony.

Mr. Nelson and Ms. Palmer suggest that if the Department wishes to approve a rate that would incentivize electrification before AMI is available, a rate with a smaller fixed charge increase would achieve a better balance with competing objectives (Exh. AG-RNCP-1, at

<!-- PDF page 48 (printed page 46 of 60) -->

46). One option would be to remove primary distribution system costs from the fixed charge component and continue to recover these expenses volumetrically, because, they assert, these non-local facility costs should be collected in a time-varying manner after AMI is deployed (id. at 46-47).

Ms. Whited suggests one alternative option is a seasonal rate, whereby distribution costs are recovered based on the seasonality of future peak loads on various distribution system components – such as Central Maine Power’s (“CMP”) Seasonal Heat Pump rate pilot (Exh. DOER-MDW-1, at 19). A second alternative option is an explicit rate discount for customers adopting beneficial electrification technologies – such as Unitil’s proposed heat pump rate in D.P.U. 23-80 (id. at 20).

Mr. Castigliego suggests the Department should reject the Electrification Pricing option and instruct the Company to instead focus in the near-term – until late-2027 when AMI is deployed – on electrification of customers currently using delivered fuels to heat their homes (Exh. EDF-CLF-JRC-1, at 75).

**Q. What is your response to the recommendations to replace the Company’s Electrification Pricing proposal?**

A. If the Department determines that immediate action is necessary to reduce operating costs for heat pump customers, and that a technology-specific solution is required or appropriate, the Company would support a rate-adjacent discount program for customers with heat pumps rather than a technology-specific rate option. A rate discount program should have

<!-- PDF page 49 (printed page 47 of 60) -->

clearly defined policy parameters including the objective of the program, the technologies supported, the program budget, participation limits, the term of the program overall, and the duration for which customers may continue to receive the discount.

The Company considered including secondary distribution costs only in the fixed base distribution charge, with primary distribution costs remaining in the per kWh base distribution charge. The rationale for exploring this option is that secondary distribution costs are considered to be largely, if not entirely, local and, therefore, driven by customer non-coincident peak demand. Typical small changes in residential non-coincident peak demands are unlikely to significantly influence local distribution costs and, therefore, charges that are fixed month-to-month may be considered cost-reflective.

The Company decided to include both secondary and primary distribution costs in the fixed base distribution charge. In the absence of demand metering capability, the Company is not able to design or implement a residential customer non-coincident peak demand charge or to base a class-wide fixed charge on an analysis of residential non-coincident peak demands. As the Attorney General points out, rate design is an exercise in balancing competing objectives and priority considerations. A smaller fixed base distribution charge (than the Company has proposed) would reduce the savings to heat electrification customers below already modest estimates.

The Company hesitates to recommend a seasonal rate similar to CMP’s Heat Pump Rate Pilot for practical reasons. Although the Company’s distribution system is currently

<!-- PDF page 50 (printed page 48 of 60) -->

summer peaking, as heat electrification increases, peaks will shift to winter months. In addition to the Heat Pump Rate Pilot, which is limited to 5,000 customers, CMP offers an Electrification Technology Rate, which is open to all residential customers. The Electrification Technology Rate is the same year-round and is comprised of a high fixed monthly charge and low per kWh charge relative to the default residential rate. The existence of the Electrification Technology Rate provides an option for customers without heat pumps, but who may overpay relative to their contribution to system costs under the default residential rate.

Ms. Whited suggests the Company’s Electrification Pricing proposal would cause a rate increase of more than 4 percent for the majority of residential customers because it would shift recovery away from the minority of high-usage customers (approximately 37 percent) onto the remaining majority of customers (approximately 63 percent) (Exh. **Q. What is your response to the comments that the Company’s Electrification Pricing proposal does not strike an appropriate balance between incentivizing electrification and other rate design objectives?**

Corrective changes to current rate designs are critical to enabling and supporting electrification and the clean energy transition. Accordingly, a framework for determining the appropriate balance of bill impacts (and other policy objectives) with corrective changes to rate design should be discussed in the anticipated Department proceeding on rate design.

**Q. What is your response to the comments that an electrification pricing proposal should be available only to customers with electrification technologies?**

Technology-specific rates can be confusing to customers. Instead, optional rate designs should consider impacts on general usage profiles and the costs they cause the Company to incur, so the Company and customers can make accurate decisions on which rate designs to adopt.

**Q. What is your response to the comments that the Company’s Electrification Pricing proposal is not cost-reflective?**

A. The Company maintains that the proposed Electrification Pricing does reasonably represent a movement toward more cost-reflective rate design compared to volumetric delivery charges, and that as a result, the resulting revenue shift and bill impacts is largely corrective on net. Without historical interval meter data, it is not possible for the Company to estimate the extent to which the revenue shift from Electrification Pricing is corrective. This represents a risk for the Department to consider.

Current volumetric delivery rates are not cost-reflective and create cross-subsidies whereby customers with higher volumetric usage and lower non-coincident and system coincident peak demands overpay relative to their contribution to delivery system costs. Distribution system costs do not vary with usage measured in kWh. Distribution costs are driven by

<!-- PDF page 55 (printed page 53 of 60) -->

the need to connect, meter, and bill customers (customer-related costs) and the need to meet demand (demand-related costs). Existing per kWh distribution charges therefore do not adequately reflect cost causation.. The Company recognizes that the simple single fixed base distribution charge proposed for Electrification Pricing does not capture the portion of base distribution costs driven by customer maximum or system-coincident peak demands. However, given that distribution system costs do not vary with per kWh consumption, and that a portion of demand-related distribution system costs are considered fixed, at least in the short term, the Company believes the fixed monthly base-distribution charge proposed for Electrification Pricing represents movement toward more cost-reflective rate design.

**Q. What is your response to the comments that the Company’s Electrification Pricing proposal does not provide price signals for energy efficiency and conservation?**

A. The price signals sent by volumetric delivery rates do not reflect delivery system costs and therefore do not promote efficient use of the electric delivery system and create uneconomic barriers to adoption and use of electrification technologies. The per kWh Base Distribution Charge accounts for approximately 19 percent of total volumetric charges on the residential bill. Price signals to promote electric energy efficiency and conservation (i.e., reduced volumetric electricity consumption) are appropriately captured in volumetric commodity rates. Additionally, the Electrification Pricing proposal maintains the use of volumetric delivery charges including the Transmission Charge and all surcharges and adjustment factors.

**Q. What is your response to the comments on the issue of the Company’s Electrification Pricing proposal not being offered to Low-Income (Rate R-2) customers?**

A. As discussed in the Company’s response to Information Request LI-NG-1-8-Revised, the proposed Electrification Pricing option is intended to alleviate the uneconomic effects of per kWh delivery rates on residential customer bills. Under the current default Rate R-1 delivery rate, customers face an incremental base distribution charge for every kWh of usage regardless of whether the incremental usage is associated with incremental contribution to distribution system costs. The proposed Electrification Pricing option serves to address this problem by effectively providing a volumetric discount where savings relative to the default rate increase with each incremental kWh of usage. Because the Rate R-2 discount is a percentage of the total bill, it provides the same effect of savings increasing with each incremental kWh of usage, thereby reducing the cost per kWh of usage.
