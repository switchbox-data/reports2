## {9} II. Summary and Recommendations

{10} **Q. Please summarize your findings to the Commission.**

{11} A. I demonstrate that the volumetric rates associated with the Company’s current residential
{12} rate schedules (“Schedules R, RL, and RD”) are overcharging heat pump customers
{13} relative to costs they cause when adopting a heat pump. This creates a substantial cross-
{14} subsidy _within_ the residential customer class, which flows from electric customers that
{15} heat with heat pumps to those that heat with fossil fuels.

{16} To establish this, I provide evidence that BGE’s electric customers with heat pumps are
{17} overcharged by an average of $359 per year on distribution, transmission, and energy
{18} efficiency — far in excess of any new infrastructure costs their heat pumps actually
{19} cause.

[PDF p. 9 | printed p. 6]

{1} Charging heat pump customers far in excess of their cost-causation has two serious
{2} consequences: first, it violates intra-class equity, one of the key principles of cost-of-
{3} service ratemaking. Second, it undermines the state’s ambitious energy and building
{4} electrification policy goals. As a direct result of being overcharged, only the half of
{5} BGE’s electricity customers that heat with fossil fuels can currently lower their energy
{6} bills by switching to heat pumps.

{7} To stop overcharging heat pump customers, I propose that the Company create a new rate
{8} schedule for heat pump customers, Schedule R-HP, with lower volumetric rates for
{9} distribution, transmission, and EmPOWER Maryland. The purpose of these lower rates
{10} would be to decrease the total revenue collected from heat pump customers for these
{11} infrastructure and policy-related line items, aligning these customers’ rates to their actual
{12} cost of service.

{13} By better aligning these rates with cost-causation, this proposal would eliminate the
{14} significant cross-subsidy that exists between heat pump and fossil-heating electric
{15} residential customers, thus making the Company’s rates more fair. As our analysis shows,
{16} this proposal would also make it cheaper to heat with heat pumps than fossil fuels for
{17} 88% of Maryland households, thus alleviating a key obstacle to the state’s building
{18} electrification goals.

{19} **Q. Please summarize your recommendations to the Commission.**

{20} A. I recommend that the Commission:

{21} 1. Create a new residential rate schedule, Schedule R-HP, for Company electric customers
{22} who use a heat pump for heating and cooling.

[PDF p. 10 | printed p. 7]

{1} 2. Set a lower delivery service charge for Schedule R-HP (compared with Schedule R). The
{2} rate should be set at a level that reflects the distribution cost-of-service of heat pump
{3} customers, and ends cost over-recovery from these customers. At present, we estimate
{4} that this rate should be 32% lower than Schedule R’s delivery service charge.

{5} 3. Set a lower transmission rate in Rider 1 for Schedule R-HP (compared with Schedule R).
{6} The rate should be set at a level that reflects the bulk transmission cost-of-service of heat
{7} pump customers, and ends cost over-recovery from these customers. At present, we
{8} estimate that this rate should be 27% lower than Schedule R’s transmission rate.

{9} 4. Set a lower energy efficiency rate in Rider 8 for Schedule R-HP (compared with
{10} Schedule R). The rate should be set at a level that ends the disproportionate recovery of
{11} EmPOWER Maryland costs from heat pump customers. At present, we estimate that this
{12} rate should be 36% lower than Schedule R’s energy efficiency rate.

{13} 5. Auto-enroll all electric customers newly installing heat pumps into Schedule R-HP, at the
{14} time of installation. Customers would be identified through any existing or new programs
{15} that track heat pump adoption, while also offering an attestation and certification option
{16} for installation that may happen outside of these programs.

{17} 6. Auto-enroll all known electric customers that have previously installed heat pumps into
{18} Schedule R-HP.

{19} 7. Recalibrate the delivery service, transmission, and energy efficiency rates associated with
{20} Schedule R-HP in subsequent rate cases or relevant proceedings, to reflect the evolving
{21} cost-of-service of heat pump customers as adoption grows and grid conditions change.

{9} **Q. What is the principle of cost-causation?**

[PDF p. 15 | printed p. 12]

{1} A. Cost-causation is a foundational principle of sound rate design.[^5] As BGE Witness Karas
{2} testifies, “[c]osts should be borne by the customers on whose behalf the costs are
{3} incurred.”[^6] In other words, if a customer’s usage causes a cost, that customer should be
{4} charged for it — and if it does not, they should not be charged as though it does.

{5} **Q. Is the extra amount that customers pay for generation after installing heat pumps**
{6} **consistent with the cost-causation principle?**

{7} A. Yes. When fossil-fuel-heated homes install heat pumps, their annual electricity
{8} consumption increases by an average of 4,861 kWh. Every one of those new kWh
{9} requires BGE to procure additional electricity from the PJM wholesale market. Had the
{10} customer not consumed that electricity, BGE would not have had to procure it. The
{11} incremental usage is causing the new generation costs, so these homes should indeed be
{12} charged an additional $575 per year for generation.

{13} **Q. You testified that every kWh of electricity consumption causes generation costs.**
{14} **Does every kWh also cause transmission and distribution (“T&D”) costs?**

{15} A. No.

[^5]: The cost-causation principle originates in the sixth of the eight Bonbright principles of a sound rate structure: “_Fairness of the specific rates in the apportionment of total costs of service among the different_ _consumers._” James C. Bonbright, _Principles of Public Utility Rates_, New York, NY: Columbia University Press, at 291 (1961) [https://www.raponline.org/knowledge-center/principles-of-public-utility-rates/](https://www.raponline.org/knowledge-center/principles-of-public-utility-rates/).

[^6]: Direct Testimony of Jennifer A. Karas on behalf of Baltimore Gas and Electric Company (“Karas Direct”), at 4:13–14 (July 2, 2026).

[PDF p. 16 | printed p. 13]

{1} For generation, every kWh a customer consumes causes a cost. Someone has to generate
{2} the electricity that customer is consuming, and BGE has to pay for it. This is true at every
{3} hour of the year — a kWh consumed at 3 a.m. in February costs money to generate, just
{4} as a kWh consumed at 5 p.m. in August does.

{5} Transmission and distribution are fundamentally different: very few of the kWh a
{6} customer consumes causes T&D costs.

{7} Transmission and distribution are networks of existing infrastructure that deliver
{8} electricity to customers. When a customer uses electricity, it flows over this existing
{9} infrastructure. The vast majority of the time, this flow _causes no costs_: the infrastructure
{10} is already there, and it has the capacity to deliver the load, without the need for capacity
{11} upgrades. (And while this existing infrastructure does need to be paid for, these are sunk
{12} costs associated with old investments, not new costs caused by customer usage in the
{13} present.)

{14} Customer usage only causes new T&D costs when the system is **constrained**: during the
{15} small number of peak hours that customer’s demand pushes their local distribution
{16} feeder/substation or the upstream transmission system near its capacity limits. This
{17} triggers the need for capacity upgrades — larger transformers, new feeders — whose
{18} costs, according to the cost-causation principle, should be borne by the customers who
{19} caused them.

{20} The bottom line is, unlike generation, only usage during these peak hours causes new
{21} T&D costs to be incurred. Usage outside of peak hours simply flows over existing
{22} infrastructure without triggering any new investment.

[PDF p. 17 | printed p. 14]

{1} **Q. Is the extra amount that customers pay for T&D after installing heat pumps**
{2} **consistent with the cost-causation principle?**

{3} A. No, these new payments are far larger than the T&D costs customers cause by installing
{4} heat pumps.

{5} When fossil-fuel-heated homes install heat pumps, they do cause some additional T&D
{6} costs — a small fraction of the heat pump’s new usage does occur during peak hours, and
{7} that peak-hour usage does trigger new investment. Under the cost-causation principle,
{8} these customers should pay for those new costs, and I am not arguing otherwise.

{9} The problem is that under BGE’s current rates, customers installing heat pumps are
{10} charged far more than the new T&D costs caused by their heat pumps. Their T&D
{11} charges increase by an average of $345 per year — as though every one of their 4,861
{12} additional kWh were triggering new infrastructure investment. But as I demonstrate in
{13} the following questions, they only cause an average of $43 in new T&D costs per year,
{14} because the vast majority of their additional kWh flow over existing infrastructure during
{15} off-peak hours without triggering any new investment.

{16} As I stated in my definition of the cost-causation principle: if a customer’s usage does not
{17} cause new costs, they should not be charged as though it does. Yet that is precisely what
{18} is happening — heat pump customers are only causing $43 per unit in new T&D costs,
{19} but the Company is charging them an additional $345. That is a clear violation of the
{20} cost-causation principle.

{21} **Q. You testified that new T&D costs are caused only during peak hours. When do**
{22} **BGE’s distribution and transmission peak hours occur?**

[PDF p. 18 | printed p. 15]

{1} A. To answer this, I estimated the marginal cost of T&D capacity during every hour of the
{2} test year (the full methodology is described in Section X.B).

{3} Figure 5 and Figure 6 show the results.[^7]

{15} **Q. Why is the overcharging of heat pump customers undermining Maryland’s**
{16} **electrification goals?**

[PDF p. 39 | printed p. 36]

{1} A. The overpayment documented in Section III of my testimony is largely responsible for
{2} the poor bill outcomes shown above. As I will demonstrate in Section VI, eliminating the
{3} overcharge would dramatically improve bill outcomes for both natural-gas and delivered-
{4} fuel customers. Poor bill outcomes, in turn, undermine adoption through two distinct
{5} mechanisms.

{6} • First, it turns what should be savings into losses. Under current Schedule R rates, 52% of
{7} natural-gas-heated households would see their total energy bills _increase_ after switching
{8} to a heat pump. When customers learn that their bills would go up — or when their
{9} neighbors report that theirs did — they are much less likely to switch from natural gas to

{11} adoption: the overcharge makes heat pumps look like a bad deal for the majority of
{12} natural-gas customers, when in fact they could save most gas-heated customers money
{13} (without the overcharge).

{14} • Second, even for customers who would save money, the overcharge suppresses savings
{15} below the level needed to overcome inertia. Switching from a familiar heating system to
{16} a heat pump involves real and perceived costs — the hassle of installation, unfamiliarity
{17} with the technology, and uncertainty about performance. For customers to act on
{18} potential savings, those savings need to be large enough to overcome inertia and status
{19} quo bias. The overcharge shrinks these savings: natural-gas customers who do save under
{20} current rates would save more under fairer rates. Delivered-fuel customers, who already
{21} tend to save on net, would see meaningfully larger savings — making the case for
{22} switching from oil or propane to a heat pump that much more compelling. In both cases,

{1} the overcharge creates a drag on adoption by reducing the financial incentive below what
{2} it would otherwise be.

## {3} V. Proposed Rate Design: Schedule R-HP

{4} **Q. What do you propose to address the overcharging of heat pump customers?**

{5} A. I recommend that BGE create a new rate schedule, Schedule R-HP, for residential
{6} customers with heat pumps. Schedule R-HP would lower the volumetric rates for
{7} delivery service (distribution), transmission (Rider 1), and energy efficiency (Rider 8 /
{8} EmPOWER Maryland). The rates would be set at a level that eliminates the overpayment
{9} I documented in Section III. Generation rates would be **unchanged**, because generation
{10} charges already fairly track costs, as I established in Section III.

{3} **Q. How did you determine the reduction for each component?**

{4} A. For **EmPOWER Maryland**, I set the rate so that the average fossil-fuel-heated
{5} customer’s annual EmPOWER bill stays flat after heat pump installation: the average
{6} customer would pay approximately the same dollar amount they paid before—$102—
{7} spread across their higher post-installation kWh.

{8} For **distribution and transmission**, I lowered each $/kWh rate until the point where
{9} multiplying the rate by the customer’s new, higher post-heat-pump kWh usage yields the

[PDF p. 43 | printed p. 40]

{1} right annual bill — specifically, their pre-installation bill plus the small increment of new
{2} T&D costs their heat pump actually causes.

{3} Take distribution as an example. Before installing a heat pump, the average fossil-fuel-
{4} heated home pays $443 per year in distribution charges. After installation, their heat
{5} pump causes $17 in additional distribution costs. Under cost-causation principles, their
{6} post-installation distribution bill should be $461: everything they were paying before,
{7} plus the new costs they cause, and nothing more. To achieve that result, I divide $461 by
{8} their new annual consumption of 14,112 kWh, which gives a rate of 3.3¢/kWh. The same
{9} logic applies to transmission.

{10} Even though the average fossil-fuel-heated home would pay slightly more per year for
{11} transmission and distribution under Schedule R-HP after installing heat pumps than
{12} before, Schedule R-HP’s rates look lower than Schedule R’s rates because they are
{13} applied to a much larger number of kWh. Nevertheless, the customer is still paying for
{14} the same fixed infrastructure costs they paid for before they got the heat pump, as well as
{15} for the new infrastructure costs their heat pump actually causes. The lower T&D rates
{16} simply ensure the customer is no longer being overcharged for costs they do _not_ cause.

## {5} VII. Enrollment: How Customers Get onto Schedule R-HP

{6} **Q. How would customers be enrolled in the proposed Schedule R-HP?**

{7} A. I recommend two enrollment channels, both automatic.

{8} • Going forward, every customer that installs a heat pump should be auto-enrolled in
{9} Schedule R-HP at the time of installation. The primary identification mechanism is
{10} EmPOWER Maryland, which already funds and tracks heat pump installations. Every
{11} customer receiving a heat pump incentive through EmPOWER should be placed on
{12} Schedule R-HP automatically upon installation. For installations that happen outside of
{13} EmPOWER — for example, self-funded purchases — BGE should provide an attestation
{14} and certification pathway: a simple form the installer or customer submits to confirm a
{15} qualifying heat pump was installed.

{16} • For existing heat pump customers, BGE should auto-enroll all known electric customers
{17} who have previously installed heat pumps. These customers are being overcharged right
{18} now. Since the day they installed their heat pump, they have been paying far more than
{19} the T&D costs caused by their new load. And these customers are readily identifiable by
{20} BGE particularly in the context of the EmPOWER programs that have made the utilities
{21} identify past heat pump incentive recipients. There is no reason to delay correcting this.

[PDF p. 49 | printed p. 46]

{1} Both channels should be opt-out, not opt-in. The customer is placed on Schedule R-HP
{2} by default and may return to Schedule R if they choose.

{3} **Q. Why do you recommend automatic enrollment rather than an opt-in rate?**

{4} A. Because opt-in rates fail. They require customers to know the rate exists, understand that
{5} it would save them money, and take affirmative action to enroll. Most customers never
{6} do.

{7} This is not a theoretical concern. Maine’s Central Maine Power offered a seasonal heat
{8} pump rate on an opt-in basis. Enrollment never exceeded 2% of eligible customers.[^41]

{9} New York’s experience is even more striking. Con Edison has facilitated more than
{10} 43,000 heat pump installations through its Clean Heat program, yet as of December 2025,
{11} only about 500 households — barely 1% — had enrolled in the utility’s opt-in Steady
{12} Use Rate, which is designed to save a typical heat pump customer roughly $500 per
{13} year.[^42] That is $131 million in cumulative bill savings left on the table over the rate
{14} period, simply because customers did not know the rate existed or found the enrollment
{15} process confusing.

{3} prevent customers newly installing heat pumps from overpaying in the first place. But
{4} creating a new rate schedule does _not_ solve the problem — it has to be adopted at scale.

{5} Massachusetts avoided the opt-in trap entirely by building auto-enrollment into its heat
{6} pump rate from the start. In DPU 25-08, the Massachusetts Department of Public Utilities
{7} approved a lower heat pump rate for the state’s three investor-owned electric utilities —
{8} Eversource, Unitil, and National Grid.[^43] Customers who install heat pumps through Mass
{9} Save — the state’s ratepayer-funded efficiency program — are automatically enrolled at
{10} the time of installation. For customers who installed heat pumps before the program
{11} existed, Massachusetts uses an attestation process.

{12} We know that opt-in rates get low enrollment. The Company can and must design a better
{13} enrollment mechanism, which should be automatic enrollment through EmPOWER.
{14} Every heat pump customer who is not enrolled continues to be overcharged by the full
{15} amount every year.

{16} **Q. Could automatic enrollment cause some heat pump customers to see higher bills?**

[PDF p. 51 | printed p. 48]

{1} A. No, because Schedule R-HP is designed so that every heat pump customer pays less than
{2} they would under Schedule R. By simply lowering Schedule R’s existing flat rates, no
{3} heat pump customer would be made worse off by being placed on R-HP.

{4} This is the key link between rate design and enrollment. A rate that only _lowers_ charges
{5} can be safely applied to all heat pump customers without risk of adverse bill impacts. If
{6} the rate raised some charges and lowered others — for example, a higher fixed charge
{7} paired with a lower volumetric rate — some customers could see higher bills in certain
{8} months. The flat volumetric reduction avoids this entirely so there are no structural
{9} losers.

{10} A rate design that only lowers charges unlocks auto-enrollment, but it necessarily
{11} constrains eligibility: it must be a tech-specific rate, rather than one available to all
{12} residential customers. A lower rate is justified for heat pump customers: as covered in
{13} Section III, they are structural “high-usage” customers who would otherwise overpay. A
{14} lower rate available to all customers would simply lower the bills of anyone who
{15} enrolled, which would create a revenue shortfall under heavy enrollment.

{16} I believe this is the right trade-off. A rate that can be safely auto-enrolled to every eligible
{17} customer, even if restricted to a defined population, will reach far more people and thus
{18} have a fundamentally bigger impact than an optional rate with a revenue-neutral design
{19} (like a high fixed charge) that is available to all customers but requires them to discover
{20} it, evaluate it, and opt in.

{21} **Q. Why is it important to auto-enroll existing heat pump customers, not just new ones?**

[PDF p. 52 | printed p. 49]

{1} A. Because enrolling only new customers stops the cross-subsidy from getting bigger, but it
{2} does not fix it for the customers who are _already_ bearing it.

{3} If the Commission only enrolls new heat pump customers going forward, every customer
{4} who has already installed a heat pump continues to be overcharged by the full amount,
{5} indefinitely — unless they opt in on their own, which as I have discussed, many of them
{6} will not. The per-household stakes are substantial: $359 per year in combined T&D and
{7} EmPOWER overcharges. These are real dollars being transferred from heat pump
{8} customers to fossil-heating customers every year, and they have been accumulating since
{9} the day each customer installed their heat pump.

{10} BGE and EmPOWER have records of past heat pump incentive recipients. For customers
{11} who installed heat pumps outside of incentive programs, an attestation process — as
{12} Massachusetts uses — can identify them. Auto-enrollment of existing heat pump
{13} customers is what makes this proposal a genuine fix rather than a half-measure.

## {14} VIII. Why Schedule RD Does Not Solve the Problem

{15} **Q. BGE already offers a time-of-use (“TOU”) rate, Schedule RD. What is Schedule RD,**

{3} **Q. Why does Schedule RD not eliminate the overcharge, even though it charges less**
{4} **during the hours of the day when peak costs are less likely to occur?**

{5} A. Because being more cost-reflective on _time_ does not solve overcollection on _volume_.

{6} To explain, consider that TOU rates and heat pump rates solve fundamentally different
{7} problems:

{8} 1. TOU rates address economic efficiency: they give customers price signals that
{9} incentivize shifting consumption away from hours when energy is expensive and capacity
{10} investment is most likely to be triggered. That is a valuable goal, and Schedule RD is
{11} designed to pursue it. But it is not the problem I have identified in this testimony.

{12} 2. The overcharge on heat pump customers is an intra-class equity problem. It arises
{13} because fixed infrastructure costs — specifically, distribution and transmission capacity
{14} — are recovered through volumetric rates that charge every kWh as though it contributed
{15} equally to those costs. High-usage customers overpay; low-usage customers underpay.
{16} That structural problem persists whether the volumetric rate is flat or time-varying.

{17} To see why, consider what Schedule RD’s on-peak period actually contains. The on-peak
{18} window is 5 hours per weekday in summer and 7 hours per weekday in winter (split
{19} across a morning and evening block). Over a full year, that is roughly 1,650 on-peak
{20} hours — but the grid’s actual capacity constraint — the peak hours that drive new
{21} distribution and transmission investment — binds during perhaps 50 to 100 of those

[PDF p. 57 | printed p. 54]

{1} hours per year. Schedule RD charges $0.10733/kWh for _every_ on-peak kWh, treating a
{2} kWh consumed at 3pm on a mild September Wednesday the same as a kWh consumed at
{3} 5pm on the hottest day of the year. Only the latter drives capacity investment.

{4} The result is that Schedule RD, like Schedule R, spreads capacity costs across far more
{5} kWh than actually cause those costs. A customer who adds many on-peak kWh — most
{6} of _which_ fall in non-constraining hours — still overpays for the capacity embedded in the
{7} on-peak rate, just as they would overpay under a flat rate. The overcollection is
{8} redistributed across fewer hours, but it is not eliminated.

{9} Heat pump customers are exactly these customers. Their winter electricity consumption
{10} adds many kWh during the winter on-peak window (weekdays 6am–9am), but as I
{11} showed in Section III, there are very few peak hours in winter that drive distribution or
{12} transmission capacity costs. The on-peak rate charges them for every one of those hours
{13} as if they were causing new T&D costs, when they are not.

{14} This is the same structural problem that BGE Witness Karas identified: volumetric rates
{15} overcollect from high-usage customers when the costs being recovered are capacity-
{16} driven rather than energy-driven. Varying the volumetric rate by time of day does not fix
{17} that — it rearranges which hours bear the overcollection, but the total overcollection from
{18} high-usage customers, and therefore from heat pump customers as a class, remains.

{19} **Q. Is it possible to design a TOU rate that would eliminate the overcharge on heat**
{20} **pump customers?**

{21} A. In principle, yes, but there are only two paths:

[PDF p. 58 | printed p. 55]

{1} The first path is to raise the fixed charge high enough that volumetric T&D rates no
{2} longer need to recover as much fixed infrastructure cost. If the customer charge absorbed
{3} most of the T&D-related revenue requirement, the volumetric rate would carry far less of
{4} the fixed-cost burden, and the overcollection on high-usage customers would shrink. But
{5} as I discuss in Section IX, the required increase in the fixed charge would be substantial
{6} — affecting every residential customer, not just heat pump customers — and would be
{7} regressive without income-graduated protections.

{8} The second path shares the same general approach as Schedule R-HP: lower the
{9} volumetric T&D rates for heat pump customers specifically, through a tech-specific rate.
{10} Instead of lowering a flat rate, one could lower the on-peak and off-peak TOU rates
{11} proportionally. The mechanism is the same — reduce per-kWh charges so that high-
{12} usage customers are no longer overcollected from — and the restriction to heat pump
{13} customers is the same, in order to avoid a revenue shortfall from lower-usage customers
{14} joining the rate, and because heat pump customers face a structural overcharge that
{15} justifies the lower rates.

{16} As discussed in Section IX, we recommend that the Commission fix the intra-class equity
{17} problem now with whatever tech-specific rate they feel comfortable auto-enrolling heat
{18} pump customers onto. A flat heat pump rate with lower volumetric charges (for
{19} transmission, distribution, and EmPOWER) is easy for customers to understand and
{20} accept, and would guarantee bill savings for 100% of heat pump customers compared to
{21} Schedule R. A TOU heat pump rate with lower time-varying volumetric charges (for
{22} those same line items) would be superior in the sense that it could simultaneously address
{23} economic efficiency and intra-class equity, but it would be more complex to understand,

[PDF p. 59 | printed p. 56]

{1} and a small share of heat pump customers might see higher bills than under Schedule R
{2} (if they have unusually high usage during the on-peak period).

## {3} IX. Discussion of Alternative Rate Designs

{4} **Q. The heat pump rate you propose in Schedule R-HP is a flat design, simply lowering**
{5} **transmission, distribution, and EmPOWER rates at all hours of the year. Are there**
{6} **other rate designs that can eliminate the cross-subsidy?**

{7} A. Yes, and I considered several before arriving at this recommendation. But any viable
{8} solution must solve two problems simultaneously: the overpayment problem — by
{9} stopping the overcollection of transmission/distribution/EmPOWER revenue from heat
{10} pump customers — and the enrollment problem — ensure that the customers who are
{11} actually being overcharged are actually on the rate that fixes it. These two constraints
{12} significantly narrow the design space.

{13} There are two broad categories of solutions: default rate redesign, which changes
{14} Schedule R for all residential customers, and tech-specific rates, which create a new
{15} schedule for heat pump customers only. Each approach has different strengths and
{16} weaknesses on the overpayment and enrollment dimensions. I will discuss each in turn.

{17} **Q. Could the cross-subsidy be eliminated by redesigning Schedule R?**

{18} A. The main virtue of this approach is that it solves the enrollment problem directly: if you
{19} managed to eliminate the overpayment by redesigning Schedule R, the vast majority of
{20} residential customers are already on this rate, so you would fix the problem at scale
{21} without requiring anyone to change anything.

[PDF p. 60 | printed p. 57]

{1} The difficulty is that the main lever for reducing the overpayment within Schedule R is
{2} raising the fixed charge. Raising the customer charge shifts revenue recovery away from
{3} volumetric rates, which reduces the per-kWh overcollection on high-usage customers.
{4} This is not a new idea — it follows directly from BGE Witness Karas’s own testimony
{5} that “[i]ntra-class inequities are created when fixed costs are not recovered through fixed
{6} charges.”[^47]

{7} The problem is magnitude. To eliminate the heat pump overpayment by redesigning
{8} Schedule R, the volumetric rates for distribution, transmission, and EmPOWER
{9} Maryland would have to be scaled down until the increase in those charges after a home
{10} switches to electric heating matches the increase in T&D costs the home actually causes.
{11} That requires reducing those volumetric rates by 89%. To keep residential-class revenue
{12} unchanged, the average monthly fixed charge would then have to rise from $10.00 to
{13} $74.59 — an increase of $64.59 per month.[^48]

{14} This is a massive change that would affect every residential customer, not just heat pump
{15} customers, and would be regressive to low-income households unless paired with an
{16} income-graduated structure.

{17} To put that in perspective, the Company’s own embedded cost of service study
{18} (“ECOSS”) supports a customer charge of $19.55 per month for Schedule R, yet in this
{19} case the Company proposes moving it from $10.00 to just $11.00 — a $1.00 step toward

[^47]: Karas Direct at 8:13–14 (full quote reproduced in Section III of this testimony).

[^48]: _See infra_ Section X.B.5.

[PDF p. 61 | printed p. 58]

{1} a $9.55 gap, in the name of gradualism.[^49] At that pace, the fixed charge would not
{2} approach the level needed to eliminate the heat pump cross-subsidy for many decades to
{3} come.

{4} **Q. What about the tech-specific approach? What design levers does it offer, and why?**

{5} A. A tech-specific rate opens an additional lever that the default rate does not have: simply
{6} lowering volumetric charges for a defined population. Within the default rate, the only
{7} way to reduce overcollection is to raise the fixed charge, shifting revenue recovery away
{8} from per-kWh rates. That approach would also work for a rate that would only be
{9} accessible to heat pump customers.

{10} Within a tech-specific rate, there is a second option: keep the customer charge the same
{11} and simply lower the per-kWh delivery rates for heat pump customers. If the
{12} overcollection problem arises from the application of volumetric rates _designed for the_
{13} _average electric customer_ to a subclass of residential customers with structurally _above-_
{14} _average_ usage, then simply lowering the volumetric rates for that population reduces
{15} overcollection from that population. Put another way, instead of fundamentally changing
{16} the rate design for Schedule R, you can simply recalibrate it, by designing the volumetric
{17} rate component for the average heat pump customer, rather than the average electric
{18} customer.

[^49]: Karas Direct, Exhibit JAK-1. Direct Testimony of Jason B. Manuel on Behalf of Baltimore Gas and Electric Company, (July 2, 2026) (the ECOSS-supported customer-related cost of $19.55 is from the combined R/RL class in the 2024 ECOSS).

{1} **Q. How does the choice between these two kinds of tech-specific rates affect how they**
{2} **can solve the enrollment problem?**

{3} A. Unlike redesigning Schedule R, a tech-specific rate does not solve enrollment
{4} automatically — customers must enroll onto it. And as I showed in Section VII, auto-
{5} enrollment is a realistic solution to reaching meaningful participation.

{6} I am working under the assumption that the Commission is more likely to approve auto-
{7} enrollment if the rate design guarantees that no enrolled customer is made worse off.

{8} A version of Schedule R-HP with a very high fixed charge would cause a minority of
{9} heat pump customers — particularly lower-usage ones — to see higher bills in certain
{10} months. Lower volumetric rates guarantee the opposite: every enrolled customer either
{11} saves or pays the same in a given month, depending on the design, but no customer pays
{12} more in any month.

{13} In other words, tech-specific heat pump rates with lower volumetric charges would allow
{14} the Commission to achieve the benefits of auto-enrollment without paying the cost of
{15} creating structural losers.

{16} **Q. Are there different ways to structure the lower volumetric charges under a tech-**
{17} **specific rate?**

{18} A. Yes. There are three main designs, each of which can eliminate the overpayment (if
{19} properly calibrated):

[PDF p. 63 | printed p. 60]

{1} 1. **Flat** — lower all non-generation volumetric rates by whatever percentage is needed to
{2} eliminate the annual overpayment. The rates would still be flat, so they would be the
{3} same for every hour of the year. This is what I propose.

{4} 2. **Seasonal** — leave the summer rate unchanged and lower only the winter rate, or lower
{5} them both but by different amounts. This is how Massachusetts has designed their heat
{6} pump rates.

{7} 3. **Time-of-use** — lower both on-peak and off-peak rates from their levels under Schedule
{8} RD, layering TOU price signals onto the tech-specific reduction.

{9} All three solve the overpayment problem as long as the resulting per-kWh rates are set
{10} low enough to stop overcollecting from the average heat pump customer.

{11} I’ll discuss the costs and benefits of each design in turn.

{12} **Q. Given these options, why do you propose a flat tech-specific rate?**

{13} A. Because it solves both the overpayment and enrollment problems with the simplest
{14} available design. It eliminates the overcharge, guarantees savings for every enrolled
{15} customer, and is simple enough that customers, the Commission, and the Company can
{16} all verify that it works.

{17} **Q. Would you support a seasonal tech-specific rate if the Commission prefers it?**

{18} A. I would support a seasonal design if the Commission prefers it. A seasonal rate has two
{19} main benefits:

[PDF p. 64 | printed p. 61]

{1} 1. Seasonal rates are targeted: as we saw in Section III, most of the overcollection occurs
{2} during winter. Lowering rates in winter reduces overcollection from the season where
{3} most of it occurs, rather than year round.

{4} 2. Seasonal rates promote bill stability: winter is when new heat pump customers see their
{5} electric bills rise most sharply — the heat pump replaces a gas furnace, so electricity
{6} consumption increases significantly during heating months. A seasonal rate concentrates
{7} the bill relief in exactly those months, smoothing out the seasonal bill shock that might
{8} otherwise discourage adoption or cause customers to question whether the heat pump was
{9} a good investment (even if they are saving money by switching overall).

{10} Designing a seasonal heat pump rate correctly requires satisfying three conditions:

{11} 1. The seasonal rate must _lower_ what heat pump customers pay annually. Some seasonal
{12} designs try to maintain revenue-neutrality for heat pump customers by lowering the
{13} winter rate but raising the summer rate, which merely shifts overcollection between
{14} seasons.

{15} 2. The seasonal rate must lower what heat pump customers pay annually _by a sufficient_
{16} _amount to eliminate the overpayment_. Reducing the winter rate by 1 ¢/kWh, for example,
{17} would be a half-measure.

{18} 3. While rates in both seasons can be lowered—the reduced revenue collection doesn’t all
{19} have to concentrate in winter—neither season’s rates should be set higher than that in
{20} Schedule R, as that could produce structural losers, thus complicating auto-enrollment,
{21} even if the rate satisfies the first two conditions.

[PDF p. 65 | printed p. 62]

{1} **Q. Why do you not recommend the other approaches you discussed?**

{2} A. While each of the alternatives I have discussed in this section could eliminate the
{3} overpayment problem in theory, in practice their costs create a significant risk of
{4} producing a symbolic outcome that leaves the cross-subsidy largely intact.

{5} A redesigned Schedule R would require raising the fixed charge by an amount I do not
{6} believe the Commission is prepared to approve in this case, particularly without
{7} introducing income-graduation to avoid regressive impacts.

{8} A tech-specific rate with a high fixed charge could strike customers as unfamiliar and
{9} would create structural losers among lower-usage heat pump customers, complicating
{10} auto-enrollment. And without auto-enrollment, creating an optional heat pump rate (that
{11} almost no customers opt into) risks allowing stakeholders to claim victory without having
{12} any meaningful impact on the problem.

{13} A tech-specific TOU rate does not solve the overcollection problem on its own, as I
{14} demonstrated in Section VIII — it would need significantly lower volumetric rates than
{15} Schedule RD (or higher fixed charges) to actually eliminate the overcharge. The TOU
{16} option would also add complexity and may create structural losers. Unless the
{17} Commission is prepared to create a properly designed tech-specific TOU rate with rates
{18} low enough to eliminate the overcharge, auto-enroll all heat pump customers onto it, and
{19} do so in a timely fashion, this route risks allowing the perfect to become the enemy of the
{20} good.

#### {1} 5. Fixed-charge alternative

{2} **Q. In Section IX, you testified that eliminating the heat pump overpayment within**
{3} **Schedule R would require reducing the distribution, transmission, and EmPOWER**
{4} **Maryland volumetric rates by approximately 89% and raising the average monthly**
{5} **fixed charge from $10.00 to $74.59. How did you calculate those figures?**

{6} A. I solved for the version of Schedule R that satisfies two conditions at the same time.

{7} The first condition is **cost reflectivity**. When a home switches from fossil fuel heating to
{8} electric heating, the increase in the volumetric charges it pays must equal the increase in
{9} marginal transmission and distribution costs that the switch causes. I applied the redesign
{10} to three volumetric charges — distribution, transmission, and EmPOWER Maryland —
{11} and scaled all three by a single uniform factor, which preserves each charge’s existing
{12} month-to-month shape. I did not change generation charges.

{13} The second condition is **revenue neutrality**. The revenue held constant is the revenue
{14} collected through the charges the redesign touches: the monthly customer charge plus the
{15} three targeted volumetric charges. Generation revenue is unaffected by the redesign and
{16} therefore does not enter the calculation on either side. Whatever revenue the reduction in
{17} the targeted volumetric rates gives up must come back through the fixed charge.

{18} **Q. How did you determine the factor by which the targeted volumetric rates would**
{19} **have to fall?**

{20} A. That factor follows directly from the cost-reflectivity condition, and it does not depend on
{21} the fixed charge at all. Because the same default rate applies to the home both before and

[PDF p. 83 | printed p. 80]

{1} after it installs a heat pump, the identical fixed charge is paid in both cases and cancels
{2} out of the before-and-after bill difference. Only the targeted volumetric charges change.

{3} The inputs are the same figures I presented in Section III. Under the rates as filed, the
{4} increase in the representative home’s targeted volumetric charges is $402 per year —
{5} $345 in distribution and transmission charges and $57 in EmPOWER contributions. The
{6} increase in marginal T&D cost that the same change in consumption causes is $43 per
{7} year. The difference between the two is the $359 annual overpayment.

{8} Aligning the two follows directly from those two numbers. Multiplying the monthly
{9} volumetric charges by the ratio of the incremental cost to the incremental charges — $43
{10} divided by $402 — scales the increase down by that same ratio, leaving it equal to the
{11} incremental cost. That ratio is therefore the scaling factor the redesign requires:

{12}

$$\lambda^{*} = \frac{\Delta MC_{T\&D}}{\Delta V_{T\&D + EmPOWER}}$$

{13} For BGE, that ratio is 10.7%. In other words, the targeted volumetric rates would have to
{14} be cut to roughly one-tenth of their filed levels — a reduction of approximately 89% —
{15} before a home switching to electric heating would pay no more than the additional T&D
{16} costs it causes. I apply this ratio only to the T&D and EmPOWER Maryland portion of
{17} the bill, leaving generation charges unchanged.

{18} **Q. How did you then determine the fixed charge?**

{19} A. From the revenue neutrality condition. Cutting the three targeted volumetric rates to
{20} 10.7% of their filed levels leaves the remaining 89% of the revenue those charges collect

[PDF p. 84 | printed p. 81]

{1} to be recovered elsewhere, and the only remaining place to recover it in this exercise is
{2} the fixed charge.

{3} Using BGE’s actual residential customer counts and kilowatt-hour sales, the three
{4} targeted charges collect approximately $868 per residential customer per year across the
{5} class as a whole. The redesign removes approximately $775 of that amount per customer
{6} per year. Spread across twelve monthly bills, replacing it requires raising the fixed charge
{7} by $64.59 per month:

{8}

$$F^{*} = F_0 + \frac{(1 - \lambda^{*}) V_{class}}{12\, N_{class}}$$

{9} Where $F_0$ is the current fixed charge, $V_{class}$ is the annual revenue the targeted volumetric
{10} charges collect from the residential class, and $N_{class}$ is the number of residential
{11} customers. Starting from BGE’s current Schedule R customer charge of $10.00 per
{12} month, the result is a fixed charge of $74.59 per month.[^71]
