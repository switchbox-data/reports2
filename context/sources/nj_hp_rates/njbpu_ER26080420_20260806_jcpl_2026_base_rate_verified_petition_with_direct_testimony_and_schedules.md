## V. TIME-OF-USE PILOT

**Q. Does JCP&L seek approval to develop any new programs that are intended to reduce peak demand and address affordability?**

**A.** Yes. JCP&L seeks approval to develop a time-of-use (“TOU”) pilot program (the “Pilot”), supported by a comprehensive customer education and outreach initiative. The proposed Pilot is intended to evaluate how TOU rates can encourage customers to shift electricity usage away from periods of peak demand, which can help reduce system costs and improve affordability over time. If approval is granted, the Company would later seek final approval of the Pilot and cost recovery (as discussed below).

**Q. Is this Pilot supportive of public policy in New Jersey?**

**A.** Yes, in Executive Order No. 2, Governor Mikie Sherrill directed the BPU to commence the development of a “virtual power plant” program to drive down peak demand.[^reed-5] TOU rates are a critical part of a virtual power plant framework, and are, thus, beneficial in helping to address New Jersey’s long-term capacity needs.

**Q. Who would be eligible to participate in the Pilot?**

**A.** Residential customers served under default basic generation service and billed under the default residential service classification would be eligible to participate in the Pilot.

**Q. Would JCP&L build any safeguards into the Pilot to minimize potential price increases?**

**A.** Yes. While the Pilot would provide participants with visibility into price signals, including the relative costs of on- and off-peak usage, and bill comparisons, not all participants may be able to leverage that information to better manage their usage, and, consequently, at

least some may experience bill increases. Accordingly, JCP&L envisions building safeguards into the Pilot in the form of temporary backstop bill protection to minimize the amount of potential bill increases for customers participating in the Pilot.

For each Pilot participant, the Company would calculate what he or she would have paid under the default residential service rate based on his or her usage. If the TOU bill exceeds the default rate bill during the defined protection period, the participant would receive a financial adjustment so that he or she does not pay more than the backstop bill amount during that period. If the TOU bill is lower, the participant would retain the savings. This structure is intended to build customer confidence and reduce the perceived risk of trying time of use pricing, while also allowing the Company to test whether and how customers respond to TOU price signals under safeguarded exposure.

The backstop protection period would likely be proposed for up to 18 months to balance customer confidence with meaningful learning. The initial six-month period would allow participants to become familiar with TOU mechanics, price signals, and bill comparisons. It would also provide a full 12-month cycle for participants to adjust behavior across seasons and optimize outcomes under time differentiated pricing, rather than disadvantaging participants who enroll immediately before a high usage season.

**Q. What does JCP&L envision as the proposed scale and duration of the Pilot?**

**A.** JCP&L envisions the Pilot will be designed to enroll up to 30,000 customers during a two- and-a-half-year enrollment window and that the Pilot will provide a four-year learning initiative. The Company believes that the scale is large enough and the duration long enough to generate meaningful and evaluable insights across multiple customer cohorts while remaining manageable for Pilot implementation and oversight.

**Q. How will JCP&L reach out to customers to inform them of the Pilot?**

**A.** JCP&L anticipates communicating with residential customers through bill inserts, the Company’s website, and social media to inform them of their eligibility to participate in the Pilot and how the Pilot works. JCP&L would also welcome collaboration with BPU Staff and the Division of Rate Counsel on customer outreach.

**Q. Does JCP&L request cost recovery at this stage?**

**A.** Yes. JCP&L requests authority to establish a regulatory asset to defer prudently incurred costs associated with program development and early implementation planning for the Pilot. Such costs are expected to include those associated with analytics enablement, integration and implementation activities, program planning and administration, initial customer education materials, evaluation support, and quality control processes.

**Q. Why is a regulatory asset appropriate for the Pilot program development costs?**

**A.** The Pilot program development costs are incremental costs that would not be incurred absent the development of the Pilot. Deferral treatment provides a structured and transparent mechanism to track these costs and to present them for recovery in a subsequent filing or in a future base rate case, subject to prudency review and any Board conditions.

## III. COSS BACKGROUND

**Q. What is the purpose of a COSS?**

**A.** The purpose of a COSS is to allocate a utility’s overall cost of service to each rate class in a manner that reflects its underlying cost of service, _i.e._, the costs to serve its customers. The approach is well established in industry literature.[^lyons-3]

**Q. What was the approach used to develop the COSS for this case?**

**A.** The approach used to develop the COSS for this case was based on three steps. First, costs were functionalized (or assigned) into functional categories. Second, functionalized costs were classified as one of three types, based on whether the costs are related to: (1) serving peak demands; (2) serving energy demands; or (3) meeting customer service requirements. Third and finally, classified costs were allocated to each rate class based on methods that best reflect how the costs were incurred.

The three steps were performed using two types of assignments: direct assignment and indirect assignment. Direct assignments were made by utilizing JCP&L’s financial data and certain assignments of plant investments and expenses to certain functions, classifications, and rate classes. Indirect assignments were made by utilizing composite allocators based on direct and indirect assignments developed during the functionalization, classification, and allocation process. The assignments that were developed by the three- step method were then relied upon to prepare the COSS, which is based on methodologies approved by the Board in JCP&L’s fully-litigated 2012 Base Rate Filing, BPU Docket No.

[^lyons-3]: _See_ James C. Bonbright, et al., _Principles of Public Utility Rates_ 109-10 (2d ed. 1988).

<!-- PDF page 563 | printed p. 8 -->

ER12111052.[^lyons-4] The Company relied upon the same methodology when preparing its COSS in its 2016, 2020, and 2023 Base Rate Filings, which were ultimately settled.

**Q. What is functionalization?**

**A.** Functionalization is the first step in developing the COSS. Functionalization is the process of assigning rate base and expense items into operational components. The functionalization of costs in the COSS was based on JCP&L’s accounting records, which are maintained in accordance with the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s Uniform System of Accounts (“USOA”).

**Q. What is classification?**

**A.** Classification is the second step in developing the COSS. Classification is the process of assigning rate base and expense items into categories that reflect cost-causation. There are three principal causes or drivers of costs related to the electric system:

- Customer-related – costs that vary with the number of customers, such as costs associated with connecting customers to the electric system and providing basic customer services, like metering and billing;

- Demand-related – costs that vary with maximum customer demands at the time of the system peak, at the time of the rate class peak, or at the time of the individual customer peak; and

- Energy-related – costs that vary with production, transmission, and/or delivery of energy, such as fuel and purchased power expenses.

[^lyons-4]: _See_ _In the Matter of the Verified Petition of Jersey Central Power & Light Company for Review and Approval of_ _Increases in and Other Adjustments to Its Rates and Charges for Electric Service, and for Approval of Other Proposed_ _Tariff Revisions in Connection Therewith; and for Approval of an Accelerated Reliability Enhancement Program_ _(“2012 Base Rate Filing”)_, BPU Docket No. ER12111052, Order dated March 8, 2015 (“2012 Base Rate Filing Order”), at pp. 75-76.

<!-- PDF page 564 | printed p. 9 -->

**Q. What is allocation?**

**A.** Allocation is the third step in developing the COSS. Allocation is the process of assigning rate base and expense items to each rate class based on allocators that best reflect how the costs were incurred. In other words, cost allocation should follow how costs were incurred.

**Q. What types of allocators were used to develop the COSS?**

**A.** There were three types of allocators used to develop the COSS:

1. Class determinants – class characteristics, such as number of customers, peak demands, kWh sales, and revenues by rate class;

2. Special studies – detailed analysis of specific plant or expense items, such as meters and services; and

3. Indirect – composite allocators based on how other costs were allocated.

**Q. What model was used to prepare the COSS for this case?**

**A.** The COSS for this filing was based on a spreadsheet model developed by ScottMadden in compliance with the 2012 Base Rate Filing Order. Rate base and expense items in the COSS were assigned to each rate class based on the three-step process described above. The results of the COSS are shown in Figure 1 (above).

**Q. What conclusions can be reached when a rate class’s ROR is lower or higher than the system, or overall, ROR?**

**A.** If a rate class yields a ROR that is lower than the system ROR, then the revenues recovered from the rate class are less than its cost of service. Conversely, if a rate class yields a ROR that is higher than the system ROR, then the revenues recovered from the rate class are more than its cost of service. As discussed in Ms. Peng’s Direct Testimony, the COSS results were used to establish revenue targets for each rate class.

<!-- PDF page 565 | printed p. 10 -->

**Q. What data was used to prepare the COSS?**

**A.** The COSS was based on financial data for the period July 1, 2025, through June 30, 2026. The COSS includes the number of customers, sales, and revenues by rate class. The COSS also includes rate base items, including intangible plant, distribution, and general plant-in- service as well as: (a) additions to plant-in-service, including materials and supplies, cash working capital, and other regulatory assets; and (b) reductions to plant-in-service, including accumulated deferred income taxes (“ADIT”), customer deposits, customer advances, and other regulatory liabilities. The COSS also includes operations and maintenance (“O&M”) expenses, including distribution, customer service, customer account, sales, and administrative and general (“A&G”) expenses, as well as taxes other than income, such as payroll and property taxes, and income taxes.

## IV. DEVELOPMENT OF THE COSS

**Q. What was the approach to functionalize costs in the COSS?**

**A.** The COSS includes only distribution costs consistent with JCP&L’s revenue requirements.

Distribution costs include: (1) rate base and O&M expenses related to the distribution facilities, (2) customer-specific facilities, such as meters (Account 370) and services (Account 369), and (3) customer services, such as meter reading expenses (Account 902) and customer records and collection expenses (Account 903). Distribution costs were identified from the Company’s accounting records, which are maintained in accordance with the USOA.

The COSS further functionalized costs by primary and secondary distribution based on voltage levels to more accurately allocate distribution costs to each rate class. It was important to functionalize costs by primary and secondary distribution since some

<!-- PDF page 566 | printed p. 11 -->

customers are served from only primary distribution facilities and thus should not be allocated costs related to the secondary distribution facilities. Costs were functionalized into primary and secondary distribution consistent with the 2012 Base Rate Filing Order in the following manner:

- Primary Distribution – Fifty percent of costs related to Land and Land Rights (Account 360), Structures and Improvements (Account 361), Station Equipment (Account 362), Poles and Towers (Account 364), Overhead Conductors and Devices (Account 365), and Underground Conductors and Devices (Account 367) were assigned to primary distribution. Ninety percent of Underground Conductors and Devices (Account 366) were assigned to primary distribution.

- Secondary Distribution – Fifty percent of costs related to Land and Land Rights (Account 360), Structures and Improvements (Account 361), Station Equipment (Account 362), Poles and Towers (Account 364), Overhead Conductors and Devices (Account 365), and Underground Conductors and Devices (Account 367) were assigned to secondary distribution. Ten percent of Underground Conductors and Devices (Account 366) were assigned to secondary distribution. The remaining rate base and expense items were functionalized based on various methods.

**Q. What was the approach to classify costs in the COSS?**

**A.** The COSS generally classified costs based on the Average and Excess (“A&E”) method, consistent with the 2012 Base Rate Filing Order.[^lyons-5]

**Q. What costs in the COSS were classified using the A&E method?**

**A.** The COSS classifies distribution costs using the A&E method.

[^lyons-5]: 2012 Base Rate Filing Order at pp. 75-76.

**Q. How were distribution costs classified and allocated using the A&E method?**

**A.** Distribution costs were classified and allocated using the A&E method in two steps.

The first step was to calculate average demand, which represents the energy portion of JCP&L’s facilities. It represents each rate class’s share of the average demand and is calculated as each class’s share of total kWh sales. The average demand component is weighted by the system load factor representing that portion of a utility’s capacity that would be needed if all customers used energy at a constant 100% load factor.

The second step was to calculate “excess” demand, which in the COSS represents the peak demand portion of the Company’s facilities. It represents each rate class’s share of the peak demand. The class peak demand is based on coincident peak (“CP”) demands. The “excess” demand component is weighted by one minus the system load factor—and then added to the average demand component to derive the A&E allocator.

Classification of costs using the A&E method was developed utilizing average demand (kWh) and CP demand data collected by the Company for each rate class utilizing data from the Company’s Advanced Metering Infrastructure electric meters. The CP demand represents class demand at the time of the system peak.

The COSS has separate classifiers for the primary and secondary distribution systems, reflecting the average and excess demands on each system.

**A.** The COSS was used as a guide to apportion JCP&L’s overall revenue requirement to each rate class. Specifically, class revenue requirements at the system ROR are determined as the sum of required return on allocated rate base and allocated expenses (_e.g._, O&M, taxes, and depreciation).

The COSS also determines class revenue requirements by cost classifications (_i.e._, customer, demand, and energy). The class revenue requirements by cost classification are utilized by Ms. Peng to develop the Company’s proposed rates for each rate class.

## V. ELECTRIC VEHICLE (“EV”) COST OF SERVICE

**Q. Has JCP&L prepared a COSS for RS class EV customers based on EV charging data?**

**A.** Yes, JCP&L has prepared such a COSS, as directed in the Board’s Order dated June 8, 2022.[^lyons-6] The COSS for RS class EV customers is based on the RS class cost of service, adjusted for demand differences between RS class customers and RS class EV customers. Specifically, the residential EV charging data shows that the peak demand requirements for RS class EV customers are lower than the peak demand requirements for RS class customers as a whole. **Q. Has JCP&L prepared a similar COSS for commercial class EV customers based on available EV charging data?**

**A.** Yes. JCP&L prepared a COSS for commercial class EV customers based on EV charging data. The COSS for commercial class EV customers was based on the commercial GS class cost of service, adjusted for demand differences between GS class customers and GS class EV customers. Specifically, the GS class EV customer demands show that the peak demand requirements for GS class EV customers are higher than the peak demand requirements for GS class customers as a whole. The higher peak demand requirements yield a higher cost of service for GS class EV customers.

## III. DESIGN OF THE PROPOSED DISTRIBUTION RATES

**Q. Please describe the fundamental goal when designing the proposed distribution rates in this proceeding.**

**A.** Cost causation is the overriding principle that guides the rate design; specifically, rate structure should reflect the underlying cost structure, and rates must be at a level sufficient to permit JCP&L to recover its revenue requirement. Electricity rates should also reflect the costs of service to appropriately convey the underlying costs to customers in an understandable manner that ensures the efficient use of resources and promotes greater customer satisfaction.

**Q. Please explain the general process of JCP&L’s rate design.**

**A.** Four ratemaking steps are incorporated in designing JCP&L’s rates:

1. Revenue Requirement. First, the revenue requirement is developed based on the Company’s operating expenses, taxes, depreciation expense, and return on rate base. Ms. Pittavino’s Direct Testimony presents the distribution system revenue requirement for JCP&L based on a test year from July 1, 2025, to June 30, 2026.

2. COSS. In this step, as detailed in the accompanying Direct Testimony of Tim Lyons (Exhibit JC-09), JCP&L’s distribution revenue requirement is allocated to various rate classes based on cost causation principles.

<!-- PDF page 791 | printed p. 11 -->

3. Interclass Revenue Moderation. Next, the impacts some rate classes would experience if rates were designed to collect their entire COSS-allocated revenue requirement are reviewed and considered for moderation consistent with the principle of gradualism.

4. Intraclass Rate Design. The final step establishes the individual rates that are ultimately used to bill customers. Such rates are designed to collect the revenue requirement, as moderated, from customers on a class-by-class basis.

**Q. What do you mean by interclass revenue requirement moderation?**

**A.** The primary objective of this ratemaking step is to moderate the impact that occurs when matching allocation of costs among rate classes based solely on the costs caused by each rate class, as determined by the COSS. This moderation is accomplished through adjustments that balance the application of cost causation principles against other concerns and interests important to sound rate design, including reducing significant rate class cross- subsidies and preventing undue customer impact.

**Q. Please explain how interclass revenue requirement moderation is implemented.**

**A.** First, the COSS presents the unitized rates of return (“UROR”) for each rate class. The UROR of a rate class is the class rate of return divided by the Company’s overall average rate of return.

A class UROR greater than 1.0 indicates that the rate class revenue requirement exceeds its cost of service. A class UROR less than 1.0 indicates that the rate class revenue requirement is less than its cost of service. URORs are used as a guide to measure the progress proposed changes in rates will achieve in moving all rate classes toward a UROR of 1.0 or “unity,” which is generally accepted as a desirable goal in rate design.

<!-- PDF page 792 | printed p. 12 -->

It is also generally recognized, however, that a very rapid movement toward unity can produce undesirable customer rate impacts. The Board has long recognized that progress toward unity should be tempered with gradualism to mitigate the impact of rate changes on customers.[^peng-2]

**Q. What is gradualism?**

**A.** Gradualism is a precept of utility ratemaking that refers to the process of moving to unity over time (_i.e._, over the course of multiple base rate cases). Gradualism is a consistent consideration in establishing rates for all rate classes. If the movement toward unity would result in subjecting one or more rate classes to an unreasonable customer impact in a particular base rate case, then the principle of gradualism would subordinate the goal of achieving unity to the goal of moderating customer impact, even though doing so would cause some classes to generate revenues in excess of their particular COSS-determined class revenue requirement. Where gradualism is an appropriate consideration for a particular rate class, the movement toward unity for that rate class may be done incrementally and spread over current and future base rate cases to reduce the risk of rate shock.

**Q. What do you mean by intraclass rate design?**

**A.** The COSS provides classified costs (_e.g._, customer-related or demand-related) and allocates those costs to each rate class, which facilitates the development of a series of specific corresponding individual rate elements (_i.e._, customer, demand, and energy

AGEN LEXIS 1666, at \*46, Order (June 15, 1993) (“It has been the long-standing policy of the Board to temper interclass revenue allocations with the concern for rate gradualism; i.e., customers should not be saddled with burdensome bill increases for the sake of moving class rates to unity sooner rather than later.”).

However, consideration of customer impact, revenue stability, ease of application and understanding, as well as other practical concerns, may temper the extent to which these individual rate elements are used to reflect the respective costs of service for each cost classification. For example, small commercial customers under Service Classification GS – General Service Secondary, did not have demand meters prior to smart meters and are not subject to demand charges, and, so, for these customers, both demand and energy costs are recovered with a per-kilowatt-hour (“kWh”) charge. JCP&L is not proposing adding a demand charge to these customers in this filing, but, with the availability of demand data from the deployment of smart meters, the Company could consider introducing a demand charge for small commercial customers under Service Classification GS in a future base rate case.

Once the amount of classified revenue each rate element should produce has been determined, charges for each rate element must be established based on the applicable billing determinants so that the rates will produce the target level of revenue.

**Q. What principles did you apply when conducting the intraclass rate design?**

**A.** My general approach to the intraclass rate design step is guided by the following principles:

1. Individual rates should reflect the associated unit cost of service, so that proposed revenues, derived with rates applied with billing determinants, should move towards full cost basis, as provided by the COSS;

2. The rates by class should increase on a percentage basis to meet the Company’s overall rate increase in a measured way with equitable recovery from other classes for any shortfall within each rate class; and

3. The proposed change in individual rates should ensure reasonable customer impacts.

More specifically, as of December 31, 2025, there were only 44 DCFC customers, and only 22 of them participated in the EV Driven program, receiving $107,574 in total credits through the distribution demand discount program. And, as of December 31, 2025, there were only 14 multi-family level 2 charging sites at multifamily complexes, which received $232 in total net off peak charging credits. Moreover, based on the limited data available, the load profiles identified in the COSS for non-residential EV charging customers relative

<!-- PDF page 802 | printed p. 22 -->

to non-EV secondary customers suggest that non-residential EV charging customers should be paying higher rates, which would run counter to the policy goal of incentivizing adoption of EV charging.

**Q. If JCP&L intends not to continue the EV Charger Off-Peak Credit Program, how does the Company propose to encourage residential off-peak EV charging going forward?**

**A.** JCP&L proposes to develop a time-of-use (“TOU”) pilot program (the “Pilot”) for residential customers to address affordability and generation capacity cost concerns, which will also benefit residential EV charging. The Company believes a TOU rate design gives all customers, including customers with EV charging, ultimate control and needed flexibility to manage their electric bills. The proposal to develop the Pilot is explained in further detail in Ms. Reed’s Direct Testimony.

**Q. Will JCP&L offer specific rate schedules or new tariff provisions for DCFC charging in the future?**

**A.** Based on the DCFC load characteristics, a specific rate schedule or special tariff provision is not warranted at this time. Should it become apparent that a separate rate schedule or tariff provision is needed, the Company will endeavor to develop an appropriate rate design at that time.

**Q. Will JCP&L offer specific rate schedules or new tariff provisions for medium and heavy-duty EV charging in the future?**

**A.** JCP&L currently has a petition pending before the Board for approval of a medium and heavy-duty charging ecosystem program in BPU Docket No. QO25020100, which proposes the addition of a new electric vehicle charging ecosystem rider (“Rider MHD”)
