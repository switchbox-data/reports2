**I. INTRODUCTION AND PURPOSE**

**Q.** Please state your name, business address, and on whose behalf you are testifying.

**A.** My name is Juan-Pablo Velez. I am Co-founder and Executive Director of Switchbox, 1 Whitehall Street, 17th Floor, New York, NY 10004\. I am testifying on behalf of the Citizens Utility Board (“CUB”).

**Q.** Are you the same Juan-Pablo Velez who submitted direct testimony in this proceeding as CUB Exhibit 4.0?

**A.** Yes.

**Q.** What is the purpose of your rebuttal testimony?

**A.** I respond to three rebuttals filed by The Peoples Gas Light and Coke Company (“PGL” or “the Company”) that address the targeted-electrification (“TE”) analysis in my direct testimony: Theodore Eidukas (NSG-PGL Ex. 15.0); the joint rebuttal of Eldringhoff and Dickson (NSG-PGL Ex. 17.0); and the Brattle Group’s Graves, Figueroa, and Sreenath (NSG-PGL Ex. 21.0).

**Q.** Does anything in PGL’s rebuttal cause you to modify the recommendations in your direct testimony and will you respond to every part of PGL’s rebuttal?

**A.** No to both. I continue to recommend the same three-part order from my direct testimony — an independent, system-wide hydraulic-feasibility-and-sequencing study; release of its inputs for independent verification; and a follow-on process to produce a _plan_ — restated in full in Section VIII. For reasons that I make clear below, I will address only the objections that do the most work; my silence on any other point is not agreement with it.

**II. WHAT MY ANALYSIS FOUND AND HOW PGL RECHARACTERIZES IT**

**Q.** Please summarize what your direct testimony found and recommended.

**A.** Two findings and one request. First, a substantial share of the residential blocks in PGL’s Pipe Replacement Program (“PRP”) would be cheaper to electrify than to replace pipe for: “\[a\]pproximately 14.3% — 213 of the 1,488 in-scope fully residential blocks” are cost-effective to electrify even under the strictest test.[^1] Second, PGL’s current process “cannot capture more than a negligible share of” that potential, for a reason that “is structural.”[^2] The request was a three-part order: a system-wide study (Part 1); release of its inputs for independent verification (Part 2); and a follow-on process to “translate the Part 1 findings into an actionable plan” (Part 3\).[^3]

**Q.** Was any part of that a proposal to run a TE program?

**A.** No. My testimony was explicit: “None of the three parts asks the Commission to commit to a Track 2 program at the present time”[^4] I asked for the _study and plan_ that would determine whether and where an electrification track makes sense — not the track itself.

**Q.** How does PGL characterize your testimony?

**A.** Accurately on the findings, then inaccurately on the ask. PGL’s witnesses do not seriously dispute that my analysis identifies blocks where electrification is cheaper than pipe replacement. Instead, they recharacterize my request as a directive to “establish a parallel decommissioning program alongside the existing PRP,”[^5] then argue my analysis is not complete enough to support _that program_. As Section III shows, that is an argument against a proposal I did not make.

**Q.** Does PGL agree that its current process captures little of the potential you identified?

**A.** In substance, yes — though its witnesses frame it as criticism. They argue that electrifying the riskiest projects would delay them, and that doing it at scale would be a major undertaking.[^6] Both points are mine. That the current riskiest-first, project-by-project process cannot accommodate electrification at scale is exactly why I asked for a study and plan to design a track that could. That this would be a major effort is not a reason to skip the planning: it is the reason to do it.

**III. PGL MISCHARACTERIZES MY PROPOSAL AS A PROGRAM**

**Q.** What is the central mischaracterization in PGL’s rebuttal, and why does it matter?

**A.** That I asked the Commission to order a decommissioning program now. I did not. Part 3 of my request asked for a follow-on process to design “a ‘track’ under the PRP program that would allow PGL to decommission a meaningful share of” the cost-effective blocks[^7]. That is, the design of that track, not the track itself. Read correctly, much of PGL’s rebuttal becomes a non sequitur: that my analysis is not complete enough to justify large-scale electrification is beside the point when I have not asked the Commission to order it. I ask for the Commission to order a study to determine whether it is justified. This single misreading shapes the objections I address in Sections IV through VII. Addressing it makes it unnecessary for me to engage with the full list of arguments PGL’s witnesses make.

**Q.** Witness Eidukas argues that your proposal is “premature” and belongs in the Future of Gas process. How do you respond?

**A.** Witness Eidukas writes that “targeted electrification proposals and request for a new docketed proceeding are premature and should be deferred to the Future of Gas process.”[^8] That assumes I proposed a pilot — a program. I did not. I proposed a plan, preceded by a study. Nor did I insist on a new, separate proceeding. If the Commission prefers the follow-on planning occur within the Future of Gas docket, that is fine. I am not an attorney, and I now offer my understanding rather than a legal conclusion.[^9] The question I raise — whether ratepayers should fund replacing pipe that may not need replacing — is a prudence question, the core business of a rate case. As I understand the Commission’s recent orders, a pilot or program may be directed to the Future of Gas series, but a study of this kind is appropriately ordered in a rate case, decided case by case rather than by categorical rule.[^10] Indeed, in a 2025 rate case the Commission approved a utility’s zonal-electrification “Territory Study,” holding that it “need not and should not wait for the conclusion of \[the Future of Gas series\].”[^11]

**IV. PGL'S "INCOMPLETE ANALYSIS, THEREFORE REJECT IT" OBJECTION IS CIRCULAR**

**Q.** Several witnesses argue that your analysis omits hydraulic feasibility and certain cost categories, and should therefore be rejected. How do you respond?

**A.** Witnesses Eldringhoff and Dickson put it most directly: my analysis is not “a reliable basis for comparing targeted electrification to pipe replacement” because it “excludes multiple categories of cost and feasibility that are necessary to inform an implementable utility decision,” which they call “threshold requirements.”[^12] Witness Eidukas concludes it “may (or may not) provide a preliminary indication of potential scale, \[but\] it is not a suitable basis for Commission action.”[^13] These objections make two errors.

**Q.** What are the two errors?

**A.** First, the reasoning is circular. The objection is that my analysis does not establish system-wide hydraulic feasibility, and the conclusion drawn is that the Commission should therefore not order a study of it. That would defeat any feasibility study ever proposed. No one yet knows the system-wide answer — as Witness Eidukas quotes from my testimony, not even “PGL, because nobody has run the sequencing analysis”[^14] — and only PGL holds the data such a study requires, which it has not released. Second, the objection mistakes a preliminary screen for a final decision tool. “A preliminary indication of potential scale” is not a deficiency — it is what a screening study is for. Cost-effectiveness and hydraulic feasibility are two sequential screens: my analysis performs stage one, identifying cost-effective blocks; stage two — which of those are hydraulically feasible to decommission — is what the independent study would determine.

**Q.** Witnesses Eldringhoff and Dickson also argue that your simplifying assumptions would “introduce material uncertainty” into PGL’s ability to meet the 2035 retirement directive. How do you respond?

**A.** That, too, is a concern about a program. A study and a plan cannot “introduce material uncertainty” into PGL’s compliance with a retirement directive,[^15] because they do not change what PGL does between now and 2035 — they generate information.

**Q.** PGL frames several of your assumptions as omissions. Were they concealed?

**A.** No. Every limitation PGL recites was disclosed up front as one of seven explicit exclusions[^16] — Witness Eidukas concedes my analysis has “limitations that he himself acknowledges.”[^17] And several exclusions make the comparison _more_ conservative: my 100%-participation assumption models the most expensive case — converting every unit on a block — so relaxing it only lowers electrification’s modeled cost. Furthermore, by counting “only upfront capital costs” while excluding the decades of operating a replaced gas main,[^18] my numbers favor pipe replacement. PGL still loses the comparison.

**V. THE STUDY CAN AND SHOULD BE DONE — AND IS NOT ALREADY BEING DONE**

**Q.** Witness Eidukas suggests the study could jeopardize PGL’s retirement deadline and “divert engineering” resources. Is that credible?

**A.** No. What I asked for is an engineering and computational feasibility study — the kind utilities and their consultants run routinely. The claim that commissioning it would put a multi-billion-dollar capital program at risk does not withstand scrutiny; and if it did, that would indict how the program is managed. PGL’s position is also internally inconsistent: it calls this analysis too burdensome to undertake[^19] while telling the Commission it has _already engaged a consultant_ to develop exactly this kind of evaluation. Both cannot be true.

**Q.** That points to PGL’s claim that it is already doing this work through the framework being developed by Charles River Associates. How do you respond?

**A.** Witness Eidukas calls “\[t\]he CRA framework … the appropriate vehicle for evaluating NPA cost-effectiveness.”[^20] That is a different thing. The CRA framework is a _project-by-project_ tool: PGL’s risk model ranks segments, and each is run through a benefit-cost screen one at a time. I asked for a _system-wide study_ that produces a _plan_ for capturing electrification at whatever scale proves cost-effective and feasible. The two are not interchangeable. Hydraulic feasibility is a network property a one-block-at-a-time screen cannot capture, and achieving it at scale requires organizational planning a project screen cannot produce. PGL answers a system-wide proposal with a project-by-project tool. Furthermore, an internal study commissioned by PGL itself cannot guarantee its own independence from PGL’s financial interest in favoring pipe replacement over TE.

**Q.** Witness Eidukas also argues it is premature to run your study before the NPA framework is “finalized and approved.” How do you respond?

**A.** That gets the sequence backwards. We already know hydraulic feasibility will be a criterion in any NPA framework, and that it is a network problem. Measuring it system-wide does not depend on the framework being finished first; given the 2035 timeline, the schedule demands the two proceed in parallel. The study’s cost is small in context — a fraction of the roughly $188 million in PRP project spending PGL has planned for 2026 alone,[^21] against a program on the order of $4.3 billion.[^22]

**VI. MY UPFRONT COST ESTIMATES WITHSTAND PGL'S CRITIQUES**

**Q.** The Brattle witnesses say you excluded blocks with mixed customers, “which would be most of Chicago.” Is that correct?

**A.** No. They assert I “excluded reviewing any blocks that have mixed types of customers (which would be most of Chicago),”[^23] but that is incorrect on my own data: 72.3% of the in-scope PRP blocks (1,488 of roughly 2,057) are fully residential, not mixed.[^24] Excluding mixed-customer blocks _shrank_ the scope of my claim — a conservative choice that cuts against the overgeneralization charge, not for it. And that low-density blocks are the strongest electrification candidates is one of my central findings, so it is curious to see it offered as a rebuttal.

**Q.** The Brattle witnesses argue you examined only a small number of blocks — about 108 miles, or 10.6% of the PRP. How do you respond?

**A.** That is what a sample is. My analysis examines a deliberately scoped sample and infers that the result generalizes — and asks for a full study precisely to test that inference at system scale. The 10.6% figure[^25] is a _floor_ set by an intentionally narrow residential scope, not a ceiling. Reading my conservative lower bound as my estimate of the whole opportunity is the same mischaracterization addressed in Section III.

**Q.** Witnesses Eldringhoff and Dickson argue your single blended-average cost-per-mile figure obscures variation. How do you respond?

**A.** I acknowledged the blended program-wide average as a simplification that should be refined in the full study — one more reason to order it. But the criticism cuts both ways. PGL says my apparently cost-effective blocks “may simply be blocks where PRP costs happen to be below the system average.”[^26] By the same logic, some blocks that did _not_ pass my screen have above-average replacement costs and are in fact cost-effective to electrify. Disaggregating the average moves blocks in both directions. PGL assumes it moves them only in the direction convenient to itself.

**Q.** The Brattle witnesses argue your $28,706 per-home electrification cost is too low — “towards the low end” against a $50,000 figure — and speculative. How do you respond?

**A.** I used local, actual data in preference to a national average: the U.S. building stock is too varied for a nationwide mean to be the better estimator for Chicago. My figure[^27] comes from ComEd’s CY2024 Cost-Effectiveness Analysis, in which Guidehouse compiled actual costs from implementer invoices and program tracking data under real Chicago-area programs — and Eldringhoff and Dickson concede in their own rebuttal that this is my source.[^28] Invoice-based actual costs are the opposite of speculative.

**Q.** The Brattle witnesses argue that sensitizing your housing-cost assumption upward, and adding the administrative costs of electrification at scale, undercut your numbers. How do you respond?

**A.** Again, a fair comparison must move both sides of the ledger. The Brattle witnesses move only the side that hurts electrification. If the electrification cost is sensitized upward, the _pipe-replacement_ cost must be too — and utility construction costs have been rising substantially faster than general inflation in recent years,[^29] so a sensitivity run on one side only is not a sensitivity analysis. It is an assumption. The same applies to administrative costs, which I asked to have developed in the plan. Against any administrative cost of electrification one must net the _reduced_ PRP costs PGL would no longer incur, and if we “zoom out” to the cost of electrification at scale we must also zoom out to its _savings_ — the economies of scale that are the entire rationale for a coordinated track.

**VII. CUSTOMER PARTICIPATION IS A SOLVABLE CHALLENGE, NOT A BAR TO STUDYING THE QUESTION**

**Q.** PGL repeatedly invokes customer participation. What is the objection, and how do you respond to it?

**A.** Across the rebuttals, PGL argues that because near-universal participation is hard to achieve, electrification cannot substitute for pipe retirement and should not be pursued. Witnesses Eldringhoff and Dickson say my analysis “assumes full customer participation,” and that “absent near-universal participation … targeted electrification cannot function as a substitute for pipe retirement.”[^30] The Brattle witnesses say “customer persuasion to reach 100% participation is not a realistic, scalable NPA prospect.”[^31] But the argument slides between two different claims — that electrification is not worth _studying_, and that it is not worth _doing_. The participation point cannot support the first.

**Q.** Why not?

**A.** Because taken at face value it proves too much. If one requires 100% participation _and_ assumes it can never be achieved, then targeted electrification — or any approach that retires a shared gas main — could never work anywhere. That is not a finding about my analysis: it is a refusal to engage the question. The whole purpose of the study and plan is to determine how large the opportunity is and what capturing it would take, including the participation and “social feasibility” questions, whose answers may call for a regulatory or legislative adjustment to the obligation to serve. Participation is a challenge to design around, as utilities do in every other program.

**Q.** Is there evidence the participation challenge is being addressed elsewhere?

**A.** Yes — and PGL’s own witnesses point to it. The Brattle witnesses raise California, noting that “PG\&E has pursued small-scale electrification NPAs.”[^32] California has since moved past those one- and two-building efforts: SB 1221 (2024) authorizes neighborhood-scale gas-decommissioning pilots that proceed on the consent of roughly two-thirds of affected customers rather than unanimity, and let the regulator relieve the utility of its obligation to serve within a pilot’s boundaries once customers convert.[^33] I do not claim this is solved at full scale. The point is narrow: “customers won’t all participate” is a problem regulators are actively solving, not a law of nature. And under cost-of-service regulation PGL earns a return on the pipe it replaces, not on the pipe it removes — so reluctance to even study the alternative is, at a minimum, convenient.

**VIII. CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATION**

**Q.** Please restate your recommendation.

**A.** My recommendation is unchanged from my direct testimony, and PGL’s rebuttals reinforce it. I recommend that the Commission order PGL to: (1) conduct, or fund an independent party to conduct, a system-wide hydraulic-feasibility-and-sequencing study; (2) release that study’s inputs — public inputs publicly, and sensitive pipe-topology and customer data to qualified intervenors under NDA — so the analysis can be independently verified; and (3) undertake a follow-on process that produces a _plan_ for how PGL could capture a meaningful share of the cost-effective electrification potential my analysis identifies. None of these asks the Commission to commit to an electrification program today. They ask it to develop the information needed to decide — information that, on this record, only PGL holds and has not produced. PGL’s central objection, that my analysis does not support ordering a program, answers a proposal I did not make. What I proposed is the study and the plan.

**Q.** Does this conclude your rebuttal testimony?

**A.** Yes, it does.
