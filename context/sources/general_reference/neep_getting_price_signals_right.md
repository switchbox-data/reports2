## Definitions

**Cold climate air source heat pump:** A cold climate variable speed air-source heat pump designed to maintain heating capacity and efficiency at low outdoor temperatures (5°F and below).

**Cost causation:** Ratepayers are responsible for the costs utilities incur to provide electric service to them.

**Cost of service:** Costs of supplying and delivering energy to a particular customer.

**Critical peak pricing:** An event-based pricing structure that increases prices during a select few annual peak load periods. Volumetric rates remain lower the remainder of the year.

**Cross-subsidization:** A pricing outcome that inflates rates above one customer class’s cost of service, while undercharging another customer class. Studies have found heat pump customers cross-subsidize oil and natural gas heating customers.

**Demand charge:** A usage-based charge that reflects a customer’s highest level of electricity used, measured in kilowatts (kW) over a certain interval. Demand charge rates are denominated in $/kW.

**Fixed charge:** A portion of a customer’s bill that is a flat fee and represents a portion of all fixed fees incurred by the utility.

**Income graduated fixed charge:** A fixed monthly charge that varies in accordance with a customer’s income level.

**Low-income discount rate:** An affordability tool that discounts utility rates for income eligible customers.

**Spark gap:** The price ratio of electricity compared to gas. It is calculated in terms of cost per MMBtu of electricity divided by the cost per MMBtu of gas.

**Standard two-part tariff:** The most common type of rate design in the United States. It includes both fixed and volumetric charges. Often, the fixed costs for utilities will be embedded in both the fixed and volumetric parts of a customer’s bill to ensure that volumetric rates are high enough to incentivize customers to use energy efficiently.

**Volumetric charge:** A usage-based charge calculated on the basis of electricity consumed, measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh). Volumetric charge rates are denominated in $/kWh.

## Executive Summary

Winter heating costs are a growing policy concern as electricity, oil, and gas rates rise and states pursue heat pump adoption to meet affordability, energy, and climate goals. While heat pumps can reduce a household’s overall energy usage, they can result in higher electric bills for many customers and customers paying more than their fair share of electric system costs. Current traditional two-part rate structures are priced to cover the costs of a summer-peaking grid, but heat pumps increase usage in the winter, where there is room for increased energy usage (Chen et al. 2026). This misalignment can result in cross-subsidization among customer classes and utilities overcharging heat pumps customers. In response, regulators and utilities have turned to rate design reforms to reduce operational costs of heat pumps and better align customer bills with the costs they impose on the grid. Properly designed rates could improve grid utilization while lowering electricity costs for households that rely on heat pumps for winter heating.

This paper examines how modern electric rate design, both technology-specific and technology-neutral, can improve affordability for heat pump customers and better align customer bills with grid costs. We compare energy costs for homes converting from heating with fuel (gas, oil, and propane) to heating with a heat pump, analyze real-world rate designs from around the region to see how they lower operational costs, and present an illustrative seasonal rate designed for heat pump users.

Our analysis finds that modern rate designs can significantly reduce heating costs for households using heat pumps. The greatest savings occur under rates designed specifically for heat pump customers, although well designed technology-neutral rates can also produce meaningful savings. For the purposes of this paper, we refer to these technology-neutral rates that produce savings for heat pump customers as heat pump friendly rates. We examine six state case studies and conclude with guidance for regulators and utilities looking to deploy modern rate reforms to address unintended cost shifts between customers due to heat pump usage.

### An Analysis of Heat Pump Operating Costs Compared to Gas and Delivered Fuels

Our analysis examined operating costs for customers switching to whole-home cold climate air source heat pumps from gas and delivered fuels, using current rate structures and an illustrative heat pump specific seasonal rate. These results are modeled estimates based on average rates and household energy use. Additionally, the results reflect energy bill savings only and do not include equipment or installation costs. Individual households’ results will vary.

Key findings from this section include:

- **Oil and propane homes save right away.** Under existing electric rates, switching to a whole-home heat pump saves the average household $1,027 per year compared to fuel oil and $2,237 compared with propane, and the average household saves money in every state.
- **Results for gas homes are mixed and track the spark gap.** Under existing rates, switching from gas to a whole-home heat pump raises bills by $103 a year on average. Seven jurisdictions see costs increase [PDF p. 8 | printed p. 7] and six see cost savings. Delaware and Maryland see the highest average savings at about $300, and the states with the highest spark gaps (Connecticut, New York, and Rhode Island) see average increases of roughly $500 to $700.
- **A hypothetical seasonal heat pump rate brings the average household that converts from gas to a heat pump to cost parity in all Northeast and Mid-Atlantic states.** A 37 percent lower winter volumetric rate yields annual savings compared with gas in all 13 jurisdictions, with an average savings of $603 per year across the region. The savings range from an average of $199 in Connecticut to an average of $951 in Massachusetts.
- **Technology-neutral rates that better align bills with grid costs can deliver savings too.** Time of use rates and demand charges lower average operating costs for customers converting from gas to heat pumps. Our analysis of three rates currently offered to all customers in the BGE and Con Edison territory found heat pump customers can save an average of $400 to over $1,000 on operating costs compared with gas, with the largest savings coming from Con Edison’s demand-based Steady Use Rate.

### Identifying the Right Rate Design

Section 4 outlines key considerations for aligning rate design with heat pump use, to help regulators, utilities, and other stakeholders design a modernized rate that fits their near- and long-term goals. The first decision is whether to adopt a technology-specific rate, available only to heat pump or electric heating customers, or a technology-neutral rate, open to all customers. States that choose a technology-specific rate face additional implementation decisions around enrollment, what bill components the rate impacts, and a long-term transition strategy. Whether a state pursues a technology-specific or technology-neutral approach, several considerations apply to every rate design, including customer education, enrollment, and alignment with programs that reduce energy burden.

## Introduction

Heat pump adoption is growing in the Mid-Atlantic and Northeast, driven by state climate goals and the efficiencies of the heating equipment (RMI 2026; McKenna, Shah, and Silberg 2020). Various market influences have driven this growth, including technological improvements, increased customer and contractor awareness, expansion of energy efficiency programs, and shifts in state-level energy and climate policies (NEEP 2022). Additionally, states throughout the region are implementing programs that incentivize participants to install heat pumps as the main heating source for a home (EMT 2024b; MA DPU 2025e; NYS DPS 2025).[^1]

While electric heating technology has advanced, electricity rate design has yet to keep pace. Not only have electric rates continued to increase but the standard two-part tariff has been shown to overcharge heat pump customers. This weakens the affordability case for heat pumps just as political momentum is building behind heating electrification, driven by high and volatile fuel oil prices. Further, without changes to rate design, households converting from gas to heat pumps can see a steep rise in winter electric bills that isn’t tied to the real cost of serving them. These trends have led to a robust discourse around how to design rates to make heat pumps more affordable with some states looking to prioritize heat pump specific rates, others modernizing rates to be more reflective of grid costs, and others looking to address peak demand.

### Homes That Heat With Heat Pumps Are Overcharged

Customer electric bills commonly use a standard two-part tariff, which comprises both fixed and volumetric charges. The fixed charge, or customer charge, portion of a customer’s bill is a flat fee each billing period that is meant to represent basic customer costs incurred by the utility like billing and metering. The volumetric bill charge recovers the costs to supply and deliver the energy used, which includes some portion of the transmission and distribution (T&D) system costs (Lazar 2016). Homes heating with heat pumps lower or eliminate onsite fossil fuel costs but significantly increase electric usage in the winter. This added usage increases the customers’ contribution to T&D costs. However, because the Northeast grid is summer peaking, existing T&D infrastructure can absorb additional winter usage from heat pumps. As a result, heat pump customers pay more toward T&D than the costs they actually impose on the grid, resulting in them overpaying and cross-subsidizing other ratepayers.

> When rates for one customer group are higher than their cost of service, resulting in undercharging another customer group, there is a cross-subsidy. Studies have found heat pump customers cross-subsidize oil and natural gas heating customers. As a result, non-heat pump customers are paying less than their fair share.

### Rate Reform Can Align Affordability and Grid Costs

Modernizing rates can address the issue of cross subsidization between customer classes and better align bills with grid costs through lowering the volumetric charge through tools such as seasonal rates, TOU rates, and demand charges. Implementing these rate designs can alleviate the issue of cross subsidization and ensure customer bills better reflect costs for the grid. This is not the first time rates have been deployed to accommodate higher energy use in the winter. Rates that reduce volumetric costs for homes that have electric resistant heat have been used since the early 1960s (Luzerne Electric Division 1963). Today, rates for heat pump customers can take many forms and address both the fairness concern for heat pump customers and better align bills with grid costs. These rates can include:

- Increasing the fixed charge to better reflect fixed costs, which allows a lower volumetric charge
- Adopting a seasonal rate with a lower winter volumetric charge, reflecting lower winter distribution costs and higher winter usage from heat pumps
- Implementing time of use rates, which charge more at peak hours of the day but otherwise provide a lower volumetric rate
- Adding a demand or critical peak pricing component that recovers peak-driven costs directly and allows a lower volumetric rate

### Scope of This Paper

In this paper, we first analyze the impacts to electric bills if households convert to a whole-home cold climate air source heat pump system (ccASHP) on an existing, standard two-part tariff rate structure. We then provide an overview of rate design options that look to better align customer costs with costs to the grid and ultimately lower costs for heat pump customers. Next, we introduce case studies that highlight examples of these rates in action for states looking to prioritize electrification, align rates with grid costs, and lower winter peak through demand flexibility. Finally, we offer key considerations for regulators, state agencies, utilities, and advocates looking to deploy heat pump friendly rates to help design and deploy a modern rate design that aligns most with their goals.

## Section 1: Analysis of Heat Pumps on Energy Bills

To assess the impacts on energy bills of converting from heating with fuel to heating with a whole-home ccASHP, we conducted a comprehensive, multi-component analysis, relying on modeling of expected energy bill fluctuations to identify yearly operating costs for heat pumps compared to gas, oil, and propane systems. We provide estimates of how various rate options can impact these costs. Specifically, we analyze the bill impacts of a technology-specific seasonal rate, time of use rate, and demand charges to identify potential customer savings under these rates.

### Methodology

For our analysis, we modeled annual HVAC operational costs for an all-electric ccASHP system fully replacing a gas, propane, and fuel oil furnace heating system and air conditioner. The baseline home assumes a furnace efficiency of 80 percent, which is characteristic of the current National Laboratory of the Rockies ResStock Northeastern building stock (ResStock n.d., Energy Star n.d.).[^2] This modeling accounts for both the increase in winter electricity usage and increase in usage due to air conditioning in the summer. Appendix A provides more detail on the data and methodology used for this report.

Three scenarios are modeled:

1. **Existing electric rate structures** in each state compared to gas, oil, and propane. We obtained electric utility data for the largest utility in each state using the Arcadia Genability database (June 2024 to May 2025) and took gas, oil, and propane price data from the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) (Arcadia n.d.; U.S. EIA n.d. a; n.d. b).
2. **A hypothetical seasonal heat pump rate** that provides a 37 percent lower volumetric rate during the winter season, defined as November through April, and with no changes to summer volumetric rates. This 37 percent lower rate is based on the proposed rate design put forward by the Massachusetts Interagency Rates Working Group. Appendix A provides further details around this calculation.
3. **Three real-world examples**, two from Con Edison in New York and one from BGE in Maryland, that model how technology-neutral demand charges and time of use rates affect heat pump customers’ bills. Appendix A provides further details on these calculations.

#### Key Caveats

There are some key caveats in the data that are important to note. First, the analysis focuses on operational costs, i.e., utility bills, and therefore does not model the initial upfront costs of heat pump installation. These costs for whole-house equipment and installations can range from $12,000 to $23,000 (Booth et al. 2024). Some states in the Northeast offer significant incentives including financing options and rebates that can reduce upfront costs for market-rate customers and provide heat pumps at no cost for income-qualified residents (e.g., Massachusetts) (MA EEAC n.d.). Additionally, the team did not model bill impacts of switching from electric resistance heat to heat pumps because heat pumps always cost less to operate than electric resistance heating, as they use the same fuel source much more efficiently. A recent study found savings of $1,200 to $7,000 per year for electric resistance customers in the Northeast (Booth et al. 2024). Further studies have found that air source heat pumps can reduce electricity use for heating by up to 75 percent compared to electric resistance heating systems (U.S. DOE n.d. a). Finally, the heat pump modeled in this analysis is a ducted, cold climate variable speed air source heat pump (HSPF2 9.2 and SEER2 21). While we did not model ductless heat pumps, the same data can apply to both ducted and ductless systems as these systems can reach similar levels of efficiency.

## Section 2: Rate Design Options Favorable to Customers with Heat Pumps

Many types of modern rate structures can better align heat pump customers’ bills with their costs to the grid and correct the overpayment issues under the standard two-part rate. Both heat pump specific rates and broader, modern technology-neutral rates can correct this by bringing bills closer to representing the actual costs customers place on the grid.

Regulators and utilities have several options for aligning rate design with heat pump adoption, as shown in Figure 4. These include establishing a dedicated rate class for heat pump or electric heating customers, adjusting fixed or volumetric charges, and introducing demand charges or event-based pricing. These approaches can be implemented individually or in combination, depending on local market conditions and policy objectives. One important limitation applies across all the options. In restructured states, which include many in the Northeast and Mid-Atlantic, regulators and utilities can directly influence transmission and distribution rates, but changes to supply rates require greater coordination with third party suppliers (Brown, Cosgrove, and Miller 2025).

This section describes each option and its trade-offs. Section 3 then examines how states have applied them in practice.

### Customer Rate Class

Utilities and regulators use cost of service studies to allocate costs among customer classes (residential, commercial, and industrial), on the theory that customers with similar usage patterns cost the same to serve (Akhmetov, Popov, and Primbet 2023). One option is to establish heat pump as their own class, conduct a cost of service study to identify what it costs to serve them, and then choose how to collect those costs through fixed charges, seasonal rates, TOU rates, or another design (Lee et al. 2026; Chernick et al. 2020). This approach differs from creating a subclass within the existing residential class—the approach examined in the remainder of this section—which does not require a separate cost of service study.

[PDF p. 21 | printed p. 20]

While customers that use heat pumps may have a different load pattern than current residential customers, there are some barriers to this approach. Some states may not yet have enough heat pump customers to justify one. Defining a narrower class also means revisiting class composition and eligibility, keeping bills transparent and understandable, deciding how to classify customers with hybrid heating systems, and tracking new installations and equipment changes as technologies emerge (NYS DPS n.d.; Bowen and Simeone 2025). These steps take time and can prove complex.

A separate class also has a limit. Switchbox finds that a dedicated heat pump delivery charge would address the overpayment heat pump customers experience under New York’s current rate structure (Lee et al. 2026). But because a technology-specific rate changes how costs are allocated and not when customers use electricity, it does little on its own to encourage load shifting (Bertrand et al. 2023).

### Fixed Charges

The fixed charge portion of a customer’s bill is a flat fee for each billing period that is meant to represent all fixed fees incurred by the utility. Experts, regulators, and utilities have debated extensively what components of a bill to include in the fixed charge (Lazar and Gonzalez 2015). Most often, fixed charges are set to cover both the costs of customer service and maintenance of grid infrastructure as regulators and utilities find a balance between these two ideologies in utility regulatory proceedings (Borden et al. 2023).

#### Increasing Customer Fixed Charges

Increasing customer fixed charges, i.e., customer fees, would move more of the cost recovery from the volumetric charge into the fixed charge, lowering per-kWh prices and benefiting heat pump customers (Dammel, Henchen, and Shah 2024). Figure 5 shows that Massachusetts’s Long-Term Ratemaking Study found that pairing a TOU rate with a higher fixed charge, $40 to $100 per month instead of $7, lowers monthly bills for fully electrified homes, making full-home electrification more cost-competitive (MA IRWG 2025).

Utilities may also favor increasing fixed charges because it reduces the risk that lower sales will result in reduced revenue (Whited, Woolf, and Daniel 2016). Because increasing fixed charges results in lower volumetric prices, it can reduce customers’ ability to cut their bills through efficiency and weaken the price signal to conserve energy. #### Income Graduated Fixed Charges

Instead of raising fixed charges on all customers, regulators could consider the more equitable choice of an income graduated fixed charge (IGFC). An IGFC creates customer tiers where the fixed charge increases as income increases. California recently implemented an IGFC with tiers based on income and whether a customer lives in deed-restricted housing (California PUC 2024a).

### Volumetric Rate Modifications

The volumetric charge portion of the bill, which charges customers in cents per kWh, increases with higher usage—setting the price signal for economically efficient electricity use (Baatz 2017). A portion of transmission and distribution charges are often included in the volumetric portion of a customer’s bill. This implies that the more energy a customer uses, the higher their volumetric rate, and the more they are contributing to grid infrastructure costs. This is not fully reflective of actual costs to provide the energy as timing of usage also plays into how customers place costs on the grid. Additionally, costs to provide energy change seasonally, with states in the Northeast and Mid-Atlantic having grids that cost less to provide energy in the winter compared to the summer. This section identifies alternative volumetric rate structures that could better align heat pump customer bills with the costs that they place onto the grid.

#### Seasonal Rates

Seasonal rates are cost reflective because they charge different volumetric prices in the winter and summer months to better align cost of service in those months. In the Northeast, seasonal rates would result in lower winter rates because the grid is summer peaking and there is lower cost to serve customers in the winter. This lowers overall operational costs of heat pumps and better aligns customer bills with grid costs (Malinowski, Sussman et al. 2025).

Seasonal rates do not require advanced metering infrastructure (AMI) use or require additional behavioral changes by customers. Further, seasonal rates can apply to either all customers, electric heating customers, or just heat pump users. Additionally, if regulators deploy seasonal rates for all electric heating customers, this lowers bills for electric resistance customers, who are overwhelmingly low-income and overpaying into the grid like heat pump customers (Lee et al. 2026).

[^6]: The Energy Information Administration’s 2020 Residential Energy Consumption Survey (RECS) shows that lower-income customers in the Northeast and Mid-Atlantic tend to consume less energy than higher-income customers (EIA 2024).

[PDF p. 23 | printed p. 22]

A seasonal rate might be a temporary rather than a permanent solution. As heat pump adoption increases, states throughout the region are expected to shift to winter peaking. So, any deployment of seasonal rates should include a way to monitor grid conditions and update rates as this shift occurs. Massachusetts is one state that has recognized technology-specific seasonal rates for heat pumps as a near-term solution and is now considering TOU rates as a next step (MA DPU 2026c).

#### Declining Block Rates

Block rates charge a different per-kWh rate based on the amount (kWh) of electricity a customer consumes during a billing period. Declining block rates decrease the volumetric rate as consumption increases and inclining block rates increase the volumetric rate as consumption increases (Brown, Cosgrove, and Miller 2025). Declining block rates are a heat pump friendly rate as they can lower operational costs for homes that use higher amounts of electricity. However, declining block rates can lower the efficiency signal in volumetric rates by encouraging usage instead of deterring it.

Some of the benefits of block rates are similar to those of seasonal rates. First, they can be deployed without AMI. Additionally, they are easier for consumers to understand and benefit from, as block rates will lower rates for customers without additional steps or any changes in behavior. Finally, block rates can be designed for only heat pump customers or for all electric customers, depending on the priorities of the state.

#### Time of Use Rates

Time of use (TOU) rates change the volumetric charge to increase it during daily peak periods and decrease it during the rest of the day. While the main purpose for creating TOU rates is incentivizing load shifting away from daily peak periods, heat pump users benefit as well. Because heat pumps have a consistent energy usage pattern, their total cost to operate is averaged out over peak and off-peak periods. This can be particularly advantageous for winter heating, when nighttime temperatures (and heating demand) are often lowest, coinciding with off-peak periods under many TOU rate structures (Sergici, Ramakrishnan et al. 2023).

TOU rates better align customer price signals with costs on the grid, but they do require AMI and customer engagement to be successful. It is important to ensure that consumers know when and how to shift their load to avoid peak periods. Technology and advances in AI can help shift load for customers as an alternative or in addition to AMI. TOU rates also benefit customers aside from those that use heat pumps and can be deployed as a broader initiative to modernize rates (Brown, Cosgrove, and Miller 2025). As loads grow and utilities seek ways to accommodate them, TOU rates offer a way to shift loads to off peak periods, leading to more efficient usage of existing transmission and distribution infrastructure and lower costs for all ratepayers.

### Demand Charges

Demand charges look to replace volumetric rates (measured in kWh) with charges for customers’ demand (measured in kW). They typically apply to a customer’s maximum demand for any 15- to 60-minute interval period over the course of the month (Sergici, Ramakrishnan et al. 2023). A demand charge can be based on a customer’s demand during set peak periods that are the same for all customers (coincident peak) or on the customer’s own highest demand whenever it occurs (non-coincident peak) (Lazar 2016).

[PDF p. 24 | printed p. 23]

Demand charges aim to align rates with the cost of the resources needed to meet peak demand. They address some of the pitfalls of volumetric charges, specifically that increased usage does not correlate with higher costs to the distribution and transmission systems. While not widely implemented, research has shown that demand charges can lead to lower bills for heat pump users because of the lower volumetric charge that results from reallocation of costs into demand charges (Sergici, Ramakrishnan et al. 2023).

There are some considerations for states looking to implement demand charges because demand charges require AMI, a customer outreach and engagement plan, and potentially modifications or additional rates to tackle grid peak (Brown, Cosgrove, and Miller 2025). Demand charges remain relatively uncommon for residential customers in the United States, in part because they are unfamiliar to many customers and require substantial educational efforts to explain (Sergici, Ramakrishnan et al. 2023). If implemented, it will be important to provide timely alerts to customers (Whited, Woolf, and Daniel 2016). In addition, a standard demand charge is based on a customer’s peak usage and not system peaks. To better reflect grid costs, jurisdictions can align peak windows with system peak or pair a demand charge with TOU rates (LeBel and Weston 2020).

### Event-Based Pricing

Event-based pricing encourages customers to shift load at times of peak demand during the year. While not important for Northeast and Mid-Atlantic states now, as the grid shifts to winter peaking, these rates can be used to enable demand flexibility and drive down grid costs. Unlike standard rates, which set prices in advance for fixed periods, event-based pricing is triggered only on a limited number of days when the grid is most strained, such as during times of extreme heat or cold. For heat pumps, these rates have been applied in situations where there is back up fuel and a need to reduce strain on the electric grid.

#### Critical Peak Pricing

Critical peak pricing (CPP) is an event-based pricing structure that increases prices during a few annual peak load periods with lower volumetric rates throughout the rest of the year (Brown, Cosgrove, and Miller 2025). Similar to TOU rates and demand charges, CPP can provide savings for heat pump customers because it provides lower volumetric rates throughout the rest of the year while increasing costs at peak times. CPP also aligns with drivers of grid costs, making rates more cost reflective. Deploying CPP can require investment in infrastructure such as AMI and potentially other advanced technology to respond to peak events.

In addition to potentially lower rates for heat pumps, CPP enables demand flexibility with heat pumps as they can serve as a price signal for customers to switch to a backup system during times of peak demand. While CPP programs lend themselves well to hybrid heating systems, they may not be good options for heat pump customers without backup fossil fuel heating systems. Customers with only heat pumps would have to turn off their system during peak pricing, which can be unsafe in extreme weather.

[PDF p. 25 | printed p. 24]

#### Peak Time Rebates

Peak time rebates (PTR) are an event-based pricing structure that provides a rebate to customers for decreasing their electricity usage during a select few annual peak periods (Brown, Cosgrove, and Miller 2025). While this strategy has not yet expressly been utilized for heat pumps by any Northeastern utility, it would work similarly to existing CPP programs and provides a less punitive option that could forgo the requirement of a backup heating system as there is no penalty associated with continued electricity use during peak periods under a PTR.

#### MASSACHUSETTS, Seasonal Heat Pump Rates

Massachusetts currently offers statewide seasonal heat pump specific rates, driven largely by the state’s climate goals and by affordability and rate design principles of cost causation and cost-reflectivity (MA DPU 2026b; MA IRWG 2024). Passed in 2021, Massachusetts state law requires a 50 percent reduction in greenhouse gas emissions by 2030 and for the state to be net zero by 2050. The law also directs the Department of Public Utilities (DPU) to prioritize greenhouse gas reductions to meet these climate goals (MA State Legislature 2021). The state’s utilities implement Mass Save, a nation-leading energy efficiency program that provides robust heat pump incentives to meet the program’s greenhouse gas reduction goals and heat pump targets (Miller 2025). Massachusetts also established an Interagency Rates Working Group, which included members from various government agencies, including the Executive Office of Energy and Environmental Affairs, Department of Energy Resources (DOER), the Massachusetts Clean Energy Center and the Attorney General’s Office (MA DOER 2025d). This working group held public-facing meetings to discuss the state’s key rate design challenges and identify short- and long-term recommendations. Based on its findings, the state decided to prioritize heat pump specific rates in the short-term and time of use rates in the long-term.

##### Heat Pump Rate 1.0

Massachusetts’ heat pump rates began as proposals in separate rate cases for two of the state’s electric utilities, and the rates were enacted statewide due to leadership from the DPU. First, in June 2024 under Docket 23-80, Unitil proposed and was approved to offer a seasonal heat pump distribution rate for residential and income-eligible customers (MA DPU 2024c). In September 2024, in Docket DPU 23150 [sic], National Grid proposed an Electrification Rate that that [sic] moved distribution charges to the fixed portion of the bill. The DPU rejected this proposal, finding that a flat distribution rate would disincentive efficiency and benefit both electric resistance and heat pump customers, which did not align with the state climate and beneficial electrification goals (MA DPU 2024a). Instead, the DPU ordered National Grid to develop a seasonal heat pump distribution rate similar to Unitil’s (MA DPU 2024a).

[PDF p. 28 | printed p. 27]

In January 2025, DOER petitioned the DPU to investigate whether all electric utilities in the state should offer a seasonal heat pump rate (MA DPU 2025f). As a result, DPU required Eversource to file a heat pump rate for the 2025-2026 heating season and began an investigation into a “Heat Pump Rate 2.0” to be implemented in the 2026-2027 heating season to better align with state policy priorities (MA DPU 2024b; 2025a). As a response to this order, in April 2025, Eversource filed a proposed seasonal heat pump rate that adjusted both transmission and distribution volumetric charges in the winter and summer months (MA DPU 2025b). In July 2025, the DPU approved the proposed decrease in winter transmission and distribution rates but rejected the proposed increase in summer rates. The DPU determined it was acceptable to also reduce transmission rates because transmission costs are usually determined in relation to system peak demand, which typically occurs in the summer. Therefore, lowering transmission rates mitigates the cost of increased electric usage while remaining cost-reflective (MA DPU 2025d). The DPU also required that Eversource maintain their current electric heating rate, to ensure existing and new customers with electric heat are paying a discounted distribution charge that aligns with their grid costs. With this approval, all electric distribution customers could enroll in a seasonal heat pump rate for the 2025-2026 heating season. See Table 5 below for details on the Heat Pump Rate 1.0, which shows the difference between the base distribution rate and seasonal heat pump distribution rate of 64-66 percent and the transmission rate for Eversource, which was also altered 66 percent. DOER estimates these rates could save customers roughly $70 million (MA DOER 2026b).

A final important feature of this process was the fact that customers were automatically enrolled and all customers, whether heat pumps provided partial or whole-home heat, were able to enroll. Because the rates simply provide a winter discount, all customers who enroll will pay less compared to their standard bill. Additionally, because there was no requirement that the heat pump provide whole-home heat, all customers who had received a heat pump rebate from the state were likely eligible. This enabled the state to automatically enroll customers that participated in their energy efficiency program, Mass Save. Customers who purchased heat pumps without Mass Save rebates may self-attest that they have a heat pump to enroll in the rate. Any customer that received a heat pump rebate through Mass Save was automatically enrolled. In the first year, this led to more than 100,000 residents enrolling in the rate (Driscoll and Healey 2025). This approach is administratively simple for both utilities and customers.

#### Low-Income Customer Considerations

Massachusetts offers their heat pump rates to low-income customers and provides low-income assistance programs. These programs include low-income discount rates, payment plans, arrearage management plans, and the Home Energy Assistance Program (HEAP) (DPU Consumer Information n.d.). Massachusetts also recently implemented a statewide five-tier discount rate structure for customers of electric and gas utilities (Commonwealth of Massachusetts n.d.). Each tier has a unique discount designed to target a specific energy burden for customers. Coupled with the seasonal heat pump rate, a tiered discount rate can serve to eliminate some of the disincentives for low-income customers who want to adopt heat pumps as it can lower the energy burden for customers.

#### Long-term Transition to Default TOU Rates

For Massachusetts, the work of the Interagency Rates Working Group helped to identify heat pump rates as a near-term solution and TOU rates as a viable long-term solution once deployment of AMI has taken place statewide (MA IRWG 2024; MA IRWG 2025). In ordering seasonal heat pump rates, Massachusetts DPU also recognized that a seasonal heat pump rate might not be preferable in the long run as grid shifts to winter peaking and more customers adopt heat pumps. In April 2026, the Massachusetts Department of Energy Resources (DOER) filed a petition asking the DPU to investigate implementation of statewide electric default TOU rates by 2028 so that the state can consider the implementation and pathway to TOU rates, alongside implementation of heat pump rates (MA DPU 2026f).

- **Regulatory leadership can drive adoption of seasonal heat pump rates.** Strong regulatory leadership and mandates can create quick, impactful changes. Seasonal heat pump specific rates are relatively quick to enact if they use a revenue-neutral design and do not require a cost allocation study. In

- **Automatic enrollment strategies can enable access to affordable rates.** Massachusetts deployed automatic enrollment by enrolling all customers that had received a heat pump rebate through the Mass Save program. Leveraging the Mass Save program and using automatic enrollment helped accelerate adoption, unlocking savings for over 100,000 customers. - **Seasonal heat pump rates might not be a long-term solution.** These rates align with near-term climate and affordability goals but might not be a long-term solution as the grid shifts to winter peaking. With evolving grid conditions, state regulators may begin transitioning towards more cost-reflective rate designs that encourage flexibility and peak-load management. Massachusetts has opened a time-varying rates proceeding to plan the state’s next phase of rates (MA DPU 2026f). Other states adopting technology-specific rates should plan a similar path, which could be TOU rates or another modern rate structure.

#### MAINE, Block Rates, Seasonal Rates, and Increased Fixed Charges

Maine has ambitious climate and heat pump deployment goals. In 2019, Maine established mandatory greenhouse gas emissions reduction targets for the state of 45 percent reduction by 2030, carbon neutrality by 2045, and 80 percent reduction by 2050 (Maine State Legislature 2019b). In 2019, An Act to Transform Maine’s Heat Pump Market established the goals of installing 100,000 heat pumps in Maine by 2025 (Maine State Legislature 2019a). Efficiency Maine Trust (EMT) met this goal two years ahead of schedule and now aims for the higher goal of an additional 175,000 by 2027 pursuant to a new goal set by the governor in 2024.

> **What Is Beneficial Electrification?**
>
> When electrification measures increase electricity sales without adding to the peak demand that drives new infrastructure investment, those fixed costs are spread across more kilowatt-hours, putting downward pressure on rates for all customers.

_Extractor note (not source text): the blockquote above is a sidebar set in the right column of PDF p. 31, beside the next paragraph._

In 2023, Maine passed the Beneficial Electrification Policy Act: this law directs EMT to develop beneficial electrification plans in their statewide energy efficiency plans. The state defines beneficial electrification as a program that reduces or replaces fossil fuel usage; provides a benefit to a utility, ratepayer, or the environment; does not cause harm to utilities, ratepayers, or the environment; and improves the efficiency of the electric grid or reduces consumer costs or emission, including carbon (Maine State Legislature 2026). This includes measures that increase electricity consumption while avoiding growth in peak [PDF p. 32 | printed p. 31] load, thereby distributing fixed transmission and distribution costs across a larger volume of sales and exerting downward pressure on rates without necessitating additional infrastructure investment (EMT 2025a; Chen et al. 2026).

Maine utilities offer a variety of rates designed to reduce costs for heat pump customers, customers that electrify, and customers that can shift their load. Additionally, like Massachusetts, the state has an open docket on deploying default TOU rates (MPUC 2024). This case study focuses on the design and impacts of the state’s recent heat pump and electric heating rates: Central Maine Power (CMP)’s Electric Technology (ET) Rate, Seasonal Heat Pump (SHP) Rate, and Versant’s Home Heating Eco Rate (HHER) (CMP n.d.; Donenfeld 2023; Versant Power 2026).

##### Central Maine Power—Seasonal Heat Pump and Electric Technology Rates

For Central Maine Power, the impetus for creating and testing out new rate structures started in 2021 when legislation (P.L. 2021 ch. 298 §7 and P.L. 2021, ch. 244 §2) directed the MPUC to open an inquiry (Case 2021-00198) to explore the role that policy goals might play in influencing modern rate design (Maine State Legislature 2021a; 2021b; 2022). Later in the year, the Commission opened Case 2021-00325 to investigate T&D rate design to promote state policies (MPUC 2021). This investigation eventually led to CMP piloting both their Residential Electric Technology (ET) Rate and their Residential Seasonal Heat Pump Rate (SHP) Rate in 2023. These rates were implemented as two years pilots, but were both extended. The SHP rate ended at the end of 2025 and the current ET rate has a termination date of January 31, 2027, “unless otherwise renewed or modified by the Company and approved by the Commission.” The commission’s consideration of these issues is ongoing and will continue through two open proceedings, Docket Nos. 2026-00264 and 2026-00248 (CMP 2026b; MPUC 2026).

- The ET rate provides a reduced delivery rate for customers that use more electricity because of electric technologies (heat pumps, EVs, and batteries). While it provides a lower distribution rate of 3 cents, or 25.9 percent, the ET rate has a monthly fixed charge significantly higher than the default electric rate ($41.24 versus $27.37) (CMP n.d.).
- The SHP rate had a fixed charge of around $40, over $13 higher than the default electric rate, and changed the volumetric rates in the winter and summer: almost 10 cents lower in the winter (November-April) and 17 cents higher in the summer (May-October). This is a key difference between the SHP rate and the heat pump rates in Massachusetts. The Massachusetts rates did not raise the summertime volumetric charges, so customers were guaranteed to benefit. However, for the SHP rate customers had no such guarantee.

CMP contracted Demand Side Analytics to evaluate the impact of the ET and SHP rates during pilot periods. It found that 1) customers on the ET rate did not increase or decrease their annual consumption, but those on the SHP rate increased electric consumption by about 1.4 percent in the heating season and 1.1 percent in the cooling season; 2) neither rate increased or decreased demand during summer or winter peaking periods; 3) ET customers paid an average of $200 less annually than default rate customers and SHP customers paid an [PDF p. 33 | printed p. 32] average of $100 more annually than default rate customers (CMP 2025). A customer satisfaction survey found that customers on the ET and SHP rates are more satisfied with their rate than those on the standard rate (Demand Side Analytics 2026).

##### Versant – Home Heating Eco Rate

Versant has been offering electric heating rates for years (Donenfeld 2023). The Home Heating Eco Rate (HHER) is a seasonal declining block rate, which provides a lower volumetric rate on electricity usage over 700 kWh during winter months only (Versant Power 2026). Under the HHER rate, all energy usage above 700 kWh receives a volumetric rate discount of 6.6 cents less than usage below 700 kWh ($0.115 versus $0.04.99 [sic] per kWh), representing 57 percent in savings (Versant Power 2026). The monthly fixed charge remains the same for both seasons at $11.54 and includes the first 100 kWh of usage. In the summer, all electricity is billed at the same volumetric rate.

This rate is available to customers with electric resistance space heating, heat pumps, heat pump water heaters, or electric storage space heating that can provide 50 percent or more of the home’s heating load. In filings, Versant discusses the decision to include all types of electric thermal heating, including electric resistance heat, as eligible measures for the HHER to promote equitable access to modern rate structures (Versant Power 2026). Versant notes that electric resistance heating may be more common in low- and moderate-income households. They explain that it is more equitable to offer the same seasonal rate to both heat pump customers and to customers with less efficient electric resistance systems who cannot overcome the upfront cost barrier of switching to heat pumps (MPUC 2021).

##### Customer Enrollment

At most recent reporting, CMP had approximately 5,900 customers enrolled in the ET rate and 2,800 customers enrolled in the SHP rate pilots. These numbers represent approximately 1 percent and 0.5 percent of the residential customer base, respectively. The customer participation rate in Versant’s HHER is not publicly available. The ET and SHP rate low participation levels are in line with LBNL’s research that found participation rates in opt-in utility rates ranged from 3 to 30 percent (Carvallo and Schwartz 2023). This trend suggests that even when favorable rates exist, like the ET rate, they can fail to gain traction without a marketing campaign to inform and enroll customers. Without a comprehensive marketing, education, and outreach plan to translate technical rate design into actionable customer signals, it will be more difficult to drive significant customer adoption. Moving forward, the focus must shift toward ensuring that rate design is not only technically sound but also paired with an authorized communication strategy that bypasses these historical legal limitations to effectively reach customers (Reardon 2026).

#### Takeaways

- **If pursuing heat pump rates, design rates that provide a clear benefit to heat pump customers.** Maine utilities have tried several approaches to lowering operating costs for heat pump customers, with mixed results: declining block rates, seasonal rates that are lower in winter and higher in summer, and higher fixed charges paired with lower distribution rates. Versant has long offered a declining block electric heating rate that discounts distribution charges by 57 percent, giving immediate savings [PDF p. 34 | printed p. 33] to homes heating with either electric resistance or heat pumps. CMP, by contrast, piloted two rate designs that raised some bill components while lowering others. Studies found the ET rate lowered bills by $200, while the SHP rate raised them by $100. Heat pump rates work best when customers can clearly see the savings, as with Versant’s rate, which directly lowers volumetric charges without increasing other parts of the bill.
- **Heat pump rates might counteract electrification’s potential to put downward pressure on rates.** Maine law recognizes that beneficial electrification, when it doesn’t add to peak demand, can lower rates for all customers by spreading transmission and distribution (T&D) costs over more electricity sales. Technology-specific heat pump rates aim to correct heat pump customers’ overpayment of T&D costs by lowering their rates to better reflect what it actually costs to serve them. However, because these rates direct the savings only to heat pump customers, they forgo the opportunity for beneficial electrification to lower rates for everyone (Chen et al. 2026).
- **Heat pump favorable rates can also help electric resistance customers.** Versant’s heat pump rate began as an electric heating rate and has expanded to include heat pumps. Unlike Massachusetts, it offers the same discount to both electric resistance and heat pump heating customers. This is an equitable approach because it enables residents with electric heating to have the same benefit as those that have heat pumps. Extending the discount to all electric heating customers can also ease energy burden for those who can’t easily switch to heat pumps, such as low-income residents and renters, who are more likely to rely on electric resistance heat.

### Technology-Neutral Rates: Blending Heating Electrification Goals With Maintaining Availability to All Customers

For some states, a technology-specific rate design might not align with regulatory precedent or policy goals. These states can turn to technology-neutral rates, which are open to all customers and bring bills closer to grid costs. The options include higher fixed charges, seasonal rates, TOU rates, demand charges, critical peak pricing, and peak time rebates. Because these designs lower volumetric charges or reward steady, off-peak usage, they benefit heat pump customers along with other ratepayers. Which rate design to choose depends on the technology available to the state, when the grid is peaking, how regulators want to allocate costs, and ease of customer usage. As summarized in Table 7 below, case studies from New York, Maryland, and California show three approaches: a demand charge, a time of use rate, and an income graduated fixed charge.

#### NEW YORK: A Closer Look at Demand Charges

New York is another state that has both climate goals and heat pump adoption goals. New York’s Climate Leadership and Community Protection Act (CLCPA), enacted in 2019, mandates a 40 percent reduction in greenhouse gas emissions by 2030 and at least 85 percent by 2050 (relative to 1990 levels) as well as a requirement for 100 percent zero-emission electricity by 2040. New York is also committed to driving heat pump adoption. New York’s Clean Heat Program is a program targeted at accelerating heat pump deployment in the state. It sets targets for heat pump deployment for each utility and establishes a statewide platform where customers can find available financial incentives, connect with participating contractors, and learn more about heat pump technologies. Unlike Massachusetts and Maine, New York Department of Public Services has chosen to not prioritize technology-specific rates, finding that rate design should remain technology-agnostic and centered on long-term value (NYS DPS n.d.). Con Edison, a utility that serves New York City and the surrounding area, has deployed a demand charge rate that provides significant discounts for homes that heat with heat pumps.

##### Con Edison Steady Use Rate

In keeping with the principle of technology-agnostic rates, Con Edison created a technology-agnostic demand charge rate known as the Steady Use Rate. The rate is available to all customers and provides significant savings to heat pump users as the rate charges customer per kW, increasing during peak times of the day. This technology-neutral, cost-reflective structure lets Con Edison charge customers accurately for their electricity usage regardless of the technology they use (Attanasio et al. 2026). This provides substantial savings to customers with stable load profiles and relatively high volumetric consumption.

#### MARYLAND: Time of Use Rates That Benefit Heat Pump Users

Maryland’s climate strategy is anchored by the Climate Solutions Now Act of 2022, which sets near-term climate targets of a 60 percent reduction in greenhouse gas emissions by 2031 and net zero by 2045 (Maryland General Assembly 2022). In 2024, the state passed the Distributed Renewable Integration and Vehicle Electrification (DRIVE) Act. This law required Maryland utilities to develop vehicle-to-grid charging, virtual power plants, and time of use rates. In 2025, the PSC issued an order directing utilities to file opt-in TOU rates. The PSC was looking to enact rates that helped clean energy technology across the utility portfolio and promote load shifting, peak flattening, and customer bill savings (MD PSC 2017).

Similar to Con Edison, BGE offers a technology-neutral rate that provides additional savings for heat pump customers. As a result of the DRIVE Act and PUC mandate, Baltimore Gas and Electric (BGE) offers an opt-in time of use (TOU) rate. The current BGE Seasonal TOU Rate offers peak and off-peak volumetric rates, and the peak times change by season. As shown in Section 1, this rate is projected to lower operational costs for [PDF p. 38 | printed p. 37] heat pump users by an additional $650 per year [source discrepancy: Table 3 (PDF p. 18) gives BGE TOU savings of $410 per year]. As of 2025, this rate had relatively low customer uptake—approximately 6.5 percent of residential customers used the rate (MD PSC 2025). This low uptake rate is consistent with NEEP’s findings for opt-in rate participation (Brown, Cosgrove, and Miller 2025).

##### Takeaways

- **Heat pump friendly rates can align with grid flexibility goals.** BGE’s rate was designed as a response to legislation that looked to enable grid flexibility and prioritize energy reduction during times of peak load. Similar to New York, the benefits to heat pump customers were not a priority in design. Yet heat pump customers benefit because of the rates alignment with actual grid costs. Heat pump friendly rates do not need to prioritize heat pump deployment, most modern rate structures look to align ratepayer charges with actual grid costs, increasing costs at time of peak usage or demand and reducing it for other times of the day, which can benefit heat pump users.
- **Rate design can be driven by legislation.** Passage of the DRIVE Act triggered the proceeding to design and implement TOU rates. While rates ultimately have to be implemented by regulators, legislation can trigger action as opportunities to change rates typically only occur when a utility files a rate case or a commission opens its own investigation. Legislation can spur action by regulators and utilities through providing a clear mandate and timeline.

#### CALIFORNIA: Income Graduated Fixed Charge

Like the Northeast, in California rates have increased faster than inflation and are among the highest electricity rates throughout the country. Researchers cite wildfire mitigation, liability insurance, shrinking demand, and higher utility return on equity (ROE) than national averages as drivers of these high costs (Barbose et al. 2025; Chhabra 2025; Brinn and Chhabra 2024). Additionally, the state has strong climate goals, which drive electrification programs offered throughout the state. To align with these goals, the California State Legislature passed Assembly Bill 205 in 2022, which authorized the California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC) to create fixed charges “to be established on an income-graduated basis” (CA State Legislature 2022). Similar to Maryland, legislation drove this rate change.

The CPUC approved an income graduated fixed charge (IGFC) that took effect for SCE and SDG&E customers in late 2025 and for PG&E customers in early 2026 (Christopher 2024). Prior to this proceeding, California IOUs largely did not utilize fixed charges for residential customers. The establishment of a fixed charge allowed for a decrease in volumetric rates of by 5-7 cents per kWh for all residential customers (CA PUC 2024a), benefitting heat pump and EV customers. The CPUC estimated these new rates could save electrified customers $28 to $44 a month. California also embedded equity in the design of the fixed charges. The resulting structure includes a fixed charge for non-low-income customers of $24.15 per month and $12 per month for low- [PDF p. 39 | printed p. 38] income customers.[^11] Further, if a customer is enrolled in the California Alternate Rates for Energy low-income assistance program, this fee is discounted to $6 per month. This ensures that low-income households and households with a higher energy burden do not pay disproportionally more into the energy grid (Brown, Cosgrove, and Miller 2025).

##### Takeaways

- **IGFC will advance heat pump adoption while preserving volumetric rates’ energy efficiency incentives.** While the impetus for the CPUC to create the IGFC was not explicitly related to heat pumps, electrification goals were part of the discussion (Walton 2024). Increasing funding collected through the fixed charge, is one tool available to utility regulators seeking to lower volumetric rates. Additionally, an income graduated fixed charge does not eliminate the efficiency incentive embedded in volumetric electricity rates.
- **IGFC can address inequities inherent in rate design.** Establishing an income graduated fixed charge bases the fixed charge of consumers’ bills on their income. This makes bills more equitable by attempting to remedy the disproportionately higher percentage of income that lower-income customers pay into the grid (Chhabra et al. 2024). One of the reasons for this adoption was that current rate-adjacent programs do not tackle core issues of high energy costs and the regressive nature of energy bills (CA PUC 2024b).

#### HYDRO-QUÉBEC: Hybrid Electric and Gas Heating Rate

Québec’s government has committed to reducing greenhouse gas emissions by 37.5 percent by 2035 and to being carbon neutral by 2050 (Gouvernement du Québec 2026). Additionally, the province has strong climate goals. But unlike states in the Northeast and Mid-Atlantic, Quebec currently experiences a winter peaking grid, and their heat pump focused rates looks to reduce this peak demand through a dual fuel strategy. Rate DT was driven by the unique challenge of managing a winter-peaking utility where winter load is already twice as high as summer load.

##### Hydro-Québec—Rate DT

Hydro-Québec, an electric utility serving Québec, Canada, offers Rate DT, an electric rate for residential and commercial dual-fuel customers. This rate is designed to benefit both customers and the utilities by prioritizing heat pumps during times of mild weather and lowering electric consumption during times of extreme weather events. This works to reduce strain on the grid and prioritize reduction of greenhouse gas emissions during milder weather (Harvey 2026). Because over two-thirds of homes in Québec currently rely on electric resistance heating and the province is prioritizing heat pump adoption, there is significant pressure to limit electric peaks (Statistics Canada 2023).

Rate DT offers customers a significantly lower electricity price[^12] (approximately $0.05 CAD/kWh) when the outdoor temperature is above 10°F or 5°F, depending on location. When temperatures drop below the threshold, the rate increases to roughly $0.29 CAD/kWh to signal the automatic switch to an auxiliary fuel system, such as a gas furnace. Hydro-Québec projects that average customers who heat with gas or oil can expect to save approximately $500 CAD per year upon switching to Rate DT (Hydro Québec n.d. b).

Rather than focusing exclusively on heat pumps, the rate is available to customers who use any electric heating technology, which is important as approximately 54 percent of Québec households heat with electric resistance baseboard heating and only 16 percent use heat pumps (Statistics Canada 2025). ## Section 4: Identifying the Right Rate Design

Section 2 explains how different rate designs work and Section 3 looks at how they have been implemented. This section will focus on key considerations for aligning rate design with heat pump use to help regulators, utilities, and other stakeholders choose a rate design that fits their near- and long-term goals. We begin with the first decision regulators and utilities face: whether to pursue a technology-specific or technology-neutral rate. We then cover design and implementation considerations that apply only to technology-specific rates, and close with considerations that apply to any heat pump friendly rate design.

### Technology-Specific or Technology-Neutral Rate

As our analysis shows, both technology-specific and technology-neutral rates can lower heat pump operating costs and better align customer bills with grid costs. Technology-specific rates, such as seasonal heat pump rates, deliver immediate savings without behavior change, can be implemented on a faster timeline, and align with climate and heat pump adoption goals. They are also justified because heat pumps have a usage pattern that current rate design does not accommodate, i.e., increased load in the winter season, when the grid has excess capacity. Technology-neutral rates, such as time of use rates and demand charges, are open to all customers and stay aligned with grid costs as conditions change.

#### Considerations for Technology-Specific Rates

States that choose a technology-specific rate, available only to heat pump or electric heating customers, face several additional decisions around eligibility, what bill components the rate affects, and a long-term transition strategy. These choices determine which customers qualify and how much their bills change.

- **Bill Components Affected by the Rate:** Seasonal rates and declining block winter rates can apply different parts of a customer’s bill, including transmission, distribution, and policy charges. In Massachusetts, the seasonal heat pump rate for National Grid and Unitil initially only applied to the distribution charge. For the 2026-2027 winter season, the DPU ordered that all utilities adjust both transmission and distribution charges (MA DPU 2026b). While Massachusetts chose not to lower policy charges, such as energy efficiency and net metering programs, regulators and utilities can extend the rate to them as well (Murray and Velez 2025). Because these charges are currently a flat volumetric rate, the added electricity use from heat pumps can lead to customers paying disproportionally more into these programs (Murray and Velez 2025).
- **Customer Eligibility:** Implementing technology-specific rates involved considerations on eligibility: whether the rate applies to all electric heating customers or only heat pump customers, whether the whole home or only part of it must be heated with the device, and whether the rate applies to all energy used in the home or just the technology.
  - **Heat Pump Only Rates or Electric Heating Rates:** While heat pump specific rates can help accelerate heat pump adoption and provide a deeper discount, electric heating rates, which [PDF p. 43 | printed p. 42] apply to both heat pumps and electric resistance heating, can also be part of this strategy. Rates that apply to all customers that heat with electric heat also address the issues of cost reflectivity and cost causation, ensuring customers’ bills align with the cost to serve. Utilities have offered seasonal rates for electric heating since the 1960s because of this fact (Luzerne Electric Division 1963). Electric heating rates can also address a significant equity concern—that heat pumps are not viable for all customers, both because of their high upfront costs and for renters, who have no control of their heating sources.
  - **Whole or Partial Home Heating Required:** Technology-specific rates must also specify whether a home needs to be fully or only partially heated by heat pumps, a decision that affects how many customers are eligible and how they enroll. A whole-home requirement means the utility will need a way to determine and verify eligible customers. Expanding eligibility to customers who partially heat with heat pumps can, depending on the definition used, streamline enrollment. In Massachusetts, the DPU made any customers with a heat pump eligible to streamline adoption and encourage adoption of heat pumps (MA DPU 2024c, 407). This allowed utilities to automatically enroll customers who received rebates through Mass Save, resulting in 140,000 customers enrolling in the first year (Wasser 2026). Rates with a threshold for eligibility, such as the 50 percent requirement for Versant’s Home Heating Eco Rate, can require extra steps to verify eligibility, such as reviewing past customer bills or electric usage (Gold-Parker, Malkani, and Picciano 2026).
  - **Heat Pump Usage Only or Whole-Home Usage:** A rate can apply to all of a home’s electricity or only to what the heat pump uses. Limiting it to heat pump usage allows a deeper discount, but many utilities cannot separate that usage from the rest of the home. Tracking heat pump usage alone could also require significant upfront investment in metering technology, which might undermine the goals of easy enrollment and lower costs (MNCEE 2023).
- **Identifying a Transition Strategy:** Technology-specific rates fit current conditions (a summer-peaking grid and heat pump customers who overpay in the winter), but they are a temporary tool for growing the heat pump market. As adoption spreads across the Northeast and Mid-Atlantic, winter electricity use is expected to grow, creating a new winter peak and higher net electricity usage in the winter (Gold-Parker, Malkani, and Picciano 2026). As a result, the cost to supply electricity in the winter will rise and rates will need to change. By signaling that heat pump rates are temporary and opening a proceeding to plan a transition to different rates, states can set customer expectations and give utilities certainty. Massachusetts has opened a time-varying rates proceeding to plan the state’s next phase of rates (MA DPU 2026f). Other states adopting technology-specific rates should plan a similar path, which could be TOU rates or another modern rate structure.

[PDF p. 44 | printed p. 43]

#### Considerations for Both Technology-Specific and Technology-Neutral Rates

Whether a state pursues a technology-specific or technology-neutral approach, several considerations apply to every rate design. These factors shape how many customers enroll, how easily they can benefit, and whether the savings reach the households that need them most.

- **Ease of Use of Selected Rate Structure:** The more complicated or unfamiliar a rate design is, the harder it can be to enroll customers. Some rate designs discussed in this paper require ongoing reminders for customers to change their behavior to avoid costly bills (CPP, TOU rates, demand charges) while others require less customer interaction (seasonal rates, block rates, and IGFC). For rates that require ongoing customer action, communicating, socializing, and reinforcing the needed behavior shifts will be important to the success of the rate and ability for customers to save money.
- **Enrollment and Marketing Strategies:** Enrollment strategies determine who enrolls a rate and, therefore, who is able to take advantage of the benefits the rate can provide. A heat pump specific rate often carries no downside for heat pump owners, as it is a discount on volumetric charges, which means states can consider automatic enrollment, as with the Massachusetts seasonal heat pump rate. For opt-in rate, a robust marketing, education, and outreach plan becomes the primary way to enroll customers with materials explaining what the rate is and how it benefits customers (Brown, Cosgrove, and Miller 2025).
- **Alignment With Low-Income Assistance Programs:** As states deploy heat pump or heat pump friendly rates, proceedings should consider how rates can lower energy burden for customers that need it the most. Massachusetts and California have both adopted low-income protections that direct the greatest savings to customers that need it the most. California’s income graduated fixed charge (IGFC), adopted by the California Public Utilities Commission in 2024, sets three levels of fixed charges, automatically assigning lower charges to customers based on their participation in state programs (CA PUC 2024a). Massachusetts DPU approved a low-income discount rate (LIDR), which offers five tiers of discounts depending on household income (Commonwealth of Massachusetts n.d.). Both approaches aim to reduce energy burden and remedy the disproportionately higher percentage of income that lower-income customers pay into the grid (Chhabra et al. 2024). They differ in two key ways. First, an IGFC only applies to the fixed charge, where the LIDR applies to the whole bill. Second, in some jurisdictions, low-income programs cap annual bill relief, after which customers pay the full rate for the rest of the year (RMI 2025; Commonwealth of Pennsylvania n.d.).

## Conclusion

As the data and case studies above demonstrate, heat pump friendly rates can take many forms. Options range from technology-specific rates designed to better reflect the costs of serving heat pump customers to technology-neutral rates that align bills more closely with grid costs while helping manage winter peak demand. The most effective approach depends on each state’s policy objectives, electricity system needs, and customer priorities.

Under current flat volumetric rates, homes that convert to heating with heat pumps from gas, oil, or propane pay more than their fair share of grid costs. Despite this rate design issue, our analysis shows that homes switching from propane or fuel oil save immediately, but homes that switch from gas see bills rise by about $100 a year on average, and by $500 or more where the spark gap is highest. Rate design can close this gap. A seasonal heat pump rate results in operational savings compared to gas in every state in the Northeast and Mid-Atlantic, averaging about $600 a year. Technology-neutral time of use and demand-based rates saved an average of $410 to $1,560 annually.

The case studies show there is no single approach. Massachusetts and Maine adopted technology-specific seasonal and block rates while New York and Maryland rely on technology-neutral rates. California adopted a fixed charge to lower volumetric rates, and Québec uses a dual-fuel rate to manage its winter peak. The right choice depends on a state’s policy goals around heat pumps, the grid, and equity. One universal lesson that does emerge is the need for consistent marketing, engagement, and enrollment practices. Opt-in rates have reached only a small share of eligible customers, while automatic enrollment in Massachusetts reached more than 140,000 as of spring 2026.

Implementing effective rate designs requires coordinated action by regulators, legislators, and utilities. Massachusetts regulators established a statewide seasonal heat pump rate, while legislation in Maryland helped drive the development of time of use rates that now benefit heat pump customers. These examples illustrate how both regulatory action and legislative initiatives can help align electricity prices with the need to electrify homes. This paper serves as a resource to help states navigate how to identify a heat pump friendly rate that best aligns with their policy goals and grid needs.

#### Real-World Example Rates

Two states were selected for further analysis, including a deeper dive into natural gas billing data and an investigation of special electric rate structures. Natural gas bills were collected from Baltimore Gas & Electric (BGE) and Consolidated Edison (Con Edison), representative of gas prices in the same utility territories as the selected real-world example electric rates.

[PDF p. 49 | printed p. 48]

The research team sourced monthly billing data from gas heating customers at BGE. To maintain a 12-month averaging period, this analysis used historical values from September 2024 through the most recent available bill from August 2025. The billing data included start and end dates for each billing period, total gas usage in therms, and total cost. It was therefore necessary to disaggregate these costs into variable and fixed fees to avoid artificially inflating volumetric price estimates. A linear regression of total bill amount vs. total therms (R<sup>2</sup> = 0.998) resulted in an estimated fixed fee of $15.85 per month over the 12-month period, using the intercept of the regression line at 0 therms. Subtracting this fixed fee estimate from the total bills from this period separates the portion of the total cost attributed solely to gas usage, which can then be divided by total therms to estimate a monthly $/therm price. These monthly volumetric cost estimates were then weighted by HDD65 from the same weather data used to weigh baseline rates, resulting in a single 12-month average of $1.70/therm, 9 percent higher than the baseline estimate from EIA residential pricing data.

For Con Edison, the team sourced monthly values from the Con Edison Gas Rates Summary document and current gas tariff (Con Edison 2026a; 2026b). Historical Con Edison gas bills were estimated from October 2024 through September 2025. Con Edison’s gas price history included a relatively high distribution charge of $1.92/therm applied in addition to their supply charges. By comparison, recent New York State Electric and Gas (NYSEG) and National Grid (Long Island) distribution charges were estimated at $0.80/therm and $2.05/therm, respectively, demonstrating a significant disparity in gas prices across the state. An HDD65-weighted 12-month average of total volumetric gas prices resulted in an estimate of $2.47/therm using Con Edison billing data. This estimate is 60 percent higher than the baseline average calculated from EIA data. As the EIA price history represents a statewide average, this can be attributed to the difference in gas prices between utilities serving the greater New York City metro area and those further outstate. #### Slipstream Modeling System Definitions:

We modeled a 2,030 square-foot single-family home, which is the average single-family square footage according to the Northeast ResStock dataset. The modeling assumes this same “basic home” across the board, preserving the same assumed levels of weatherization and home vintage. Variances in load and cost outcomes are thus due to varying climates, baseline furnace fuel type, and heat pump type. Weatherization levels were calibrated by comparing model output to the annual peak heating and cooling loads of ResStock homes with similar square footage in similar locations. For context, the average vintage of these ResStock homes is 1970.

We modeled a furnace efficiency of 80 percent, which is characteristic of the current ResStock Northeastern building stock, though furnaces currently being sold on the market average at a higher efficiency. We assumed the specified furnace’s Annual Fuel Utilization Efficiency (AFUE) remained constant across all temperatures, which is consistent with standard energy-modeling tools and furnace rating procedures.

The model used in this paper is built and run in the R programming language. Model inputs include weather, home, and HVAC system attributes. It then runs heat transfer and energy use calculations for each hour of the year and outputs hourly data and graphics. Key functions and underlying assumptions of this model are outlined in Table A6 below.

The loads represent a common ccASHP (HSPF2 9.2 and SEER2 21). This is above the baseline standard for the NEEP Cold Climate Air Source Heat Pump Specification for ducted systems (HSPF2 ≥ 7.7; SEER2 ≥ 14.3) (NEEP 2023). The air conditioning unit is a model with a SEER of 15. We have derated COPs by 25 percent for ACs, and 15 percent for the variable speed heat pump, based on the assumption that inverter-driven modulation decreases cycling, providing part-load efficiency boosts that support better real-world performance. These derates are based on field testing literature, which consistently shows field performance lagging behind manufacturer specified performance (i.e., system performance under laboratory testing procedures). For example, NEEP’s recent Residential Heat Pump Efficiency Rating Representativeness Project found that laboratory ratings overestimated field performance by 10 to 36 percent (Jaiswal, McWilliams, and Lis 2024).

Note that the deviation between manufacturer specifications and field performance varies. In high-quality installations the gap may be small, whereas systems with poor sizing, ductwork limitations, or suboptimal controls may underperform significantly. A recent field study of 12 variable-speed heat pumps illustrates this range, showing both close alignment and substantial divergence between measured and rated COP depending on site conditions. Our modeling approach is therefore designed to represent the typical direction and magnitude of underperformance observed across field studies, while acknowledging that real-world outcomes depend heavily on installation quality and system conditions.

Additionally, while the model used a ducted system, the results can be applied to ductless systems as these systems can reach similar levels of efficiency.
