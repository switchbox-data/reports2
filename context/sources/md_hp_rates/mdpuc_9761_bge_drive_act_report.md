## DRIVE Act Capital Deferral Potential July 1 Report

### 1. Executive Summary

In the 2024 Legislative Session, the Maryland General Assembly passed Senate Bill ("SB") 959, also known as the Distributed Renewable Integration and Vehicle Electrification ("DRIVE") Act. The DRIVE Act mandates that each investor-owned electric company submits a report to the Maryland Public Service Commission ("Commission") by July 1, 2026 evaluating (1) the potential to avoid or defer electric distribution system capital projects through the use of time-of-use ("TOU") rates, demand-response and demand-side programs, and renewable on-site generating systems; and (2) the merits and feasibility of transitioning all customers to a time-of-use tariff on an opt-out basis.[^1]

[^1]: Annotated Code of Maryland, Public Utilities Article ("PUA") § 7-1003(c).

Baltimore Gas and Electric Company ("BGE" or the "Company") submits the following report ("Report") in compliance with SB 959 and Commission Order No. 91218[^2] to illustrate the potential to avoid or defer electric distribution system capital projects through the use of TOU rates, demand response, demand-side programs, and renewable on-site generating and explain the merits and feasibility of transitioning all customers to a time-of-use tariff on an opt-out basis. BGE also seeks to clarify the complicated dynamics of the system in alignment with electric system planning processes and procedures. The Report is grounded in BGE's existing distribution planning framework and the 2026 Annual System Plan Update, which identifies forecasted distribution needs through a recurring planning cycle that develops load forecasts, identifies system constraints, evaluates mitigation solutions, and builds a prioritized system plan.[^3] This Report uses BGE's existing distribution planning framework and the 2026 Annual System Plan Update to assess the potential scope of capital projects that could theoretically be avoided or deferred through customer-owned distributed energy resources, while also distinguishing that screening-level assessment from the more detailed, project-grade analysis required to actually rely on a non-wires solution for a specific constraint.

[^2]: In the Matter of Transforming Maryland's Electric Distribution System to Ensure the Electric Service is Customer-Centered, Affordable, Reliable and Environmentally Sustainable in Maryland, Order No. 91218, PC 44 (July 11, 2024).

[^3]: Electric System Planning for Maryland Electric Utilities, BGE's Annual Electric System Plan Update, Case No. 9665 and PC 44 (June 12, 2026). See Mail Log No. 331109.

This Report addresses two key questions:

1. What is the overall population of potentially avoidable and deferrable capital projects on the system?
2. Knowing that various programs in and outside of the proposed DRIVE Act pilots can stack to achieve avoided/deferred transmission and distribution ("T&D") outcomes, and that costs vary over time, what is the qualitative assessment today of the ability of various distributed energy resources ("DERs") programs to meaningfully contribute to achieving those results?

### 2. Regulatory Framework

This section provides context on related regulatory proceedings that may influence future program design and valuation of distributed energy resources.

#### 2.1 The DRIVE Act Mandate

This Report responds to the mandate established by the DRIVE Act: "The potential to avoid or defer electric distribution system capital projects through the use of time–of–use rates, demand–response and demand–side programs, and renewable on–site generating systems." To meet these goals, BGE performed a rigorous review of all planning forecasts while simultaneously assessing potential programs. During production of the Report, BGE also considered the practical operational limits and other real-world limitations in deploying customer-side resources and dependable alternatives to grid infrastructure.

#### 2.2 Utility RELIEF Act and Net Metering Successor Alignment

A major milestone in Maryland's DER evolution is House Bill 1532, also known as the Utility RELIEF (Reducing Energy Load Inflation for Everyday Families) Act. This statute directs the Commission to approve a successor net metering framework by February 1, 2027, with implementation scheduled for July 1, 2027. While NEM historically functioned as a system-wide volumetric credit, the successor framework must move towards a granular, balanced valuation of grid benefits. Specifically, the act says the following:

> "(3) Balances, on a statewide basis and across technologies and industry sectors participating in net energy metering:
>
> (i)
>
> 1. Fair compensation for energy exports; and
> 2. The benefits of an eligible customer–generator's or facility's reduced load on the electric transmission and distribution system; against
>
> (ii)
>
> 1. The needs of the transmission and distribution system;
> 2. Ratepayer costs and benefits; and
> 3. Potential impacts on customers, including low– and moderate–income customers, who do not participate in the net energy metering program resulting from eligible customer–generators' reduced contributions to the distribution system."[^5]

[^5]: Md. Code Ann., Pub. Util. § 7-306.4(c)(3), as enacted by the Utility RELIEF (Reducing Energy Load Inflation for Everyday Families) Act, ch. 353, 2026 Md. Laws.

The legislative intent of the successor program is structurally aligned with the goals of compensating third-party resources for behaviors or actions taken to support deferral of distribution costs. The Commission has mandated that utilities balance fair compensation for customer exports against the broader needs of the transmission and distribution system. Crucially, the successor framework must weigh the quantifiable benefits of a customer's reduced load on the local T&D network against the overall system costs, ratepayer impacts, and particularly the financial burdens shifted to low-to-moderate-income ("LMI") non-participating customers. By evaluating the benefits of reduced customer load on the electric transmission and distribution system in parallel with the DRIVE Act's capital deferral goals to use DERs as non-wires solutions ("NWS") that avoid or defer infrastructure buildout, the Commission is driving toward a unified method for valuing localized grid services.

The impact of designing this successor program with results realized from the DRIVE Act pilot is important for many reasons, but primarily because it impacts the overall financial prospects of renewable on-site generating systems. Since residential energy storage is often collocated with solar installations, if the successor tariff results in more or less residential solar than currently forecasted, the change in solar penetration will likely impact the propensity for installation of energy storage.

#### 2.3 Stacked Tariffs and Avoided Distribution Value

Case No. 9715[^6] is the Commission's vehicle for implementing the Maryland Energy Storage Program ("MESP") and translating the State's energy storage targets into actionable procurement, tariff, approval, and implementation frameworks. A workgroup was initiated to develop program design recommendations for meeting Maryland's statutory energy storage deployment targets of 3,000 MW by May 31, 2034. The MESP work group is actively designing a grid services stack and incentives framework for deployment. The proposed program will create a wide variety of value-streams and will include any deferred capital expenses related to distribution grid projects within its scope.

[^6]: Maryland Energy Storage Program, Case No. 9715 (October 2, 2023).

Case No. 9715 provides a potential contractual and commercial mechanism needed to realize the capital deferrals explored in this Report for behind-the-meter ("BTM") and FTM energy storage.

Also noteworthy within Case 9715 is Order No. 91705, which contains the Commission order to submit plans on distribution-connected energy storage procurement.[^7] Within Order No. 91705, the Commission allocated BGE an 87MW procurement goal for FTM distribution connected storage to be installed by November 2028.[^8] The procurement mechanisms to meet this goal are a combination of third party owned and utility owned projects. These procurements are an alternative contractual mechanism to realize capital project deferral.

[^7]: Order No. 91705, Case No. 9715 (June 24, 2025).

[^8]: Id. at 7.

Case 9715 is relevant because energy storage procurement and tariff mechanisms are closely connected to the DRIVE Act's core question of whether distributed resources can avoid or defer distribution infrastructure. First, storage can complement other DRIVE Act resources by helping aggregate enough capacity on a constrained feeder or substation to make deferral feasible. Second, FTM storage faces different scaling constraints than demand-side programs. While demand-side resources depend on customer adoption, technology availability, and customer willingness to participate, a strategically located FTM battery may be able to fill the remaining gap between achievable load reduction and the amount of capacity needed to defer a traditional infrastructure investment.

### 3. The Scale of Deferral: Considering Technical Needs with Program Design

BGE's approach to capital deferral is to match resource capabilities with both real-world and forecasted needs. This involves identifying the intersection where load growth, such as those driven by localized EV adoption and electrification, intersect with the use of flexible grid services programs and incentives for capital deferral.

This report seeks to answer the statutory question on how BGE will use customer- and utility-owned DERs to defer capital investments. In addition, BGE must design services that can be called upon with the same level of predictability and reliability as traditional infrastructure.

With this holistic vision in mind, any future grid service must incorporate specific critical design elements to be evaluated as NWS:

- **Simple and Understandable**: To achieve deep market penetration, the complexity of the grid's technical need must be translated into simple, intuitive, and highly visible price and/or dispatch signals for the customer. If the mechanism is not easily understood by the end-user or their automated devices, participation collapses.
- **Low Attrition / High Retention**: Realizing capital deferral requires sustained and predictable load shape modifications over multi-year planning horizons. Services must be engineered to operate automatically when called and maintain or have engaging retention mechanics to minimize "opt-out" or fatigue behaviors that erode long-term load shape reliability. Planners must also have the assurance that programs will be maintained for extended durations to allow for resources to be accounted for in system planning.
- **Proactive Deployment**: Grid constraints cannot be solved reactively once a component is overloaded. Services and customer-facing programs must be able to meet grid needs when, where, and at a duration that is needed, before acute physical needs manifest, ensuring that the resource capacity is reliable and mature when the planning threshold is reached. For identified constraints, this can be targeted to defer specific high-growth areas; for system wide support, this must also be deployed broadly.

### 4. The BGE Planning Process

BGE's distribution planning process, including forecasting methodology and system scope, is described in detail in the 2026 Annual System Plan Update. The process supports analyses of system components and includes development of a 15-year forecast to support, among other things, the development of the 10-Year Distribution Capacity Plan.

BGE's distribution planning process includes four primary phases:

1. Development of distribution load forecasts,
2. Identification of system constraints,
3. Evaluation of mitigation solutions,
4. And development of the prioritized system plan.

The Report uses the same framework to evaluate where customer-side load management, demand response, time-of-use rates, renewable on-site generation, and storage-enabled resources may have potential to reduce or defer future distribution investment needs.

#### 4.1 Develop the Load Forecast (15-Year Horizon)

The development of the load forecast begins with the analysis of supervisory control and data acquisition ("SCADA"), advanced metering infrastructure ("AMI"), and weather station data to determine baseline behavior. A forecast is developed with the weather-normalized base net loading incorporating future load additions, topology changes, and technology adoption (EV/PV) which produces a granular 15-year peak load forecasts for each feeder and substation. For additional forecast details please reference the 2026 Annual System Plan Update.

Beginning with the 2026 planning cycle, BGE is implementing enhanced forecasting capabilities through the Distributed Energy Resources and Load Forecasting system. This supports more granular hourly forecasting, spatial allocation of EV, PV, and load growth projections, and overlay of DER generation and EV charging profiles onto baseline feeder load profiles. These capabilities are directly relevant to future distribution deferral analysis because the ability to identify where and when flexible resources can provide value depends on hourly, locational insight rather than only peak-based planning.

#### 4.2 Identify System Constraints

Once a 15-year horizon load forecast has been developed, planning processes including comparing forecasted peak demand to component ratings and identifying system constraints by identifying specific components projected to exceed capacity as mitigation targets.

#### 4.3 Develop Constraint Solutions (10-Year Horizon)

The above noted efforts allow planners to begin solution design activities where they develop solutions for constraints expected within 10 years.

For capacity-related projects, BGE's current planning process evaluates potential NWS as part of the mitigation review. Existing NWS evaluations have primarily focused on battery energy storage systems as potential alternatives to traditional infrastructure solutions. Those evaluations examine hourly feeder and station loading profiles, seasonal peak loading conditions, required MW and MWh load relief, overload duration, recharge capability, feeder load shape impacts, seasonal loading variability, and the operational feasibility of deferring or mitigating forecasted overload conditions. This same analytical logic informs the Report's discussion of whether DRIVE Act resources could eventually provide dependable capital deferral value.

#### 4.4 Build the Prioritized Plan

The final phase of the distribution planning process is integration where solutions are prioritized into the formal 10-year distribution system plan.

The following section provides an initial screening of the potential magnitude of distribution deferral opportunities on BGE's system. The analysis is not intended to identify a final set of projects that can be avoided or deferred through DRIVE Act resources. Rather, it establishes a starting point for understanding the scale, timing, and operational characteristics of projected system needs. The system need identification summarizes the aggregate MW of forecasted overloads by component type, including feeders and substations, and provides an initial estimate of the size of projected distribution needs over the planning horizon.

The duration and time-of-day analyses further refine this initial screen by identifying how long a resource would need to perform and when during the day performance would be required. Duration is critical because it indicates the minimum operational window needed for a grid service resource to provide a credible deferral benefit. A short-duration overload may be more suitable for targeted demand response, managed charging, or storage-backed dispatch, while a longer-duration overload may require a larger, more dependable, or more diverse resource portfolio. Similarly, the time-of-day analysis helps assess whether forecasted needs align with the expected availability of different resources. Together, these analyses provide the basic inputs including MW, duration, and timing needed to evaluate the potential magnitude of deferral opportunities and to identify where more detailed project-level analysis may be warranted.

#### 5.4 Takeaways

This analysis selected year 10 as a snapshot in time. A different year would likely produce a different mix, because overloads can begin as smaller, shorter duration constraints and then increase in magnitude and duration over time. The real deferral capability will therefore require component level forecasting to understand how long each component could be deferred, what program or portfolio of programs could be deployed to achieve that deferral, and whether the financial value to customers is clear enough to support participation at the scale needed.

Overall, the analysis indicates that most forecasted feeder and substation overload MWs in the 10-year planning horizon are longer duration constraints that are not well suited to behavioral demand response or many current energy storage configurations or chemistries commercially viable today. Across the components reviewed a large portion of the identified overload MWs fall into the 6 plus hour category. However, the analysis also identifies a smaller but meaningful population of shorter duration overloads, representing approximately 10 percent of total overload MWs, that may be appropriate scenarios to begin building non wires capabilities, including targeted dispatch, customer sited resources, front of the meter storage, or hybrid solutions that could defer or reduce the need for traditional capital infrastructure.

The timing of peak load reduction is also important. The summer peaks identified in this analysis appear generally consistent with the operating windows of existing demand response and peak load reduction programs, particularly during afternoon and early evening hours. By contrast, the winter peaks appear to occur in the early morning and later at night, which raises different operational and customer participation considerations. As a result, winter-focused programs present an opportunity to build on BGE's existing load management evolution by using customer-centered, automated solutions that can deliver dependable reductions during early-morning and late-evening periods. BGE has already begun applying this approach through winter load management efforts and continues to advance it through managed charging, where flexible load can be optimized in a way that supports grid needs while minimizing customer disruption.

#### 6.1 Standardized Program Operational Definitions

To ensure consistency, the operational characteristics of the current programs are defined below. Detailed program design, tariff structures, and incentives are addressed in BGE's tariffs, supporting materials, and PC77 program inventory.

##### 6.1.1 Time-of-Use ("TOU") Rates

TOU rates vary electricity prices by time of day, day of week, and season. Higher peak and lower off-peak prices are intended to encourage customers to shift usage away from high-demand periods. This analysis assumes a system-wide tariff with fixed time windows.

Detailed rate structures are described in BGE's filed tariffs and supporting materials and are noted and defined in the BGE PC77 Inventory Report. Existing BGE TOU rates include Schedule EV, Schedule RD, Schedule RL, and Electric Vehicle Charging Time-of Use.

##### 6.1.2 Residential Demand Response

Residential demand response allows the utility or a third party to remotely manage specific customer appliances, typically smart thermostats, electric water heaters, or pool pumps, during high-load periods. Events may be dispatched system-wide or by location, with defined notice and duration parameters.

Detailed program design, incentive structures, and performance characteristics are described in BGE's tariffs and PC77 filing. Existing programs include Connected Rewards and PeakRewards.

##### 6.1.3 Commercial & Industrial ("C&I") Demand Response

This Report focuses on C&I customers because residential prosumers are addressed under residential demand response and renewable on-site generation. C&I demand response compensates larger customers for reducing load through curtailment, operational changes, backup generation, or managed equipment. For this analysis, it is assumed to be similar to BGE's proposed DRIVE Act Aggregator program.

BGE does not currently incentivize large customers to dynamically shift load when called up for grid services. This has been proposed in BGE's Aggregator program in the Company's DRIVE Act Revised proposals.[^12]

[^12]: See Mail Log No. 326404.

##### 6.1.4 Demand Side Management (Energy Efficiency and EV Managed Charging)

Demand Side Management ("DSM") programs reduce or shift customer electricity use through energy efficiency and managed electrification. Energy efficiency provides persistent, non-dispatchable load reductions, while Smart Charge Management enables time-based shifting of vehicle charging.

Smart Charge Management is described in the BGE PC77 Inventory Report. The inventory found that managed charging is strategically important but still emerging: it can address EV charging peaks and off-peak start-time spikes, but its current filed MW impact remains modest relative to residential demand response.

Additional BGE programs include Energy Efficiency programs funded through EmPOWER. These programs encourage customers to adopt energy-efficient equipment and provide education on how to use energy in a more efficient manner through customized reporting.

##### 6.1.5 Renewable Onsite Generation (Solar paired with storage)

Renewable on-site generation in this Report refers to customer-sited solar photovoltaics ("PV") paired with behind-the-meter battery storage. Storage converts weather-dependent solar output into a more controllable resource by allowing charging and dispatch to support peak reduction, load shifting, and potential grid constraints.

BGE does not currently incentivize solar paired with storage to support the distribution system.

##### 6.1.6 Stand-alone Storage — Front of the Meter ("FTM") Battery Procurement and Behind the Meter ("BTM") Systems

Stand-alone battery energy storage systems operate without co-located generation. FTM systems interconnect directly to the distribution system and can be monitored and controlled through BGE's Utility-DERMS platform. BTM systems sit behind customer meters and can provide responsive demand management, though performance may be affected by customer overrides, sizing, and backup-power preferences. BTM systems are typically controlled by a Grid-Edge DERMS.

Existing BGE FTM storage examples include the Hereford Substation (in progress), Chesapeake, and Fairhaven BESS projects, as described in BGE's June 12, 2026 Annual System Plan Update. Hereford was integrated into capital plans through reliability planning. Chesapeake and Fairhaven used BCA methods that may not align with current and forthcoming UBCA standards, so BGE cannot satisfy the following Order No. 92422 request using those prior analyses:

>

#### 6.3 Temporal & Locational Potential Assessment

Evaluating program ability to address component-level violations requires temporal and locational analysis. The PC77 inventory notes that winter and off-peak impacts require additional validation.

Accordingly, filed program MW values should be treated as system-level indicators, not direct estimates of feeder-level deferral value. A program may reduce system peak while still requiring additional analysis to confirm whether it can mitigate a localized overload.

##### 6.3.1 Temporal Assessment

When evaluating temporal characteristics, three primary dimensions are considered:

- **Time of Day**: The specific hours' behavior changes are required.
- **Seasonality**: The months or conditions during which the constraint occurs.
- **Duration**: The total length of time load reduction is required per event.

These variables will shift as electrification changes local load shapes, requiring programs that can adapt to evolving peak windows.

##### 6.3.2 Locational Precision

Distribution deferral value depends on whether load reduction occurs at the specific component experiencing the constraint. Unlike system-wide peak reductions, localized relief is directed to the relevant feeder, transformer, or substation.

VPP enablement is needed to test differentiated temporal and locational signals and to evaluate the customer experience impacts of hyper-local dispatch or rate signals.

These figures show the scale of adoption, but capital deferral requires additional evaluation of dispatchability, location, duration, customer priorities, behavior, and coordination capability.

##### 6.4.1 Time-of-Use ("TOU") Rates

BGE supports a cautious interpretation of TOU rates for distribution-level capital deferral. The Pilot program for Schedule RD demonstrated behavioral impacts, including a -9.3% summer weekday peak impact and a -4.9% non-summer weekday peak impact, but the Pilot also included incentives for customer participation in surveys. Additionally, the Pilot was tested for a two-year period and evaluated for differences between years 1 and 2. Observed TOU impacts and evaluation results are documented in the Brattle Group's PC44 Time-of-Use Pilot Evaluation. Schedule RL and Schedule EV have not been studied for MW reduction potential. Vehicle Charging TOU is active and directionally useful for EV load shaping, but its filed MW reduction potential is modest at current enrollment levels.

TOU rates remain useful for customer engagement and load shaping, but they are less suitable as the primary tool for component-specific deferral because they are behavioral, generally not dispatchable, and difficult to target a constrained feeder or substation. They are best viewed as a complementary foundation for broader load shaping and customer education.

**Temporal Assessment**:

- **Time of Day**: Constrained to rigid, predefined pricing blocks (e.g., 2 PM to 6 PM). It is unable to dynamically shift timing to target a localized 1-hour peak that occurs outside this window (such as a late-evening EV charging surge).
- **Seasonality**: Set statically on a seasonal basis (typically a higher summer peak rate). This aligns reasonably with predictable seasonal load shapes but lacks the agility to address sudden, off-season localized weather extremes.
- **Duration**: Continuous daily signal throughout the peak window. However, customer behavioral response is subject to "fatigue" over long durations, leading to eroding performance as event days persist or peak periods are lengthened.

**Locational Signal**: Low. System-wide tariffs make it difficult to target a specific feeder.

**Scalability**: Poor for targeted needs. Organically achieving a substantial load shift on a single constrained component is unlikely without additional intervention or supplemental DERs.

##### 6.4.2 Residential Demand Response

Residential demand response is BGE's strongest existing demand-side resource for near-term summer peak reduction. Connected Rewards and PeakRewards represent approximately 125 MW of filed on-summer-peak reduction potential and provide mature, dispatchable, customer-facing resources for VPP development.

For distribution deferral, residential demand response must have sufficient device saturation on the constrained component, customer availability during the relevant hours, and reliable performance for the overload duration. Winter value is being evaluated, and scalability will depend on heat pump penetration, heating mix, comfort limits, and additional testing.

**Temporal Assessment**:

- **Time of Day**: Highly dispatchable to target specific localized afternoon/evening peak periods (e.g., hour-ending 14 to 18).
- **Seasonality**: Highly seasonal. Residential demand response currently provides its strongest value during summer, when central air conditioning load creates a dependable demand response opportunity. As heat pump adoption grows, the resource may also become increasingly useful for winter distribution needs, particularly in areas with high heat pump penetration and forecasted constraints. Shoulder-season and winter performance will require additional testing, but these periods present an opportunity to expand residential demand response beyond its traditional summer peak role.
- **Duration**: Limited duration capability. Events are usually limited to 1 to 4 hours per event and restricted to a capped cumulative number of hours per season to avoid significant customer opt-outs and attrition, often mandated by the original equipment manufacturer ("OEM").

**Locational Signal**: High. Participants can be grouped by component for targeted relief, and the DRIVE Pilot is expected to test feeder- or substation-level dispatch. The main customer experience risk is that local dispatch may not align with an individual customer's visible usage pattern, which could create confusion without clear communication.

**Scalability**: Medium. Scale depends on participant density, smart thermostat adoption, controllable device availability, acquisition, and retention. Although BGE has approximately 125 MW of system-wide residential demand response capacity, localized feeder penetration may require targeted marketing, tailored incentives, and complementary resources to support reliable distribution deferral.

##### 6.4.3 Managed Charging

Smart Charge Management manages enrolled EVs or compatible Level 2 chargers to better align home charging with grid needs. The program reduces charging during peak periods and spreads off-peak charging to avoid a secondary start-time spike.

BGE's PC77 inventory highlights current program enrollment and estimated load reduction potential. The values show strategic importance for EV-driven planning, but current MW relief remains modest compared with residential demand response.

For distribution deferral, Smart Charge Management is most valuable where EV adoption is concentrated on constrained components and charging can be shifted away from the specific local overload hours. Current data supports treating it as an emerging localized load-shaping tool rather than a broad near-term infrastructure substitute.

**Temporal Assessment**:

- **Time of Day**: Managed charging provides an ongoing daily signal across the applicable peak period.
- **Seasonality**: Year-round capability. The managed charging schedule is designed to meet customer charging preferences along with distribution and bulk system needs.
- **Duration**: Once enrolled, a customer's charging schedule is managed by BGE's managed charging partner every time a participating device is plugged in at home. This avoids the creation of a secondary 'timer' peak, which would negatively impact the distribution system.

**Locational Signal**: High. SCM participants are grouped by the feeders serving their homes to support targeted charging optimization. SCM incorporates feeder-specific load forecasts to ensure managed charging schedules are tailored to distribution needs. This allows BGE to manage EV charging in a way that aligns with localized grid needs, particularly where EV adoption is concentrated on constrained components.

**Scalability**: High. With scale over time, this advanced optimization approach is expected to support the following reliability and affordability-focused goals: 1) Maximize existing infrastructure utilization, 2) Defer avoidable capital investments, and 3) Reduce utility and customer energy costs.

##### 6.4.4 Commercial and Industrial Demand Response

C&I demand response can provide concentrated, localized load reduction where flexible large customers are served by a constrained feeder or substation. Unlike residential demand response, a limited number of C&I participants may provide material reductions where operations or components are flexible.

This makes C&I demand response a strong complement to residential programs, particularly where local load shapes differ or residential device saturation is limited. Its deferral value is customer-specific and depends on location, peak coincidence, operational flexibility, compensation, performance requirements, and willingness to commit to reliable dispatch.

**Temporal Assessment**:

- **Time of Day**: Flexible and dispatchable. C&I demand response events can be targeted to match specific daytime process schedules, commercial building operating hours, or peak patterns. This makes C&I demand response highly useful where the distribution need is tied to a predictable local peak window and load coincides with that need.
- **Seasonality**: Potentially year-round. C&I demand response can support summer and winter needs where customer operations and flexible loads align with local constraints, with industrial process loads offering particularly strong potential for dependable winter performance, however, the degree of winter dependability will vary by customer type.
- **Duration**: Flexible but facility-specific. Many C&I customers may be able to provide multi-hour reductions where operations, backup systems, or scheduling flexibility allow. Coordination between PJM and BGE dispatch will be important to preserve customer participation and avoid unnecessary dispatch burden.

**Locational Signal**: Very High where suitable customers are located on the constrained component. C&I demand response can be targeted at the individual account level, allowing BGE to evaluate customer load shape, peak coincidence, and curtailment capability for localized distribution needs. Its value depends on the presence of flexible C&I load on the relevant feeder, transformer, or substation. Under BGE's proposed aggregator model, aggregators can help identify available curtailment, communicate its locational value, and support dispatch when the local system needs arise.

**Scalability**: High where suitable customers exist, but uneven across the system. A single large customer or small aggregation can provide material load reduction, making C&I demand response a strong candidate for targeted deferral where flexible load is concentrated on the constrained component. However, scalability will vary by customer mix, location, operational flexibility, and willingness to accept dispatch obligations.

Lessons from programs such as Con Edison's Commercial System Relief Program suggests that C&I demand response can provide meaningful local relief, but penetration levels are likely to vary significantly by network and customer characteristics.[^17] For many distribution needs, C&I demand response will be most effective when paired with residential demand response, managed charging, energy efficiency, or storage resources to achieve the scale and reliability needed for deferral.

[^17]: Consolidated Edison Company of New York, Inc., Report on Program Performance and Cost Effectiveness of Demand Response Programs – 2025, Case No. 14-E-0423, Appendix B: CSRP Enrolled and Achieved Network Impacts, at 32–35 (filed Nov. 17, 2025).

##### 6.4.5 Renewable Onsite Generation (Solar paired with storage)

Solar paired with BTM storage can provide distribution deferral value where there is sufficient penetration on a constrained component. Once installed, enrolled, visible, and dispatchable, its usefulness depends primarily on local penetration, usable battery capacity, and available state of charge.

Pairing storage with solar converts a passive renewable resource into a dispatchable grid resource. Storage can shift solar production to afternoon, evening, or winter peaks and, where program rules allow, charge from the grid before a forecasted distribution event.

**Temporal Assessment**:

- **Time of Day**: Highly versatile. While passive solar output is naturally strongest during midday hours, paired battery storage can be dispatched dynamically to meet afternoon and evening distribution peaks, including later peak windows. This makes the resource more flexible than standalone solar because the battery can shift solar output to the hours when the constrained component is most likely to experience overload.
- **Seasonality**: High. Weather-influenced but year-round capable. The storage system can mitigate this seasonal limitation, particularly if the battery can charge from the grid when solar production is insufficient. This means solar paired with storage can provide summer peak reduction through a combination of solar output and battery discharge, while also providing winter peak-shaving capability when the battery is charged in advance of an event. The winter value of the resource will therefore depend less on solar production itself and more on storage availability, control capability, and program rules governing charging and dispatch.
- **Duration**: Medium. BTM solar-plus-storage duration is constrained by battery size, customer backup preferences, and available state of charge. For distribution deferral, the key question is whether the enrolled fleet has enough usable capacity and energy duration to cover the specific overload period. If the overload is short, BTM solar-plus-storage may be well suited. If the overload is longer or recurring, additional storage capacity, revised operating rules, or complementary resources may be needed.

**Locational Signal**: Medium to High. Solar-plus-storage systems can be mapped to specific feeders, transformers, and substations, allowing clusters of participating systems to be targeted and dispatched to relieve local overloads. The locational value is strongest where there is sufficient concentration of systems on the constrained component and where the utility has the operational visibility and dispatch rights needed to rely on the resource. The locational signal is weaker where adoption is geographically dispersed or where only a small number of systems exist on a given feeder. As a result, the resource is most useful for deferral when program design can identify constrained areas, encourage adoption in those areas, and aggregate systems into a dependable local portfolio. Since dispatching the storage has little impact on the customer and there are existing vendors that facilitate seamless dispatch of storage, there should not be an issue from the customer perspective dispatching storage at the times required for the locational based need.

**Scalability**: Medium. The core planning test is whether enough BTM solar-plus-storage exists on the constrained component to reduce the overload for the required duration. Key constraints are system count, kW and kWh capacity, dispatch availability, state of charge, and backup reserve. Targeted marketing and incentives downstream of a known constraint could improve penetration.

Real-world scalability is promising but uneven. High-adoption areas may support meaningful local relief, while low-adoption areas may require targeted incentives or complementary resources. Near-term value depends on local penetration, standardized dispatch rules, and clear treatment of backup reserve requirements. Existing DER systems provide a foundation for future deferral.

##### 6.4.6 Stand-alone Storage (BTM and FTM)

Although FTM storage is not a DRIVE Act resource, it illustrates how an engineered, dispatchable resource can address a defined distribution constraint. A stand-alone battery can be sized to a specific MW need, charged in advance, and dispatched during the hours when relief is required.

**Temporal Assessment**: Stand-alone storage has a strong temporal fit because it can discharge during the relevant constraint hours and recharge during lower-load periods, provided dispatch does not create a new peak or conflict with other obligations.

- **Time of Day**: Technically flexible, but subject to economic and operational prioritization. Discharge can be targeted at any single peak hour or spread over a block across the 24-hour cycle, with recharging scheduled during off-peak periods. This flexibility allows storage to be aligned with summer afternoon peaks, winter morning or evening peaks, or non-traditional local constraints that do not match system peak conditions. However, it is common for a front-of-the-meter battery to rely on multiple value streams to cover the capital cost of building the battery. As a result, while a battery may be technically capable of responding to system needs at different times, that does not mean it can always be economically counted on to do so unless the relevant use cases are coordinated. For example, a battery may be needed both to provide distribution services and to address PJM peak conditions, but those needs may arise at different times.
- **Seasonality**: Year-round capability. Operating characteristics are largely independent of seasonal weather generation or thermal patterns, except for minor efficiency losses in extreme temperatures. This gives stand-alone storage an advantage over resources whose output or participation is concentrated in a particular season. A storage resource can be used for summer distribution peaks, winter peaks, or both, provided that the same capacity is not committed to conflicting obligations during the same period.
- **Duration**: Highly predictable, but capacity-limited based on the design of the resource. There is nothing technically preventing a front-of-the-meter battery from being designed for more than four hours of duration. For purposes of this assessment, however, four hours is used as the relevant benchmark because four-hour performance has been the maximum duration discussed in the Grid Services Tariff portion of the MESP Work Group. In addition, there is a general understanding that developer returns may become harder to realize as duration extends beyond four hours. Accordingly, a four-hour battery provides a useful planning reference point for evaluating the ability of storage to address traditional distribution needs that are defined around a multi-hour peak or post-contingency condition.

**Locational Signal**: Precise. Front-of-the-meter systems can be sited at or near the exact constraint and operated without customer override, making them the clearest fit for a known need.

**Scalability**: High, subject to site control, interconnection, permitting, procurement timing, and cost-effectiveness. However, reliance on a single storage asset can create a concentrated point of failure compared with a diversified portfolio of customer resources.

BGE has practical experience using storage as a non-wires solution. Hereford, Chesapeake and Fairhaven BESS projects, described in the Annual System Plan Update, show that storage can provide deferral value where the need is well-defined and the resource can be engineered, sited, and operated to match the constraint.

##### 6.4.7 Energy Efficiency - Demand Side Management

The PC77 filing identifies Smart Energy Manager and Business Energy Manager as customer-facing tools that support energy awareness, benchmarking, usage alerts, technical assistance, and operational recommendations.

These tools support engagement and voluntary load reduction, but they are not dynamically dispatchable. They are best viewed as supportive DSM resources that can build awareness and participation pathways for more dispatchable programs.

**Temporal Assessment**:

- **Time of Day**: Rigid as a standalone tactic.
- **Seasonality**: Year-round but not dispatchable. Messaging is generalized to address typical seasonal concerns but not targeted to meet needs as they arise.
- **Duration**: Ongoing but dependent on customer behavior.

**Locational Signal**: Targeting not currently deployed.

**Scalability**: High. Messaging is quickly deployed in high volume to customers despite demographics. Energy efficiency messaging is assessable to all, able to be translated into common languages, and allows customers to take individual actions to manage energy use.

#### 6.5 Readiness Conditions for Distribution Deferral

##### 6.5.1 Distribution Deferral Screening Capabilities

The Commission directed the utilities to describe their capability to screen demand-side programs and rate designs for their potential to defer or avoid distribution system capital investments. The analytical approach presented in this Report reflects a conceptual screening framework based on the integration of system planning outputs and program performance characteristics. Specifically, BGE has:

- Identified the location, magnitude, and timing of forecasted system constraints through its Electric System Planning ("ESP") framework (Section 5); and
- Evaluated demand-side programs and rate designs based on their peak load reduction potential, ability to target specific geographic areas, temporal performance characteristics, and operational considerations (Section 6).

Conceptually, deferral screening asks whether demand-side resources are locationally coincident, time-coincident, sufficient in magnitude, and reliable enough for planning use. This Report evaluates those dimensions, but BGE does not yet combine them into a standardized screening methodology or decision framework.

A formal screening capability will require additional data, operational experience, and methodological alignment. The UBCA work group and DRIVE Act Pilots are expected to inform future methods by establishing valuation approaches and producing empirical evidence on participation, performance, behavior, and dispatch reliability.

##### 6.5.2 Distribution Deferral Readiness

The PC77 filing reinforces the need to distinguish between program expansion and deferral readiness. Enrollment strategies can increase participation, but capital deferral depends on whether added resources are located where the need exists and can perform during the relevant hours and seasons.

Based on BGE's planning process and current program experience, distribution deferral through DRIVE Act resources depends on several readiness conditions:

- **Sufficient locational saturation**: Resources must be enrolled on or electrically relevant to the constrained feeder, transformer, or substation.
- **Reliable dispatch capability**: Resources must be capable of responding during the specific hours, seasons, and durations associated with the constraint.
- **Validated performance**: Program planning assumptions must be confirmed through event results, nominated-versus-delivered capacity analysis, and repeatable operational performance.
- **Customer retention and availability**: Participation must be stable over the multi-year period needed to support deferral of a capital project.
- **Telemetry and platform integration**: DERMS, aggregator platforms, and utility systems must provide visibility, event notification, dispatch coordination, and performance measurement.
- **Cost recovery and compensation certainty**: Customers, aggregators, and the utility need clear compensation mechanisms and program duration certainty.
- **Project-specific BCA and risk allocation**: A resource should not be relied upon as a substitute for infrastructure unless the avoided or deferred capital value, program cost, customer impacts, reliability risk, and operational responsibilities are clearly evaluated.

These readiness conditions explain why the DRIVE Pilot is an important step toward potential distribution deferral. The pilot can generate the operational evidence needed to determine which resources can be counted on, where they are most valuable, and how they should be structured to support future planning decisions.

### 7. Merits and Feasibility of Opt-Out TOU Transition (DRIVE Act (C)(2))

The DRIVE Act directed that utilities provide a report on the merits and feasibility of transitioning all customers to a time-of-use tariff on an opt-out basis. This section of the Report also addresses the following Commission decision on metrics and methodologies per Commission Order No. 92422.[^18]

[^18]: Order 92422, p. 8 (May 29, 2026).

> "a portion of OPC's recommended guidelines and to the extent information is available directs the IOUs to provide the following: the IOUs should identify key implementation, operational, and customer bill-impact considerations relevant to evaluating whether opt-out TOU rates would be reasonable and practical;"

#### 7.1 Merits

**TOU Rates in Maryland**

Residential TOU rate structures have been available to BGE residential customers for more than 40 years. Recently, Public Conference 44 ("PC44") has provided meaningful experiences and analyses for further review of residential TOU rates. Schedule RL, a residential supply-only TOU option, was supplemented through PC44, which introduced Schedule RD, a distribution and supply TOU option. Additionally, BGE offers TOU rates specific to electric vehicle charging as described in this report. Schedule RD was piloted for two years before being added as an option available to all residential customers with a smart meter. The Brattle Group ("Brattle") provided an evaluation of the pilot program in October of 2021.[^19] Based on the two-year pilot, Brattle found that participating residential customers were able to reduce their summer weekday peaks by 9.3% and their non-summer weekday peaks by 4.9%. Of note, the pilot analyzed customers on an opt-in basis. As described below, the impact of opt-out customers compared to those who may self-select may result in less meaningful shifts in peak usage. Additionally, the pilot program provided financial incentives through bill credits for participation which may have impacted customer behavior.

[^19]: PC44 Time of Use Pilots: End-of-Pilot Evaluation. The Brattle Group. October 4, 2021.

**Other States' Experience with Opt-Out TOU**

Industry benchmarking indicates that large-scale TOU transitions can reduce peak demand, but the magnitude of impact varies significantly based on rate design, customer engagement, and whether customers affirmatively choose the rate or are defaulted onto it. Across TOU rate types, results showed peak demand reductions generally ranging from approximately 1.3% to 8.0%. However, the strongest results tend to occur where rates are targeted, well-designed, and paired with customers who are engaged or have flexible end uses, while broader default or opt-out transitions tend to produce more modest and less uniform behavioral response.

TOU rates are most effective when customers understand the rate, see a clear value proposition, and have either the motivation or enabling technology to shift load. Voluntary participants and customers on more tailored rates, particularly those with flexible end uses such as EV charging, tend to produce stronger impacts because they are more engaged and better positioned to respond to the price signal. By contrast, customers defaulted or effectively forced onto TOU rates may remain enrolled, but their behavioral response can be weaker if they are less aware, less able to shift usage, or less motivated to actively manage energy consumption. This distinction is important: high enrollment under an opt-out structure does not necessarily translate into high, durable, locationally useful load reduction.

The case studies also underscore the implementation challenge of moving all residential customers to TOU. Outside of smart meter deployment, successful transitions required careful customer segmentation, shadow billing or bill protection, rate comparison tools, multilingual and omnichannel communications, ongoing education, call center preparation, billing-system readiness, and clear opt-out pathways. These requirements are not simply administrative; they are directly tied to significant investments in billing system enhancements, heavy change management initiatives, and to whether customers understand and respond to the rate. For BGE, this supports a cautious conclusion: TOU rates can provide broad system-level load-shaping value, but it is not the right time for such a transition given affordability constraints and customer perceptions at this time. A full opt-out transition would require significant customer education, technology, operational readiness, affordability protections, and regulatory design work. After implementation, the resulting peak load reduction may be limited where they would not be able to be considered a dependable tool for distribution-level capital deferral.

#### 7.2 Feasibility

There are meaningful lessons from other state's experiences with opt-out TOU. While BGE does not believe that this is the proper time to transition to opt-out TOU in the state of Maryland due to affordability concerns, there are very important considerations if the decision is made to shift to an opt-out framework. Prior to transitioning customers to an opt-out TOU, BGE believes that extensive customer communications would be needed. BGE believes that it would benefit customers to have pre-transition communications to allow customers to choose from BGE's multiple existing TOU options.

If the Commission decides that an opt-out TOU framework is appropriate in Maryland, there are certain considerations that should be made.

**1. Customer Education and Marketing**

TOU rates require customer education in order to be successful. In the PC44 Pilot, the Company provided education and outreach to recruit customers to participate in TOU. The Pilot was successful in part due to the outreach and communication continued throughout the Pilot. Customers were emailed weekly with specific usage information using Oracle tools. Customers opting into the pilot were provided information about the rate differences and time periods. The same, if not more, communications would be required to ensure that customers understood the time periods and potential bill impacts. A successful shift to opt-out TOU would require additional outreach to ensure that customers were aware of the shift, understood the impact or potential impact, and were able to actionably opt out if desired.

As part of the PC44 Pilot, BGE provided customers with a customer specific bill impact analysis describing if the customer would see a higher or lower bill if they did not shift their usage. Through Pilot participant feedback, this tool was useful in customer's consideration of the TOU rate. The bill impact analysis for the Pilot program provided bill impacts for over 100,000 customers. Of those customers, 40% would have seen higher bills under the Pilot program at an average of almost $4 per month more. This demonstrates the impact that moving customers without proper customer communication and engagement may have on customer bills.

**2. Call Center Operational Impact**

Opt-out TOU has the potential to increase call volumes to the Company's care center. In order to ensure that the opt-out process is easy to navigate, the Company would need to review current webpage interactivity and care center procedures. The Company would expect costs to ensuring that appropriate call center staffing levels are met and that the process for opting out of a TOU rate is easy to navigate for residential customers. For example, the Company would consider a webpage that would allow customers to submit requests for opting out of a mandated TOU rate rather than requiring customers to call.

**3. Affordability and Customer Protections**

While BGE's TOU rates are designed to be revenue neutral, there are potential impacts to individual customers who may not be reached through customer communications and who are unwilling or unable to shift energy usage. As described above, the Pilot program estimated that 40% of customers would have seen increased bills of almost $4 per month, on average, if they did not shift usage.

**4. Impact on Decoupling**

BGE calculates TOU and non-TOU rate schedules separately within its decoupling (Electric Rider 25). Any large shifts in customers from a non-TOU rate schedule to a TOU rate schedule need to be reflected properly in the test year data that is used for electric decoupling. Electric Rider 25 compares the actual number of customers in a month to the test year number of customers. To the extent that a shift in customers from moving to an opt-out TOU framework is not included in a test year, there should be an adjustment or consideration within the decoupling calculations.

**5. Implementation and Transition Costs**

To encourage successful shifts in energy usage and maintain customer engagement with a transition to opt-out time of use, utilities would need to make investments in multiple areas. These implementation costs may include expanded customer education and marketing, call center training and workforce readiness, and IT upgrades required to ensure customers are able to opt-out with minimal barriers. The magnitude of costs may vary dependent on utility's and the Commission's prioritization of customer experience.

#### 7.3 Recommendation

- **Alternatives**: Support an engaged customer base managing load proactively.
- **Pathway**: Existing and proposed programs via the DRIVE Act allow customers to become "prosumers" through advanced technology channels.
- **Final Stance**: Recommend against Opt-Out TOU rates for Distribution Level Capital needs.

This recommendation does not mean TOU rates lack value. Rather, TOU rates can support broad customer engagement, off-peak charging, and system-level load shaping. However, for distribution-level capital deferral, TOU rates are generally less precise than dispatchable resources because they are difficult to target to a specific constrained component and do not guarantee customer response during the exact hours of local need. For that reason, TOU rates are best viewed as a complementary tool that can reduce overall system stress and support customer education, while more dispatchable resources such as managed charging, demand response, storage, and aggregated DERs are more likely to provide dependable distribution deferral value when paired with locational targeting and verified performance.
