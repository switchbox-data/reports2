## SUMMARY

Juan-Pablo Velez is the Co-Founder and Executive Director at Switchbox, an independent, non-profit, non-partisan think tank with expertise in data science, economics, and energy modeling. Their work focuses on providing data-driven evidence-based insight to state policy-makers, who play a pivotal role in the energy transition. This testimony covers (1) bill analysis for RI Energy customers who adopt heat pumps, (2) calculation of cross-subsidies within the residential customer class, (3) proposed reallocation of the residential revenue requirement across residential customer subclasses, (4) proposed creation of a heat pump rate for recovery of the residential requirement heat pump subclass of customers, and (5) a bill analysis for customers who adopt heat pumps with the new rate.

{11} **Q. What is the purpose of your testimony?**

{12} **A.** I demonstrate that Rhode Island Energy’s (“RIE” or “the Company”) current residential
{13} electric delivery rates (A-16 and A-60) are overcharging heat pump customers, creating a
{14} substantial cross-subsidy within the residential customer class. The subsidy flows from
{15} customers that heat with heat pumps to those that heat with fossil fuels.

{17} To measure this _intra_-class cross-subsidy, I performed a cost-of-service study of
{18} residential customer subclasses. These subclasses were defined according to their heating
{19} technology and fuel, e.g. customers who heat with heat pumps, natural gas, oil/propane,
{20} electric resistance, and other. The study produced the cost-of-service for each subclass,
{21} which I then compared to the revenue RIE collected from them during the Test Year.

[PDF p. 9 | printed p. 4]

{1} I found that heat pump customers overpay relative to their cost of service by $902 per
{2} year, on average, whereas customers that heat with natural gas and delivered fuels
{3} underpay relative to their cost of service by an average of $128 and $141 per year,
{4} respectively. This cross-subsidy results in heat pump customers being charged unjust and
{5} unreasonable rates.

{7} I propose the creation of a new residential electric rate class for heat pump customers.
{8} This rate class would set a target revenue that reflects these customers’ true cost-of-
{9} service. Moving from cost allocation to rate design, I propose a simple flat rate that
{10} would recover all costs allocated to heat pump customers. This rate would collect both
{11} distribution-related delivery revenue, as well as all other non-distribution delivery
{12} revenue, such as transmission and renewable energy surcharges.

{14} By aligning delivery rates with cost causality, this proposal would eliminate the
{15} significant cross-subsidy that exists between heat pump and fossil-heating electric
{16} residential customers, and make it cheaper to heat with heat pumps than natural gas for
{17} most Rhode Island households.

{19} **Q. Please summarize your recommendations to the Commission.**

{20} **A.** I recommend that the Commission:

   {2} heat pump for heating and cooling, distinct from the current residential rate
   {3} classes, A-16 and A-60.

2. {4} **Assign the heat pump rate class a target delivery revenue requirement** equal
   {5} to heat pump customers’ cost-of-service, as determined by cost-of-service studies

   {14} attestation and certification option for installation that may happen outside of
   {15} these programs.

6. {16} **Recalibrate the HP Rate in subsequent rate cases** to reflect evolving cost-of-

{12} **Q. You are recommending the creation of a new rate class. For clarity of terms, how are**
{13} **you using the term rate class, and how is it different from a customer class?**

{14} **A.** A _customer class_ is a broad umbrella category that groups together customers based on
{15} their primary use for electricity, e.g. domestic end-uses, lighting, or moving trains, for the
{16} purpose of cost allocation. Examples of customer classes include Residential,
{17} Commercial, Industrial, Propulsion and Lighting.

{19} A _rate class_ is a specific tariff that groups customers within a customer class, based on
{20} factors like financial need, peak demand, and voltage requirements, for the purpose of
[PDF p. 14 | printed p. 9]
{1} rate design. For instance, the Residential customer class is made up of two distinct rate
{2} classes, the Basic Residential Rate (A-16) and the Low-Income Rate (A-60), while the
{3} Lighting customer class is made up of four distinct rate classes: S-05, S-06, S-10, and S-
{4} 14. All customers within a rate class are charged using the same rate, regardless of their
{5} individual usage patterns.

{7} **Q. The new rate class you propose is designed to serve a particular customer subclass,**
{8} **heat pump customers. For clarity of terms, how do you define a customer subclass?**

{9} **A.** A customer _subclass_ is a set of customers within a customer class that share similar usage
{10} characteristics. Some customer subclasses have rate classes designed specifically for
{11} them, while others belong to larger rate classes that are designed to serve several
{12} subclasses.

{14} For the purposes of this testimony, I define a set of residential customer subclasses, based
{15} on how customers heat their homes. These subclasses include customers who heat with
{16} heat pumps, natural gas, delivered fuels, and electric resistance. One of the key
{17} recommendations in my testimony is that customers belonging to the heat pump subclass
{18} be assigned to their own rate class, rather than to the two rate classes currently defined
{19} for the residential customer class, which only differentiate based on financial need.

{11} **Q. What financial impact does heat pump and electric resistance customers paying**
{12} **more than their cost-of-service have on the other residential customer subclasses?**

{13} **A.** Jointly, the overpayments from heat pump and electric resistance customers create a

{1} delivery relative to their cost-of-service. During the Test Year, for instance, customers

{6} **Q. How did you determine the cost-of-service of each subclass?**

{7} **A.** The complete discussion of methodology is in detail in Section IX. As an overview, I
{8} estimated the hourly marginal delivery cost of every hour of the Test Year, reflecting
{9} hours when the transmission and distribution grids are constrained, and incremental load
{10} is likely to trigger the need for additional capacity investment. I then used the hourly
{11} electrical load profiles from the National Renewable Energy Laboratory’s (NREL)
{12} ResStock dataset to measure how much load each subclass had during those high-cost
{13} hours. This gave me each subclass’s share of current cost-causation, which I applied to
{14} the total Residential Delivery Revenue Requirement, in line with the Company’s cost
{15} allocation methodology.

{17} **Q. How did you measure the cost-of-service for each residential customer subclass?**

{18} **A.** The full methodology is described in Section IX. In summary, I employed the same
{19} general cost allocation philosophy that RIE uses in its Allocated Cost-of-Service Study:
{20} measure the present-day cost-causation of each customer class, and then use it to divide
{21} up the revenue requirement. But I applied the methodology one level deeper, within the
[PDF p. 19 | printed p. 14]
{1} residential class, to allocate costs among residential customer subclasses. I also used a
{2} more precise method to measure cost-causation.

{4} The goal was to divide the delivery revenue that RIE collected during the Test Year, the
{5} total Delivery Revenue Requirement and not just the Distribution Revenue Requirement,
{6} into subclass cost-of-service allocations, based on each subclass’s cost-causation. To
{7} accomplish that goal, I measured the hourly marginal delivery cost of every hour in the
{8} Test Year, capturing the hours when the transmission and distribution grids are
{9} constrained and incremental load is likely to trigger the need for additional capacity
{10} investment. As is described more fully in Section IX, by capturing _when_ delivery costs
{11} are caused, this hourly allocator measures cost-causation much more accurately than the
{12} NCP allocator the Company uses for cost allocation, which essentially just measures each
{13} class’s annual peak demand level—regardless of whether it occurs at a time of year that
{14} causes costs.

{16} I then examined how much electricity each subclass consumed during those high-cost
{17} hours, using a sample of residential load profiles for each subclass. These load profiles
{18} came from NREL’s ResStock end-use load profiles dataset, which are statistically

{9} **Q. What is the central finding of your cost-of-service study?**

{10} **A.** Under the residential electric rates that were in place during the Test Year, heat pump
{11} customers in Rhode Island paid 65% more than their delivery cost-of-service.

{13} **Q. Why do heat pump customers pay more than their cost-of-service?**

{14} **A.** Heat pump customers pay more than their cost-of-service because delivery costs are
{15} recovered primarily through volumetric per-kWh charges. Volumetric rates assume, by
{16} design, that a customer’s cost-causation scales with their total electricity consumption.
{17} That is to say that the more kWh you use, the more you pay for delivery. But delivery
{18} costs are not caused by every kWh equally. As detailed in Section III, a kWh consumed
{19} during a peak summer hour when a feeder is near capacity contributes to the need for new
{20} investment, and a kWh consumed during an off-peak winter hour with ample spare
{21} capacity does not.

[PDF p. 21 | printed p. 16]

{1} When a household installs a heat pump, its annual electricity consumption grows
{2} significantly, but its cost-of-service would grow either modestly (if they are gaining
{3} cooling) or would stay the same (if they already have full cooling). That is because the
{4} vast majority of this new load occurs during the heating season, when it is absorbed by
{5} the massive spare winter capacity of Rhode Island’s distribution grid, without triggering
{6} capacity upgrades. As described in Section III, not a single one of RIE’s feeders is winter-
{7} constrained, and the median feeder has 56% of winter headroom. Nevertheless, under
{8} volumetric delivery rates, the customer’s delivery bill grows in lockstep with their
{9} consumption, regardless of their cost of service. The gap that results is the cross-subsidy.

## {1} V. Proposed Cost Allocation and Rate Design

{2} **Q. What do you recommend the Commission do to address the cross-subsidy?**

{3} **A.** I recommend that the Commission create a new residential heat pump customer rate
{4} class, with a target revenue equal to their true delivery cost-of-service. This is
{5} fundamentally a cost allocation correction — the same kind of correction the
{6} Commission makes when it finds that one customer class is paying more or less than its
{7} cost-of-service, except applied one level down, within the residential class. The
{8} Commission should then determine the appropriate Delivery Revenue Requirement for
{9} the heat pump rate class based on the cost-of-service for this customer subclass, and my
{10} analysis finds that this revenue amounts to $11.9 million.

{12} **Q. How do you propose the Commission collect this Delivery Revenue Requirement**
{13} **from customers that qualify for the HP Rate?**

{14} **A.** I recommend that the Commission create a new set of flat volumetric delivery rates to
{15} collect this Delivery Revenue Requirement appropriate to the HP Rate Class. This set of
{16} flat rates would be lower than the current default rates, calibrated so that heat pump
{17} customers’ average annual delivery revenue equals their delivery cost-of-service.

{19} **Q. How would this new heat pump rate be calculated?**

{20} **A.** The subclass cost-of-service analysis tells us the total delivery revenue to collect from
{21} heat pump customers. I then subtract the fixed customer charges already collected from
{22} those customers (the current fixed charges times the number of HP customers), and divide
[PDF p. 60 | printed p. 55]
{1} the remainder by the total kWh consumed by the HP subclass. The result is the _total_ per-
{2} kWh volumetric delivery rate for heat pump customers.

## {16} VIII. Discussion of Alternative Rate Designs

{17} **Q. In looking at the subclass analysis done in Section II, it also shows that electric**
{18} **resistance customers are also paying a cross-subsidy to fossil fuel customers. Are you**
{19} **recommending that this rate apply to those customers as well?**

{20} **A.** No. While the analysis does show a significant cross-subsidy for these customers, the
{21} purpose of my testimony has been to identify and address how current rate structures are
{22} a barrier to heat pump adoption. That purpose presumes that adoption of heat pumps is a
[PDF p. 68 | printed p. 63]
{1} desirable policy outcome. My understanding of the current state of Rhode Island policy is
{2} that heat pump adoption is a core component of the 2025 Climate Action Strategy and
{3} aligns with the findings and studies of other states on how to meet state emissions
{4} reductions requirements that are analogous to those in the Act on Climate.

{6} An electric heating rate that aims to solve the cross-subsidy of electric resistance
{7} customers would further entrench the use of electric resistance heating, which is not
{8} consistent with these state policies. For that reason, it is my opinion that the Commission
{9} and other stakeholders should prioritize the transition of electric resistance heating to heat
{10} pumps. This outcome would (1) **significantly** lower costs for customers using electric
{11} resistance heating, (2) reduce state-wide emissions through efficiency gains, (3) eliminate
{12} the cross-subsidy through auto-enrollment in the heat pump rate.

{14} How we move electric resistance customers to heat pumps is outside the scope of my
{15} testimony, and I highlight the obvious and well-known benefits that flow from that
{16} investment.

{18} **Q. Instead of using a technology-specific rate, based on subclass cost allocation, for**
{19} **heat pump customers, could the cross-subsidy be eliminated by redesigning the**
{20} **existing default rate instead?**

{21} **A.** The cross-subsidy is structural: it arises from the interaction between volumetric delivery
{22} rates and the fact that heat pump customers consume substantially more electricity than
[PDF p. 69 | printed p. 64]
{1} other residential customers, but without having proportionally higher cost-of-service (See
{2} Section III). There are two ways to eliminate this kind of intra-class cross-subsidy.

{4} The first is through **subclass cost allocation:** creating a separate residential rate class
{5} with a target revenue equal to the heat pump customers’ actual cost-of-service and
{6} designing a rate that recovers that “right-sized” target revenue. This is what I propose.

{8} The second is through **default rate design** alone. The cross-subsidy could indeed be
{9} eliminated by redesigning the default rate, but not without a radical redesign.

{11} **Q. Why would a radical redesign of the default rate be required to eliminate the cross-**
{12} **subsidy?**

{13} **A.** The default residential rates (A-16 and A-60) have two components: fixed per-customer
{14} charges and flat volumetric charges. Because fixed charges are small, the vast majority of
{15} residential delivery revenue is collected through flat volumetric charges. That is what
{16} produces the cross-subsidy: heat pump customers consume far more kWh than average
{17} — mostly in winter — so flat per-kWh charges collect far more delivery revenue from
{18} them than their cost-of-service warrants. Eliminating the cross-subsidy within this two-
{19} component structure requires either increasing the fixed charge and thereby shifting cost-
{20} recovery away from volumetric charges, making the volumetric rate seasonal rather than
{21} flat, or some combination of both. But the degree to which these levers must be moved to
[PDF p. 70 | printed p. 65]
{1} eliminate the cross-subsidy would produce radically different-looking rates — and,
{2} potentially, significant bill shock for RIE’s residential customers.

{4} Raising the fixed charge sufficiently removes the cross-subsidy because it reduces the
{5} overcollection from high-use customers (including those with heat pumps). But it would
{6} also (1) change every customer’s bill significantly, (2) be regressive by raising bills for
{7} low-use customers, who are disproportionately low-income , and (3) weaken incentives
{8} for energy efficiency and distributed generation. In Docket 4770, nearly every intervenor
{9} opposed National Grid’s proposal to increase the residential customer charge from $5 to
{10} $8.50—a 1.7x increase—for exactly these reasons.[^17] In order to eliminate the cross-
{11} subsidy from heat pump to other customers through the customer charge alone, the
{12} customer charge would need to increase more than _tenfold_ (10.6x) — from the current
{13} $6/month to approximately **$64/month.** This would allow the total volumetric delivery
{14} rate to fall from 14.1¢/kWh to roughly 3.8¢/kWh. The full calculation is in Schedule JPV-
{15} 7.

{17} **Q. Rather than adjusting the fixed rate, could this cross-subsidy be addressed by**
{18} **adding seasonal differentiation to the default rate?**

[PDF p. 76 | printed p. 71]

{1} **A.** I reject a higher fixed charge on the grounds that it would cause adverse bill impacts for
{2} some heat pump customers, and thus potentially prevent RIE from auto-enrolling all
{3} known heat pump customers into an opt-out rate. While I did not evaluate a fully cost-
{4} reflective seasonal rate, it is possible that such a rate would also produce adverse bill
{5} impacts for some heat pump customers, for the reasons described in the preceding
{6} question.

{8} A flat rate would guarantee that all customers would save under the new rate compared to
{9} the default, which would clear the way for auto-enrollment, an essential to actually
{10} solving the cross-subsidy problem at scale. A Massachusetts-style seasonal rate, where
{11} the summer rate stays equal to the current default and only the winter rate is lowered,
{12} would also guarantee that all customers would save. I propose the flat rate over the
{13} Massachusetts-style seasonal rate because it is simpler: it is a single volumetric charge
{14} applied to all heat pump customers in all hours. I also chose it because it makes the
{15} underlying cost allocation visible and verifiable: the rate is simply the heat pump
{16} subclass’s allocated delivery cost-of-service, minus revenue from fixed charges, divided
{17} by the subclass’s total yearly kilowatt-hours. By contrast, a Massachusetts-style seasonal
{18} rate only eliminates the cross-subsidy if the winter rate is set low enough.

{20} That said, if the Commission is interested in tech-specific seasonal rates in order to
{21} achieve a degree of cost-reflectiveness, I would support it as long as: 1. the summer and
[PDF p. 77 | printed p. 72]
{1} winter rates are set at a level that eliminates the cross-subsidy, 2. the summer rate is not
{2} higher than the current default rate, so auto-enrollment is not blocked.

{4} **Q. Is a technology-specific rate for heat pump customers incompatible with time-of-use**
{5} **rates?**

{6} **A.** No. On the contrary, these concepts solve fundamentally different problems and are
{7} complementary.

- {8} **Time-of-use rates** address **demand management:** they incentivize load
  {9} reduction during expensive peak hours, or load shifting from such hours to
  {10} cheaper off-peak hours. They improve _economic efficiency_ (see Section IV).
- {11} **Technology-specific rates** based on cost-of-service allocation address **cross-**
  {12} **subsidy:** flat volumetric rates collect more from HP customers than their cost-of-
  {13} service. Cost-based technology-specific rates solve this, improving _fairness_ (see
  {14} Section II).

{15} Time-of-use rates alone cannot fully solve the cross-subsidy problem as described in the
{16} preceding question. However, a technology-specific rate that eliminates the cross-subsidy
{17} can adopt time-of-use pricing to become more cost-reflective, thus improving efficiency
{18} as well as fairness.

{20} While this is a laudable goal, waiting years until AMI deployment is complete and such
{21} rates are developed means accepting years of continued overcollection from heat pump
[PDF p. 78 | printed p. 73]
{1} customers. The cross-subsidy exists today — it costs $902 per year per heat pump
{2} customer — and the Commission should not defer in correcting it. Rather, the
{3} Commission should correct the cross-subsidy now through a simple heat pump rate that is
{4} viable with Rhode Island’s current meters, and make time-of-use heat pump rates
{5} available as AMI deployment progresses.

{6} **Q. What methodology did you use to measure the delivery cost-of-service for heat**
{7} **pump and non-heat pump customers?**

{8} **A.** I performed a cost-of-service study at the subclass level — the same kind of exercise the
{9} Company performs at the class level in its Allocated Cost of Service Study (“ACOSS”),
{10} but applied one level deeper, within the residential class. The goal was to measure what
{11} share of the residential delivery revenue each subclass is responsible for and compare that
{12} to the revenue each subclass pays under today’s rates. The difference is the cross-subsidy.

{14} The steps were analogous to those used in the Company’s ACOSS:

1. {15} **Measure cost-causation.** I need a metric that tells us how much of the
   {16} system’s delivery costs each subclass causes. The Company’s ACOSS
   {17} uses Non-Coincident Peak (“NCP”) demand as its cost-causation metric at
   {18} the class level. I use hourly marginal costs as the cost-causation metric at
   {19} the subclass level — a more granular signal, but serving the same purpose.
   {20} I multiply each ResStock household’s hourly load profile by the marginal
   {21} cost of delivery in that hour (see subsequent questions for how these
   {22} hourly costs were constructed) and sum across all 8,760 hours of the year.
   [PDF p. 82 | printed p. 77]
   {1} This annual marginal cost total represents each household’s delivery cost-
   {2} causation “score”: the new delivery costs its consumption pattern drove in
   {3} the Test Year.

2. {4} **Compute subclass shares.** I sum these cost-causation scores across all
   {5} households within each subclass — heat pump, natural gas heating, and so
   {6} on — to get the subclass-level marginal cost totals. Each subclass’s share
   {7} of the total marginal costs caused by all residential customers in the Test
   {8} Year represents that subclass’s share of cost-causation.

3. {9} **Allocate residential delivery revenue.** I apply each subclass’s share of
   {10} cost-causation to the total residential delivery revenue to arrive at each
   {11} subclass’s allocated cost-of-service. On a technical-level, this is the equi-
   {12} proportional marginal cost (“EPMC”) method, the standard reconciliation
   {13} approach recommended by NARUC for bridging marginal cost analysis
   {14} and revenue requirement recovery. On a conceptual level, it is the same
   {15} logic the Company’s ACOSS uses to allocate costs across customer
   {16} classes: measure cost-causation, then divide the revenue requirement
   {17} proportionally.

4. {18} **Measure the cross-subsidy.** I sum the delivery bills actually paid by each
   {19} subclass under today’s default rates, and compare that to the subclass’s
   {20} allocated cost of service. A subclass that pays more than its cost of service
   {21} is being overcharged — it is cross-subsidizing the other subclass. This
   [PDF p. 83 | printed p. 78]
   {1} comparison is known in the academic literature as the Bill Alignment
   {2} Test.[^22]

{3} The methodology is described in full detail in my Schedule JPV-2.

{4} **Q. Why did you choose hourly marginal costs as your cost-causation metric for**
{5} **subclass-level cost allocation rather than the Company’s NCP demand metric?**

{6} **A.** Because the NCP allocator produces inaccurate cost-of-service estimates when applied to
{7} residential customer subclasses. It confuses the _magnitude_ of a peak with the _cost_ it
{8} causes, which depends on when it occurs. An NCP allocator measures each subgroup’s
{9} peak demand magnitude without regard to when that peak occurs — and at the subclass
{10} level, this produces results that contradict the Company’s own engineering data says
{11} about when the distribution grid is constrained.

{13} Consider: heat pump customers make up just 2% of residential customers, yet an NCP
{14} allocator would assign them 12% of the residential class’s demand-related distribution
{15} costs. Why? Because their aggregate peak occurs on a cold January morning and that
{16} winter peak happens to be 12% of the sum of all subclass peaks. Meanwhile, natural gas
{17} customers — 55% of residential customers — would be assigned only 33% of costs, on
{18} account of an aggregate peak that falls on an August afternoon. But we know from RIE’s
{19} own feeder data that the distribution grid has 56% median winter headroom and zero
{20} winter-constrained feeders. The system is nowhere near capacity in winter. Heat pump
{21} customers’ January peak is not straining the grid and is not driving the Company to build
{22} more infrastructure. Yet the NCP allocator would say these 2% of customers are causing
[PDF p. 88 | printed p. 83]
{1} nearly 12% of distribution costs _because of their winter loads_ — on a grid with abundant
{2} winter spare capacity. That is not an accurate measure of cost-causation.

{4} What is needed is a metric that captures _when_ costs actually occur on the transmission
{5} and distribution system and measures each subclass’s load behavior during those high-
{6} cost hours. Our hourly marginal cost approach does this. It finds that heat pump
{7} customers account for 2.7% of total residential delivery cost-of-service — not the 12%
{8} that the NCP allocator would assign them.

### Schedule JPV-8: derivation of the seasonal volumetric rates needed to eliminate the heat pump cross-subsidy

A four-page derivation (PDF pp. 297-300) asking whether a winter rate and a summer rate, with the customer charge held at $6 per month, could make the heat pump subclass's delivery revenue equal its cost of service. It sets up a 2x2 linear system solved with Cramer's rule, shows that the unconstrained solution requires a negative winter rate, and then reports the constrained optimum of a summer rate of 28.52¢/kWh and a winter rate of 0¢/kWh, which still leaves a remaining heat pump cross-subsidy of $390,875 per year. It explains that no feasible seasonal pair exists because heat pump buildings use a larger share of summer kWh (2.85%) than their share of volumetric cost of service (2.75%).
