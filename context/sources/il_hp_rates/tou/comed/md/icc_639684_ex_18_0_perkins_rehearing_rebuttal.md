## II. REFINEMENT OF STRAWMAN RATE BEST PROPOSAL FOLLOWING STAKEHOLDER OUTREACH

**Q.** What was the purpose of ComEd's initial Rate BEST proposal, presented in ComEd direct testimony on rehearing?

<!-- EPON: goal, bundling — original Rate BEST was a "strawman" designed to be "harmonized" with the approved DTOU -->

**A.** The Rate BEST proposal presented in ComEd's direct testimony on rehearing, was designed to be a "strawman" STOU rate proposal, which would serve as a starting point to which Staff and intervenors could react and respond. It was specifically designed to be harmonized with ComEd's DTOU rate, recently approved by the Commission in this docket.

**Q.** Did ComEd engage in outreach activities to Staff and Intervenors to collect feedback to the proposed Rate BEST strawman?

**A.** I disagree with any assertions that ComEd did not seek to collaborate with stakeholders on its Rate BEST proposal. See, e.g., Capellan RH Dir., Staff Ex. 14.0 at 6:119-122. ComEd sought to collaborate with stakeholders throughout the rehearing period. For example, ComEd discussed its Rate BEST proposal with representatives from CUB-EDF and ELPC-

[p. 4]

VS-UCS on multiple occasions both before and after filing its direct testimony on rehearing. I also informed ICC Staff that we were meeting with stakeholders to discuss our Rate BEST proposal and offered to include Staff in those discussions or to meet separately with Staff. I understand that ComEd Counsel offered to discuss ComEd's Rate BEST proposal with representatives from the Commission's Office of General Counsel and the Office of the Illinois Attorney General ("IL AG").[^1]

[^1]: The IL AG declined to meet with ComEd about the Rate BEST proposal and did not file direct testimony on rehearing.

**Q.** Has ComEd modified its Rate BEST strawman proposal in response to feedback received from stakeholders?

**A.** Yes, ComEd considered the feedback received both through discussions during rehearing and through the direct testimony filed in rehearing. While ComEd continues to believe that its Rate BEST proposal presented in direct testimony on rehearing is an appropriate rate design, in order to reach agreement, ComEd has modified its Rate BEST proposal to reflect stakeholder feedback in what it calls the "Compromise Proposal."

I note that Staff's position on rehearing is seemingly focused on whether an STOU can be considered in this proceeding, and ComEd has not received feedback from Staff – whether through informal discussions or in pre-filed written testimony on rehearing – regarding the design of ComEd's Rate BEST proposal.

[p. 5]

**Q.** What modifications are reflected in ComEd's Compromise Proposal?

<!-- EPON: capacity-mdppec, tmc, midday-peak — KEY: Compromise moves 100% of capacity obligation cost recovery from a kW-based charge (strawman, Ex. 17.0 at 21:333-346) to the volumetric kWh Supply charge in the Mid-Day Peak period; Transmission and Miscellaneous Charge stays a single fixed-kWh rate as for Rate BES -->

**A.** Under ComEd's strawman Rate BEST proposal presented in its direct testimony on rehearing, 100% of the capacity obligation costs were to be recovered through a kW-based capacity obligation charge. See Kremer RH Dir., ComEd Ex. 17.0 at 21:333-346. In response to critiques regarding ease of customer use by CUB-EDF and ELPC-VS-UCS, under ComEd's Compromise Proposal, ComEd will recover 100% the capacity obligation costs through the volumetric kWh Supply charge in the Mid-Day Peak period. The Transmission and Miscellaneous Charge will continue to be recovered as it is today for Rate BES – Basic Electric Service ("Rate BES") customers (i.e., ComEd's non-hourly bundled supply customers), which is through a single fixed-kWh rate. ComEd witness Kremer provides more details on the Compromise Proposal in her rebuttal testimony on rehearing. See ComEd Ex. 19.0.

**Q.** Do other parties support ComEd's Compromise Proposal?

<!-- EPON: goal — CUB-EDF and ELPC-VS-UCS support, per the data-request responses (Exs. 18.01, 18.02, 18.03); see those exhibits for the exact question asked -->

**A.** Yes. CUB-EDF and ELPC-VS-UCS support ComEd's Compromise Proposal. See ComEd Exs. 18.01; 18.02; and 18.03.

**Q.** What objectives are met by ComEd's Rate BEST Compromise Proposal?

**A.** The Rate BEST Compromise Proposal is designed to meet both compliance and broader policy goals. The Rate BEST Compromise Proposal satisfies the Commission's directive to provide an STOU rate strawman within 120 days of the Final Order that is harmonized with the recently approved DTOU rate. It also reflects that ComEd engaged in collaborative discussions among ComEd and stakeholders to further refine the strawman.

[p. 6]

<!-- EPON: goal, bundling, midday-peak, bill-comparison — KEY policy statement: "easy-to-use tool"; only four periods; "alignment and mandatory pairing with DTOU"; shifts usage via a "significantly higher mid-day peak rate"; "customer-centric design will minimize participating customers' efforts while maximizing their savings opportunities" -->

From a policy perspective, ComEd's Rate BEST Compromise Proposal is designed to provide residential customers with an easy-to-use tool to help them manage their energy use and their energy costs. ComEd designed Rate BEST to be intuitive to understand and use (e.g., having only four periods of time, and alignment and mandatory pairing with DTOU). It incentivizes customer behavior that shifts energy usage to non-peak hours through a simple price signal (i.e., the four daily time periods, with a significantly higher mid-day peak rate). This customer-centric design will minimize participating customers' efforts while maximizing their savings opportunities. It also has the potential to drive grid and environmental benefits by shifting usage to non-peak hours.

**Q.** Are there similar historical examples that support ComEd's customer behavioral focus on TOU periods?

<!-- EPON: goal, midday-peak — rate is designed to "train customers" to shift overall energy management to lower-price periods; analogy to legacy telephone "nights and weekends" pricing (premise: discretionary, shiftable usage) -->

**A.** Yes. ComEd's Rate BEST Compromise Proposal is designed to train customers to familiarize themselves with the TOU time periods and shift their overall energy management behavior to lower-price time periods. This time period-based behavioral modification is similar to efforts by legacy telephone companies like AT&T and MCI to incentivize customers to shift their calls to lower volume periods (i.e., "nights and weekends") by offering lowering [sic] prices during those periods. See, e.g., [2003 Verizon Wireless "Unlimited Nights and Weekend Minutes" USA TV Ad](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TQNpYI0KZYo).

[p. 7]

**Q.** Does shifting capacity obligation cost recovery from a kW-based demand charge (i.e., ComEd's proposal presented in its direct testimony on rehearing) to the volumetric kWh-based supply charge (i.e., ComEd's Compromise Proposal) emphasize different behavioral incentives?

<!-- EPON: capacity-mdppec, midday-peak, goal — kW charge targeted specific high-system-demand days/times (e.g. a hot summer afternoon); kWh-in-Mid-Day-Peak targets "systemic" daily behavior across the four periods, i.e. every Mid-Day Peak kWh bears capacity cost regardless of system conditions that day -->

**A.** Yes. While both approaches focus on driving customer behavioral changes, they strive for different goals. The demand-based capacity charge recovery mechanism presented in ComEd's direct testimony on rehearing was focused on driving customer behavior at a more granular level (i.e., customers must change their behavior on specific days and times where there is likely to be high system capacity demand, like a hot summer afternoon), while the volumetric kWh-based supply charge recovery mechanism reflected in the Compromise Proposal is more focused on driving systemic customer behavior (i.e., managing daily electric use based on the four daily TOU periods).

**Q.** How does ComEd intend to engage in a customer education campaign to help customers understand the mechanics of the Rate BEST Compromise Proposal, and whether Rate BEST may be right for them based on their daily experiences?

<!-- EPON: goal, midday-peak, bill-comparison — marketing will emphasize the 1pm–7pm Mid-Day Peak premium vs the other three periods, "programmable technologies", "set it and forget it" -->

**A.** ComEd's customer education and marketing campaign for the Rate BEST Compromise Proposal will focus predominantly on the four daily time periods, and emphasize the rate difference between the most expensive period (the Midday Peak Period from 1 pm to 7 pm) and the other three periods. In addition, it will educate customers about how undertaking their daily activities during these different periods can result in lower energy costs, and it will encourage customers to use the programmable technologies to maximize potential savings. Increasing customers' awareness of the time periods, and how their

[p. 8]

energy usage behavior during those time periods impacts their energy bill, provides the simplest way for customers to "set it and forget it" when it comes to their energy management at home.

<!-- EPON: goal, profile, bill-comparison, midday-peak — KEY: admits Rate BEST "may not be the best option for every residential customer" (e.g. those who cannot reliably shift to off-peak); ComEd will help customers self-select; period rates will fluctuate year to year with supply cost, capacity cost, and "the behavior of fellow participants (e.g., a change in the peak load contribution of the class)" — i.e. the participant-class profile feeds back into rates -->

Rate BEST (coupled with DTOU) may not be the best option for every residential customer (e.g., a customer that doesn't think they can reliably shift their usage to off-peak periods), so ComEd will also focus on helping customers determine if Rate BEST (taken with the DTOU rate) is the best rate for them based on their lifestyle. This will require helping customers understand how the rate works, and bring general awareness to the fact that while the daily time periods are static, the daily periods' rates will fluctuate year-to-year, depending on factors including energy supply costs, capacity costs, and the behavior of fellow participants (e.g., a change in the peak load contribution of the class).

## III. RATE BEST COST RECOVERY AND REVENUE NEUTRALITY

**Q.** What categories of costs will ComEd incur related to Rate BEST?

**A.** There are two categories of costs associated with implementing the STOU: (1) supply procurement costs, and (2) administration costs (which includes IT, implementation, customer education and marketing, and program administration costs).

**Q.** How does ComEd propose to recover these costs?

<!-- EPON: fixed-vs-volumetric, capacity-mdppec — supply procurement costs pass through directly to STOU customers (reconciled in annual Power Procurement proceedings); administration costs (IT, marketing, etc.) recovered through distribution revenue requirements, i.e. from all customers -->

**A.** The two different types of costs will be recovered in two different ways. Supply procurement costs will be recovered directly from STOU customers as a pass-through, just as they are recovered today for ComEd customers taking supply under ComEd's other supply rate offerings. As with ComEd's bundled flat rate (Rate BES) and hourly rate (Rate BESH) offerings, these costs will be reviewed and reconciled in ComEd's annual Power Procurement reconciliation proceedings.[^2]

[^2]: ComEd's annual reconciliation of revenues collected under power procurement riders with actual cost associated with power procurement expenditures proceedings are commonly referred to as its "Power Procurement" proceedings.

ComEd proposes to recover the Rate BEST administrative costs in the same manner as it will recover the DTOU administrative costs, which is through ComEd's distribution revenue requirements.

[p. 10]

**Q.** Are these two cost categories the same for the Compromise Proposal as they were for the initial strawman Rate BEST proposal?

**A.** Yes.

**Q.** Why is it appropriate to recover the STOU rate administrative costs through the delivery services rate?

<!-- EPON: bundling, fixed-vs-volumetric — rationale for socializing STOU administrative costs into delivery rates: joint implementation/marketing synergies of the harmonized DTOU+STOU; both rates "available to all residential customers"; avoid deterring early adopters -->

**A.** Part of the synergies and efficiencies inherent to harmonizing the DTOU and Rate BEST STOU is that they can and will be jointly implemented and marketed to customers. This will help reduce overall project costs compared to if they had disparate designs and had to be implemented and marketed separately. For example, the IT development to create the kWh groups for DTOU will be leveraged to support Rate BEST too. Because the daily time periods are the same, marketing and customer education materials will be developed so that they support both TOU offerings.

The two TOU offerings' intertwined nature means that any attempt to allocate administrative costs between various cost-recovery mechanisms would be imprecise, unnecessarily complicated, and unwarranted. Recovering these joint administrative costs from all customers in distribution rates makes sense as both the DTOU and the STOU will be available to all residential customers. Furthermore, recovering the administrative costs through distribution charges supports the future success of the STOU as it will not deter potential early adopters with prohibitively high rates.

[p. 11]

**Q.** Why would recovering Rate BEST administrative costs only from STOU customers, as recommended by ICEA-RESA witness Wright, deter potential early adopters with prohibitively high rates? See Wright RH Dir., ICEA-RESA Ex. 1.0RH at 5:91-99.

<!-- EPON: bill-comparison — illustrative arithmetic: total annual joint marketing cost for DTOU + Rate BEST "no more than \$500,000"; hypothetical \$350,000 total (\$250,000 DTOU / \$100,000 BEST) over 100 enrolled customers = \$83 per customer per month if borne by BEST customers vs \$0.007 per month spread across delivery customers; ComEd expects only a "handful of initial Rate BEST adopters" -->

**A.** As I mentioned earlier, ComEd planned to jointly market its DTOU and Rate BEST. At this time, ComEd estimates that total annual marketing costs for the two rates will be no more than \$500,000. While a relatively small program budget, recovering this amount from the handful of initial Rate BEST adopters would be a steep amount per residential customer.

For example, let's assume that the joint TOU marketing costs were \$350,000, of which \$250,000 was allocated to DTOU, and \$100,000 was allocated to Rate BEST. In that year, assume there were 100 customers enrolled in Rate BEST. Under ICEA-RESA witness Wright's proposal, the \$100,000 would be recovered from the 100 Rate BEST customers, which would result in a monthly bill impact of \$83 per Rate BEST customer. Such a result would deter any customer enrollment in Rate BEST.

In contrast, recovering the total TOU marketing costs of \$350,000 from delivery service rates would result in less than a penny per month per customer (\$0.007).

**Q.** Does the STOU discussed in rehearing have to be "revenue neutral," as suggested by Staff witness Martin? See Martin RH Dir., Staff Ex. 13.0 at 3:52-54.

<!-- EPON: fixed-vs-volumetric, goal — ComEd says the rehearing STOU need not be revenue neutral; the DTOU's IT/billing, education, marketing and administration incremental costs are recovered through the distribution revenue requirement (cites Beneficial Electrification Plan 1 order) -->

**A.** No. Staff witness Martin's concern appears to be based on the idea that every topic discussed and decided in a rate design investigation, including on rehearing, is limited solely to considering revenue neutral changes to rate design. See Martin RH Dir., Staff Ex. 13.0 at 2:26-39. While I generally agree with this premise (and ComEd argued as much in

[p. 12]

the underlying case), the Commission has recognized in its January 16, 2025 Order and elsewhere that other issues that are not revenue neutral are relevant and appropriate for discussion in this rate design investigation. For example, in response to the Commission's final order in ComEd's Beneficial Electrification Plan 1 proceeding (Docket Nos. 22-0432/22-0442 (cons.)), ComEd proposed, and the Commission adopted, a DTOU rate in this proceeding. While inherently related to rate design, the DTOU requires upgrades to ComEd's information technology and billing systems, as well as customer education, marketing, and program administration, all of which will incur incremental costs to be recovered through the distribution revenue requirement. See ComEd's Application for Rehearing at 7-9. Any such costs incurred will be reviewed for prudency and reasonableness in ComEd's annual multi-year rate plan reconciliation proceeding. As I discussed above, the Rate BEST Compromise Proposal is expected to incur similar types of costs, which will be recovered and reviewed in a similar manner.

**Q.** Will the STOU cost allocation impact Staff's ongoing investigation of the appropriate cost allocation between supply and delivery service costs, as suggested by Staff witness Martin? See Martin RH Dir., Staff Ex. 13.0 at 3:63 – 4:73.

**A.** No. Staff is currently conducting an informal (non-docketed) investigation into the allocation of delivery and supply costs, and a Staff Report is anticipated by January 2026. The supply and administration cost categories for Rate BEST are the same as the categories of costs ComEd incurs for its other supply rate options. Thus, these costs could be allocated to supply in the same manner as its existing supply rates. Furthermore, ComEd would modify the Rate BEST cost allocation if the Commission were to order the modification to

[p. 13]

cost allocation between supply and delivery service costs as a result of Staff's ongoing investigation.

## IV. IDC WAIVER AND RETAIL COMPETITION

**Q.** Why isn't ComEd seeking an IDC waiver for the implementation of STOU?

**A.** Contrary to assertions by Staff witness Capellan, it would be premature to seek an IDC waiver. The Commission has not approved an STOU rate. As I noted in my direct testimony on rehearing (Perkins RH Dir., ComEd Ex. 16.0 at 14:272 – 15:282), if the Commission approves Rate BEST and determines that a waiver from the Commission's IDC Rules is required in order for ComEd to implement and market Rate BEST, then ComEd will seek such a waiver.

**Q.** If the Commission were to reject ComEd's Compromise Proposal and ComEd were ordered to recover capacity obligation costs through a demand-based charge, would ComEd receive a "financial advantage" in charging a demand charge, as claimed by CUB-EDF witness McCann? See McCann RH Dir., CUB-EDF Ex. 4.0 CORR at 15:275-283.

<!-- EPON: capacity-mdppec, tmc — ComEd: a demand charge is "merely a mechanism" to recover PJM capacity and transmission charges with no effect on total supply cost; supply is a pass-through procured via competitive auction with no markup -->

**A.** No. A demand charge is a merely [sic] mechanism through which ComEd can recover the capacity and transmission charges from PJM – it has no impact on the total supply costs incurred to be recovered from customers. See also Kremer RH Dir., ComEd Ex. 17.0 at 18:289 – 21:332 (discussing how capacity and transmission charges are incurred). Also, ComEd does not make money on supply rates. Illinois has a competitive retail market, and supply for ComEd-supplied customers is procured by ComEd through a competitive auction, with results approved by the Commission. The costs related thereto, including

[p. 14]

supply-administration costs, are passed through to customers without any markup or discount.

**Q.** Will a ComEd-offered supply TOU rate negatively impact retail competition?

<!-- EPON: lockout, goal — retail competition: ComEd says Rider IMUD gives RESs interval data for 12 months before Rate BEST starts, so RESs can offer competing TOU products -->

**A.** No. Notably, Staff witness Capellan and ICEA-RESA witness Wright have provided no evidentiary basis for their concern. See Capellan RH Dir., Staff Ex. 14.0 at 6:123-129; Wright RH Dir., ICEA-RESA Ex. 1.0RH at 3:43-65. They allege that there is a barrier for RES entry to TOU rate offerings because of a lack of access to interval data. But this is untrue. As I stated in my direct testimony (see Perkins RH Dir., ComEd Ex. 16.0 at 6:110 – 7:122), if the Commission decides to approve Rate BEST, Rider IMUD – Interval Meter Usage Data will provide RESs access to interval data for 12 months before Rate BEST goes into effect. That timeline will provide RESs sufficient time to design and offer TOU products to compete with Rate BEST in the retail marketplace.

**Q.** If the Commission were to reject ComEd's Compromise Proposal and ComEd were ordered to recover capacity obligation costs through a demand-based charge, would ComEd be taking advantage of its position as the monopoly utility in order to impose demand charges that could not otherwise be charged in a competitive marketplace, as alleged by CUB-EDF witness McCann? See McCann RH Dir., CUB-EDF Ex. 4.0 CORR at 14:262– 15:269.

<!-- EPON: capacity-mdppec — ComEd sees no legal or logistical barrier to a RES recovering capacity costs through a demand charge (ComEd already sends RESs PLC and kWh data via EDI) -->

**A.** No. CUB-EDF witness McCann provides no legal or factual basis for his allegations. I am not aware of any applicable state or federal law that prevents RESs from recovering capacity costs through a demand charge. In addition, I am not aware of any logistical barrier to a RES recovering capacity costs through a demand change [sic; context indicates "demand charge"]. For example, when

[p. 15]

a RES is serving a customer, ComEd currently sends RESs data regarding customers' PLCs and kWh usage through its Electronic Data Interchange system for RES billing purposes, which could be utilized to formulate and implement a demand charge.

## V. CONCLUSION

**Q.** Does that conclude your rebuttal testimony on rehearing?

**A.** Yes.
