**I. INTRODUCTION AND PURPOSE**

**Q.** Please state your name, business address, and on whose behalf you are testifying.

**A.** My name is Juan-Pablo Velez. I am Co-founder and Executive Director of Switchbox, 1 Whitehall Street, 17th Floor, New York, NY 10004\. I am testifying on behalf of the Citizens Utility Board (“CUB”).

**Q.** Are you the same Juan-Pablo Velez who submitted direct testimony in this proceeding as CUB Exhibit 4.0?

**A.** Yes.

**Q.** What is the purpose of your rebuttal testimony?

**A.** I respond to three rebuttals filed by The Peoples Gas Light and Coke Company (“PGL” or “the Company”) that address my direct testimony: Theodore Eidukas (NSG-PGL Ex. 15.0); the joint rebuttal of Eldringhoff and Dickson (NSG-PGL Ex. 17.0); and the Brattle Group’s Graves, Figueroa, and Sreenath (NSG-PGL Ex. 21.0).

**Q.** Does anything in PGL’s rebuttal cause you to modify the recommendations in your direct testimony, and will you respond to every part of PGL’s rebuttal?

**A.** No to both. I continue to recommend the same three-part order from my direct testimony — an independent, system-wide hydraulic-feasibility-and-sequencing study; release of its inputs for independent verification; and a follow-on process to produce a _plan_ for a potential targeted electrification "track" within the PRP program — restated in full in Section VII. I will address only PGL's most significant objections; my silence on any other point does not indicate agreement with it.

**II. WHAT MY ANALYSIS FOUND AND HOW PGL RECHARACTERIZES IT**

**Q.** Please summarize what your direct testimony found and recommended.

**A.** Two findings and one request. First, a substantial share of the residential blocks in PGL’s Pipe Replacement Program (“PRP”) would be cheaper to electrify than to replace pipe for: “\[a\]pproximately 14.3% — 213 of the 1,488 in-scope fully residential blocks” are cost-effective to electrify even under the strictest test.[^1] Second, PGL’s current process “cannot capture more than a negligible share of” that potential, for a reason that “is structural.”[^2] The request was a three-part order: a system-wide hydraulic-feasibility-and-sequencing study (Part 1); release of its inputs for independent verification (Part 2); and a follow-on process to “translate the Part 1 findings into an actionable plan” (Part 3\).[^3]

**Q.** How does PGL characterize your testimony?

**A.** Accurately on the findings, then inaccurately on the ask. PGL’s witnesses mischaracterize my proposal as a request to “establish a parallel decommissioning program alongside the existing PRP,”[^5] then argue my analysis is not complete enough to support _that program_. As Section III shows, that is an argument against a proposal I did not make.

**Q.** Does PGL agree PGL’s “riskiest-first” project selection process is structurally incompatible with large-scale TE?

**A.** Effectively, yes — though its witnesses frame it as criticism.

Eldringhoff and Dickson claim that "for higher-risk segments, delaying action to evaluate or coordinate electrification would be inconsistent" with the ICC's directive to "PGL to prioritize safety."[^6] This is precisely the "timing" problem I identified in my testimony, one of the two reasons I offer for why the current PRP project-selection process and emerging NPA evaluation framework cannot capture more than a negligible share of the cost-effective TE potential identified in my analysis: Once a segment has been selected on the basis of its risk score, the 'would it be feasible to electrify this block instead?' question is, by construction, being asked about the riskiest remaining pipe in the system. The likely answer: 'we cannot wait two to three years for full block electrification, because the pipe is too risky. We need to replace it now.'"[^6b]

In addition, Eldringhoff and Dickson argue that large-scale TE would require PGL to simultaneously execute its existing PRP while also planning and coordinating large-scale decommissioning efforts.[^6] This is precisely why I asked for a plan to design a program that could perform large-scale electrification. That such a program would represent a significant undertaking is not a reason to avoid the planning: it is the reason to do it. Without it, one in four of Chicago's pipes will be replaced over the next decade, at the same time that the City is trying to electrify one in three of its buildings.

**III. PGL MISCHARACTERIZES MY PROPOSAL AS A PROGRAM**

**Q.** What is the central mischaracterization of your testimony in PGL’s rebuttal?

**A.** That I asked the Commission to order a large-scale TE program. For instance, Eldringhoff claims that I recommend that the Commission "establish a parallel decommissioning program alongside the existing PRP."[^5] I did not. Similarly, Eidukas characterizes my proposal as an "electrification pilot" that should therefore be addressed in the Future of Gas proceeding, where the Commission is considering such proposals.[^8]

However, Part 3 of my request actually asked for a follow-on process to design “a ‘track’ under the PRP program that would allow PGL to decommission a meaningful share of” the cost-effective blocks[^7]. That is, I asked for a _plan_ of that track, not for a _program_. On this point, my testimony was explicit: “None of the three parts asks the Commission to commit to a Track 2 program at the present time.”[^4]

**Q.** What did your proposal actually ask for?

**A.** It asked for a _system-wide study_ that would determine the pipe segments within the PRP program that would be hydraulically feasible to decommission (under a decommissioning sequence that maximizes system-wide hydraulic feasibility), and a follow-on process that would produce a _plan_ for expanding PGL's operations to perform targeted electrification on cost-effective, hydraulically feasible segments (the "Track 2" program). _Our proposal does not call for such a program to be created at this time._

Rather, the goal of our proposal is for the ICC to have the information it needs to understand (1) the potential scale of such a program, based on the number of hydraulically feasible and cost-effective segments within the PRP program, and (2) the internal capacity PGL would need to develop to execute it, so that it can explicitly decide whether and how to proceed with such a program.

**Q.** Witness Eidukas argues that your proposal is “premature” and belongs in the Future of Gas process. How do you respond?

**A.** Witness Eidukas writes that “targeted electrification proposals and request for a new docketed proceeding are premature and should be deferred to the Future of Gas process.” Again, this assumes I proposed a TE pilot, which would indeed require the prior resolution of "threshold legal, policy, and funding questions that must be resolved before electrification
pilots can proceed".[^8] I did not propose a TE pilot. I proposed a _study_, and a follow-on process that would produce a _plan_. Nor did I insist that this follow-on process take the form of a new, separate docketed proceeding. If the Commission determines that the existing Future of Gas docket is the appropriate venue for this follow-on planning process to occur, I have no objection.

As for whether the study part of my proposal can be ordered in this rate case, I am not an attorney, and I now offer my understanding rather than a legal conclusion.[^9] The question I raise — whether ratepayers should fund replacing pipe that may not need replacing — is a prudence question, the core business of a rate case. As I understand the Commission’s recent orders, a pilot or program may be directed to the Future of Gas series, but a study of this kind is appropriately ordered in a rate case, decided case-by-case rather than by categorical rule.[^10] Indeed, in a 2025 rate case the Commission approved a utility’s zonal-electrification “Territory Study,” holding that it “need not and should not wait for the conclusion of \[the Future of Gas series\].”[^11]

**Q.** What are the consequences of PGL's mischaracterization of your testimony?

**A.** Much of PGL's rebuttal testimony becomes a non sequitur. Several witnesses argue that due to the limitations of our analysis, our proposal, which they assume to be a large-scale TE program, should therefore be rejected. For instance, Witness Eidukas concludes that because our analysis does not include hydraulic feasibility, assumes 100% customer participation, and "considers only upfront capital costs" rather than "full lifecycle cost analysis", it “may (or may not) provide a preliminary indication of potential scale, \[but\] it is not a suitable basis for Commission action.”[^13] Similarly, witnesses Eldringhoff and Dickson claim that my analysis is not “a reliable basis for comparing targeted electrification to pipe replacement” in order to "inform an implementable utility decision" because it “excludes multiple categories of cost and feasibility.”[^12]

However, that my analysis is not complete enough to single-handedly justify large-scale electrification is beside the point, _when I have not asked the Commission to order it_. The question is whether our analysis indeed offers a sufficiently robust preliminary indication of potential scale to justify the "Commission action" on TE we are actually asking for, which is further study and planning. We contend that it does, as further discussed in Sections IV and V.

**IV. WHETHER STUDY LIMITATIONS MEAN MY PROPOSAL SHOULD BE REJECTED**

**Q.** Does the lack of hydraulic feasibility in your study mean your "study and plan" proposal should be rejected?

**A.** No. Let's assume for a moment that in citing these study limitations as a reason for the Commission to reject our proposal, the witnesses meant our actual proposal to "study and plan", rather than a proposal to create a TE program. Under that reading of their objections, their logic becomes circular: the ICC should reject our request for a system-wide hydraulic feasibility study because our analysis lacks hydraulic feasibility. That argument would defeat any feasibility study proposal. Moreover, no one yet knows the system-wide answer — as Witness Eidukas quotes from my testimony, not even “PGL, because nobody has run the sequencing analysis”[^14] — and only PGL holds the data such a study requires, which it has not released.

**Q.** Does the assumption of 100% customer participation in your study mean your "study and plan" proposal should be rejected?

**A.** No. Customer participation is an implementation challenge to solve (in the follow-on process), not an obstacle to initiating a feasibility study and plan in the first place.

Across the rebuttals, PGL argues that because near-universal participation is hard to achieve, electrification cannot substitute for pipe retirement and should not be pursued. Witnesses Eldringhoff and Dickson say my analysis “assumes full customer participation,” and that “absent near-universal participation … targeted electrification cannot function as a substitute for pipe retirement.”[^30] The Brattle witnesses say “customer persuasion to reach 100% participation is not a realistic, scalable NPA prospect.”[^31] However, if one requires 100% participation _and_ assumes it can never be achieved, then targeted electrification — or any approach that retires a shared gas main — could never work anywhere. That is not a finding about my analysis: it is a refusal to engage the question. (Which is unsurprising, given that PGL's profits are directly tied to pipe replacement.)

The whole purpose of the study and plan is to determine how large the opportunity is and what capturing it would take, including “social feasibility” questions, whose answers may call for a regulatory or legislative adjustment to the obligation to serve, combined with robust customer outreach. In other words, the participation challenge is a policy problem regulators are actively solving elsewhere, not a law of nature: in California, SB 1221 (2024) authorizes neighborhood-scale gas-decommissioning pilots that proceed on the consent of roughly two-thirds of affected customers rather than unanimity,[^33] and extensive block-level marketing and engagement is underway in PG&E's territory, with the utility's support.

**Q.** Does the lack of a full lifecycle cost analysis in your study mean your "study and plan" proposal should be rejected?

**A.** No. Witness Eidukas notes that our analysis "considers only upfront capital costs and explicitly excludes ongoing operational costs, utility rate impacts, and financing mechanisms," citing this as one of the reasons that our analysis "is not a suitable basis for Commission action.”[^13] Given that the capital costs of these capital investments dwarf their attendant operating costs, and given that utility rate impacts could vary dramatically based on how the ICC designs cost-recovery, we contend that upfront capital costs are sufficient to provide a preliminary indication of potential scale for cost-effective TE. Moreover, excluding operating costs actually favors pipe replacement: it ignores decades of operating costs associated with rebuilt pipes, which will be far higher than any incremental operating costs on the electric side, which are largely independent from load. That said, we would welcome a full lifecycle cost analysis to be performed as part of the feasibility study.

**V. PGL OBJECTIONS TO THE HYDRAULIC FEASIBILITY STUDY**

**Q.** PGL claims that the hydraulic feasibility study is not needed because this topic will be addressed through the NPA framework being developed by Charles River Associates. How do you respond?

**A.** According to Witness Eidukas, "The Commission should decline to order a system-wide independent hydraulic feasibility study at this time. Consideration of hydraulic feasibility is an inherent part of the NPA evaluation framework that CRA is already developing." This argument, that hydraulic feasibility is already considered in CRA's NPA framework, entirely ignores the second reason I identified in my testimony for why the current PRP project-selection process and emerging NPA evaluation framework cannot capture more than a negligible share of the cost-effective TE potential identified in my analysis: whether it is hydraulically feasible to decommission a particular pipe segment "depends on which adjacent segments have already been decommissioned." Witnesses Eldringhoff and Dickson make this same argument,[^TK] but neglect to take it to its logical conclusion: "Decommissioning is thus a _network_ problem, not a project-by-project problem... maximizing the hydraulic feasibility of TE requires system-wide planning and proactive decommissioning, not a reactive project-by-project NPA evaluation." It is therefore highly disingenuous for PGL to claim that CRA's project-level NPA framework is a suitable replacement for the system-wide study we are requesting. Only a system-wide study can accurately reveal the maximum number of segments that are hydraulically feasible to decommission, and PGL has a clear financial incentive for this system-level potential not to be revealed, so as to avoid proactive decommissioning and thereby minimize the number of segments that are hydraulically feasible to decommission.

**Q.** Witness Eidukas also argues it is premature to perform your study before the NPA framework is “finalized and approved.” How do you respond?

**A.** This doesn't stand up to scrutiny. Hydraulic feasibility will obviously be a criterion in any viable NPA framework. Accurately measuring maximum hydraulic feasibility through a system-wide study therefore does not depend on the NPA framework being finished first. On the contrary: given the 2035 timeline, the schedule demands the two proceed in parallel.

**Q.** Witness Eidukas suggests that conducting a hydraulic feasibility study could jeopardize their 2035 PRP deadline. Is that credible?

**A.** No. Eidukas implies that the study would jeopardize the 2035 PRP deadline: "ordering a system-wide study now would effectively require the Companies to divert engineering and operational resources from meeting the Commission’s 2035 retirement directive—the very directive that is designed to ensure the safety and reliability of Peoples Gas’s distribution system, and which implicates the threat of substantial monetary penalties for non-compliance."[^19]

However, the study’s cost is small in context — a minuscule fraction of the roughly $188 million in PRP project spending PGL has planned for 2026 alone,[^21] against a program on the order of $4.3 billion.[^22] The notion that PGL would be unable to hire an external firm to do a computational engineering study without putting a multi-billion-dollar capital program at risk does not withstand scrutiny; and if it did, that would indict the Company's ability to execute the PRP program in the first place.

**VI. MY UPFRONT COST ESTIMATES WITHSTAND PGL'S CRITIQUES**

**Q.** The Witnesses made several criticisms of your up-front capital cost analysis. Taken as a whole, do they invalidate your conclusion that approximately 14% of fully residential blocks are strictly cheaper to electrify than pipe replacement, and that there are likely hundreds to thousands of blocks that are cost-effective to electrify in the city as a whole?

**A.** No. I stand by my conclusions that the results offer a sufficiently solid preliminary indication of the potential scale of TE to warrant a more complete system-wide study. In particular, it seems very likely that TE is cost-effective in lower-density neighborhoods like Englewood, which have many empty lots. The items in this section address Witness criticisms in detail.

**Q.** The Brattle witnesses say you excluded blocks with mixed customers, “which would be most of Chicago.” Is that correct?

**A.** No. They assert I “excluded reviewing any blocks that have mixed types of customers (which would be most of Chicago),”[^23] but that assertion does not square with data from the County Assessor: 72.3% of the in-scope PRP blocks (1,488 of roughly 2,057) are fully residential, not mixed.[^24] It is also an obvious fact to anyone who has lived in Chicago.

**Q.** The Brattle witnesses argue that my results "do not support his conclusions that the scope for electrification is 'large.'" How do you respond?

**A.** The witnesses base this assertion on the following argument: "Witness Velez evaluates only 108 miles of pipe out of the 1,020 miles of CI/DI mains that PGL must retire by 2035. That means that even if he found that 100% of “in-scope” blocks cost less to electrify than PRP, he will have only avoided 10.6% of the PRP." First, this 10% sample largely reflects the fact that, despite requesting to replace nearly one in four of the pipes in Chicago, PGL has not made publicly available a map of where these pipes are located. We were therefore forced to use the planned project map on their website, which represents a much smaller subset of those 1,020 miles. Second, we trust that Brattle's witnesses are familiar with the notion of a statistical sample: we examine a portion of fully residential blocks, assume that our results generalize to the entire population of fully residential blocks, and ask for a full study precisely to test the validity of that inference across the entire system. If it holds, then we estimate that on the order of 2,000 fully residential blocks are cost-effective to electrify (before taking hydraulic feasibility into account). Whether the scope is "large" and would "meaningfully reduce the scale of the PRP" is a question of semantics: substantively, under the current process, PGL will likely only electrify a few dozen blocks by 2035, if it electrifies any at all.

**Q.** Witnesses Eldringhoff and Dickson argue your single blended-average cost-per-mile figure obscures variation. How do you respond?

**A.** The witnesses argue that my apparently cost-effective blocks “may simply be blocks where PRP costs happen to be below the system average.”[^26] But that logic cuts both ways: some blocks that did _not_ pass my screen likely have above-average pipe replacement costs and are in fact cost-effective to electrify. Disaggregating the blended average moves blocks in both directions, which the Witnesses neglected to mention. In any event, my testimony acknowledged the blended program-wide average as a simplification that should be refined in the full system-wide study — one more reason to conduct one.

**Q.** The Brattle witnesses argue your $28,706 per-home electrification cost is "toward the low end" of public estimates. How do you respond?

**A.** The witnesses cite a Lawrence Berkeley Lab presentation that finds that "achieving >50% reductions in carbon emissions typically requires investments of at least $50,000 per home." However, this number reflects a national average. My figure[^27] comes from ComEd’s CY2024 Cost-Effectiveness Analysis, in which Guidehouse compiled actual costs from implementer invoices and program tracking data under real Chicago-area programs. Given the heterogeneity of the U.S. housing stock, I claim that using local program data provides a better cost estimate for Chicago-area single-family electrification projects than a nationwide average.

In addition, it is worth noting that the analysis assumes the most expensive electrification scenario: one where every unit on a block must be fully electrified. In reality, some units will have already partially electrified. Relaxing this assumption lowers electrification’s modeled cost at the block level.

**Q.** The Brattle witnesses argue that sensitizing your housing-cost assumption upward, and adding the administrative costs of electrification at scale, undercut your numbers. How do you respond?

**A.** Again, a fair comparison must move both sides of the ledger. The Brattle witnesses move only the side that hurts electrification. If the electrification cost is sensitized upward, the _pipe-replacement_ cost should be too — and utility construction costs have been rising substantially faster than general inflation in recent years.[^29] The same asymmetry applies to administrative costs: against any administrative cost of electrification one must net the _reduced_ PRP admin costs PGL would no longer incur. (We declined to include these costs in our analysis because they would be speculative, which is why we instead asked for these costs to be developed through the follow-on process and plan.) Finally, if we wish to include the additional costs associated with block-level electrification, we should also include its _savings_: one of the key advantages of targeted electrification is precisely the ability to reduce hard and soft costs of electrification through the economies of scale.

**VII. CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATION**

**Q.** Please restate your recommendation.

**A.** My recommendation is unchanged from my direct testimony, and PGL’s rebuttals reinforce it. I recommend that the Commission order PGL to: (1) conduct, or fund an independent party to conduct, a system-wide hydraulic-feasibility-and-sequencing study; (2) release that study’s inputs — non-sensitive inputs publicly, and sensitive pipe-topology and customer data to qualified intervenors under NDA — so the analysis can be independently verified; and (3) undertake a follow-on process that produces a _plan_ for how PGL could capture a meaningful share of the cost-effective, hydraulically feasible electrification potential my analysis indicates is out there. None of these ask the Commission to commit to an electrification program today. They ask it to develop the information needed to decide — information that, on this record, only PGL holds and has not produced. PGL’s central objection, that my analysis does not support ordering a program, answers a proposal I did not make. What I proposed is the study and the plan.

**Q.** Does this conclude your rebuttal testimony?

**A.** Yes, it does.
