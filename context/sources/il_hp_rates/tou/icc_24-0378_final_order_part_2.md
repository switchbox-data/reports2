## VI. TARIFFS

### A. Rider NS – Nonstandard Services and Facilities

#### 1. Rider NS Overview

##### a. ComEd’s Position

According to ComEd, Rider NS – Nonstandard Services and Facilities (“Rider NS”) allows ComEd to address and charge for the unique service needs of its customers. Rider NS, ILL. C. C. No. 10, Sheet Nos. 277-281; ComEd Ex. 9.0 REV. at 7. Rider NS ensures that those same customers pay the costs associated with their nonstandard electric needs, i.e., to ensure the costs associated with nonstandard service are not socialized among all customers. Rider NS, ILL. C. C. No. 10, Sheet Nos. 278-280; _see also_ ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 63-64. This rider furthers the principle: “the cost of supplying public utility services is allocated to those who cause the costs to be incurred,” or simply put, “cost causation.” 220 ILCS 5/1-102(d)(iii); ComEd Ex. 9.0 REV. at 7-8.

In accordance with the Act and its Commission-approved rates, ComEd provides an individual customer with facilities that are adequate to serve that customer’s electric usage requirements at a single delivery point. _See_ General Terms and Conditions, ILL. C. C. No. 10, 5th Revised Sheet No. 155. These are called “standard” services or facilities. _Id._ ComEd works with new customers to appropriately size facilities to provide standard service at a single delivery point. ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 63. Standard facilities can range from a simple secondary voltage connection to serve a small lighting customer, to a high voltage electric service station to serve a large non-residential customer. _Id._ at 63-64. There are additional parameters related to standard distribution facilities provided in

the General Terms and Conditions section of ComEd’s Schedule of Rates. _Id._ at 64; _see also_ General Terms and Conditions, ILL. C. C. No. 10, Sheet Nos. 154.1-186.

Rider NS is also the mechanism by which a customer pays the incremental costs above those that are included in base rates for standard service for any customer requesting that ComEd provide services that are larger, more, or different than standard, i.e. “nonstandard.” ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 63. Rider NS also allows ComEd to collect the costs of relocating facilities to accommodate customer construction, such as when a residential customer plans to build a deck for a swimming pool that could conflict with their service line and requests the line to be relocated. ComEd Ex. 9.0 REV. at 8.

There are two formulas in Rider NS to determine the incremental dollar amount above costs for standard service paid by a customer for its nonstandard services and facilities. First, a single cost payment formula on Sheet Nos. 278-280 determines one-time costs, like incremental ComEd labor costs and nonreusable ComEd material costs, to determine a customer’s Contribution In Aid of Construction (“CIAC”). ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 64. Second, a monthly rental cost payment formula on Sheet No. 280 determines the incremental costs of materials, such as transformation or switching facilities, that could potentially be reused elsewhere on the ComEd system if they were no longer needed by the original customer. _Id._

On average, ComEd collects CIAC payments from approximately 4,000 customers per year and monthly rental payments from approximately 16,000 customers per year under Rider NS. ComEd Ex. 9.0 REV. at 9. In the last five years, ComEd has avoided at least $500 million of incremental costs that would have been borne by all customers if they had not been collected from these cost-causers through Rider NS. _Id._ ComEd has billed approximately 20,000 customers a year in the last five years under Rider NS. _Id._ During this five-year period, there has not been a single formal complaint filed with the Commission relating to ComEd’s administration of Rider NS. _Id._

##### b. City’s Position

In addition to the overview provided by ComEd, the City notes that, in certain cases, ComEd applies a standard services credit on invoices. As ComEd witness Donovan explains:

> There are instances when ComEd does additional work as part of a customer requested project for the benefit of ComEd’s system. When this occurs, ComEd does not charge the customer for that additional work. Instead, ComEd will provide a credit (called a “standard service credit”), designed to ensure that the customer is not paying anything extra. This Standard Service Credit appears on the design and construction invoices. For example, if a customer requests that we relocate a pole and ComEd already planned upgrades to that pole, ComEd will perform both the upgrade and the relocation at the same time. ComEd will then issue the customer a credit for the upgrade work since it was not needed to complete the customer’s request.

ComEd Ex. 13.0 REV. (Public) at 5.

Mr. Donovan also explains that certain charges are paid upfront, whereas others are recurring monthly charges. He states: “[w]hen a project is nonstandard, project costs must be paid upfront. Once the project is complete and in service, recurring monthly rental costs are applied to facilities and equipment like transformers and switchgears.” _Id._ at 9-10. Mr. Donovan adds that “[o]nce a customer no longer has a need for the equipment, ComEd can cease the monthly rental charge and use the equipment for another customer. That new customer will then incur the monthly rental charges.” _Id._ at 10.

#### 2. Suggested Changes

##### a. City of Chicago and BOMA/Chicago

###### (i) City’s Position

The City demonstrates that it has experienced substantial difficulty in trying to understand the manner in which ComEd implements Rider NS and calculates charges assessed to the City. The City’s efforts to resolve these issues with ComEd have been unsuccessful. In addition, the City believes that the record evidence also shows that other Rider NS customers have experienced the same problems.

The City asserts that the Commission is authorized by the Act to ensure that “[a]ll rules and regulations made by a public utility affecting or pertaining to its charges to the public shall be just and reasonable.” 220 ILCS 5/9-101. When those regulations and rules are found to be unjust and unreasonable, the Commission must take action to correct those regulations and rules. _See_ 220 ILCS 5/9-250. As the City demonstrates throughout, real-world experience with ComEd’s administration of Rider NS has proven that the tariff and/or ComEd’s implementation of the tariff are unjust and unreasonable. When asked reasonable questions about the manner in which it implements Rider NS, the City points out that ComEd has flouted the basic obligation under the Act to ensure that “the application of rates is based on public understandability and acceptance of the rate structure and level.” 220 ILCS 5/1-102(d)(ii); _see also_ 220 ILCS 5/9-101. The City has offered tariff revisions that are crafted to resolve the specific problems identified by Rider NS customers and to ensure, on a prospective basis, that ComEd’s rules and regulations pertaining to its implementation of Rider NS are just and reasonable. The City advises that the Commission should adopt those changes and enshrine in the tariff basic customer rights and accountability provisions.

The City expresses that its proposed revisions to Rider NS are rooted in foundational principles of transparency, fairness, and due process that other administrative bodies have embraced in similar contexts. As one example, the City cites the FERC requirement that public utilities’ formula rate tariffs include minimum protocols to provide transparency, ensure that customers can have a sufficient understanding of the rate being charged, and establish an enforceable process whereby customers have an adequate opportunity to challenge costs that have not been justified. _See, e.g.,_ Midwest Indep. Transmission Sys. Operator, Inc., 139 FERC ¶ 61,127 (2012), order on investigation, 143 FERC ¶ 61,149 (2013) (MISO Investigation Order), order on reh'g, 146 FERC ¶ 61,209, order on compliance, 146 FERC ¶ 61,212 (2014) (“MISO Compliance Order”), order on reh'g, 150 FERC ¶ 61,024, order on compliance, 150 FERC ¶ 61,025 (2015) (“MISO Compliance Order II”). The City avers that these fundamental principles are not unique to FERC proceedings. Rather, they are basic customer protections that should apply across the board to ComEd’s administration of all its tariffs.

The City presents substantial evidence in this proceeding to support a finding that ComEd’s administration of Rider NS is unjust and unreasonable. The City avers that the evidence shows ComEd’s failure to: (1) detail how the line items on each invoice align with the NS COST equation specified in Rider NS, and (2) provide reasonable documentation to support each line item. ComEd states that it uses the NS Cost Equation specified in Rider NS to determine cost estimates. COC Cross Ex. 11. That equation specifies the following components: Direct labor cost, department overhead, paid absence, general and administrative overhead, indirect labor cost, material cost, material handling cost, common indirect cost, company engineering cost, State use tax, federal and State combined statutory income tax rate, related contractor services cost, and present value of the tax write-off. COC Ex. 1.02 at 3-5. However, the City contends that the level of detail ComEd provides on invoices does not align with these components. COC Ex. 1.0 at 13; _see also_ COC Ex. 1.04 (Public) at 2 (excerpt from a ComEd invoice). The City further argues that ComEd fails to sufficiently explain how the line items listed on the invoices (i.e., ComEd Labor + Burden, Supervision & Project Support, Material, Contracting, Other, Rental, and Corporate Overheads) relate to the categories of costs listed in the Nonstandard Cost Single Payment equation set forth in Rider NS.

While ComEd states that it “develops workpapers or other documents to determine its calculation of the single cost payment amount on Sheet Nos. 278-280 and the monthly cost payment amount on Sheet No. 280” (COC Cross Ex. 15 at 1), the City asserts that it has repeatedly refused to provide this information to the City Department of Aviation (“CDA”). The City points out ComEd witness Donovan’s remarkable admission that the Company does not “provide reasonable documentation to support each line item” specified in the NS Cost equation in Rider NS. ComEd Ex. 13.0 REV. (Public) at 3; _see also_ COC Cross Ex. 11 at 1.

The City emphasizes that most troubling is ComEd’s failure or inability to substantiate the category of costs labeled “Corporate Overhead” charges, which can comprise a significant percentage of the total project cost. _See, e.g.,_ COC Ex. 1.04 at 2 (listing $2,029,102.47 in Corporate Overhead charges). CDA has previously asked for invoice documentation and reiterated that request in this case. _See_ COC Ex. 2.06 at 1 (“Please provide all invoices, documents, or other records to support the $2,675,185.43 in “Corporate Overheads.”). Again, the City asserts that ComEd has refused to provide basic documentation which would allow CDA to verify the component costs of its invoices. _See_ COC Ex. 2.06 (refusing to provide the requested information and stating that “any questions regarding specific invoices can be addressed in conversation between the utility and customer.”).

Mr. McHugh’s own analysis of the Corporate Overhead line-item further underscores why substantiation of this line-item is needed. Mr. McHugh analyzed ComEd invoices associated with the O’Hare Modernization Program, provided in COC Ex. 2.05, and came to two troubling conclusions. First, the City demonstrates that the variability of this line item is significant, making it difficult to predict and understand. COC Ex. 2.0 REV. (Public) at 18. Second, the City maintains that the Corporate Overhead charges can exceed fifty percent of the total bill. COC Ex. 2.05 at 1. The City states that ComEd never responded to, much less refuted, this evidence.

The City demonstrates that ComEd’s administration of the Rider NS tariff is unjust and unreasonable. The City believes the tariff revisions it proposes would address these problems on a prospective basis. The City emphasizes that the proposed audit is necessary to review ComEd’s past administration of ComEd’s tariff given the Company’s inability or refusal to demonstrate the propriety of its actions.

The City asserts that ComEd also admits its prior failure to adhere to the provisions of Rider NS. Mr. Donovan explains that in “2021, ComEd initiated an internal review of its billing practices with CDA and determined that its billing practices to CDA were not sufficiently aligned with the tariff.” ComEd Ex. 13.0 REV. (Public) at 8.

The City expresses that these same concerns persist today. The City asserts that ComEd’s decision-making is not sufficiently documented. As Mr. McHugh explained, “[l]ittle to no disclosure is provided by ComEd when they change their project scopes and schedules during construction. Decisions are made in the field with no official notice or approval from CDA, leading to increased ComEd project costs.” COC Ex. 1.0 at 11. The City argues there is no procedure mandating that ComEd provide support or explanation for total invoice costs. Mr. McHugh also noted that “without clear expectations of how the total amount on each design and construction invoice is calculated in the first place, it is difficult to gauge whether any deviations from project scope are reasonable.” COC Ex. 2.0 REV. (Public) at 10; _see also_ _id._ at 9 (“there is no obligation for the Company to monitor costs or to communicate when the costs exceed the initial estimate.”). Further, the City believes that even where the Company has strived to document its processes, such as its proposed ComEd/CDA Change Order Form, “there is no obligation for the Company to complete this form and share it with CDA,” rendering its value “minimal” at best. _Id._ at 10. The City argues that this discussion shows ComEd’s administration of Rider NS is unjust and unreasonable because decisions are not clearly communicated or documented, perpetuating ComEd’s prior failure to adhere to the terms of its own tariff. The City asserts that its recommendations in this proceeding would address these concerns, provide needed clarity and transparency, and ensure the public’s confidence in ComEd’s administration of Rider NS. _See generally_ COC Ex. 1.03 (City’s proposed revisions to Rider NS).

The City states that compounding the deficiencies detailed above are a host of issues CDA has experienced with ComEd. _See_ COC Ex. 2.0 REV. (Public) at 1. Each of these seven issues will be detailed in turn below.

First, the City argues that ComEd requires Rider NS customers to pay design and construction invoices upfront, despite any proposal or quote detailing how those charges were calculated. COC Ex. 1.0 at 10. The design and construction invoices are typically one page with a single dollar amount and minimal description of the cost components that comprise the total dollar amount. For example, as shown in COC Ex. 2.01, the current requested payment for this invoice is $521,695.60. This dollar amount is purportedly associated with “the estimated project cost required to upgrade customer switchgears in all of Terminal 5 Feed from ComEd NC5635,” as well as “[t]emp service needed to allow for the upgrades,” “Labor hours,” “Material,” “engineering,” and “metering for the temp services.” COC Ex. 2.01 at 1. However, as Mr. McHugh explained, there are no estimates of the quantity of materials, the unit price of such materials, the number of labor hours, or the hourly rate for that time. COC Ex. 2.0 REV. (Public) at 7. The City explains that this contravenes customary billing practices, which “specify both a breakdown of the quantity of materials and the price.” _Id._ at 13. Even more troubling, this lack of detail makes it difficult to later ascertain whether changes in project scope are reasonable, and renders ComEd’s administration of Rider NS unjust and unreasonable. The City asserts its proposed revisions to Rider NS would fix these problems by obligating ComEd to provide written information, with reasonable documentation, that customers need to make informed decisions about the nature and scope of their projects. _See, e.g.,_ COC Ex. 1.03 at 5 (requiring ComEd to provide “reasonable supporting documentation” to substantiate the nonstandard cost single payment).

Second, the City cites ComEd’s statement that “the inputs used in computing a new or updated Rider NS rental amount are updated annually.” COC Cross Ex. 1 at 1. Yet, ComEd witness Leick concedes that “Rider NS rentals are not periodically updated and do not always reflect recent O&M charges.” ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 24. The City maintains that for ComEd’s administration of Rider NS to be just and reasonable, it needs to provide transparent information on monthly rentals “upfront as part of the design and construction invoices, with a clear delineation of which materials trigger monthly charges and how those costs can be expected to change over time.” COC Ex. 2.0 REV. (Public) at 8. Again, the City shows that its proposed revisions to Rider NS would resolve these concerns. COC Ex. 1.03 at 6 (requiring ComEd to provide “reasonable supporting documentation” to support the calculation of the nonstandard cost monthly payment).

Third, the City asserts that ComEd’s design and construction invoices specify: “[i]f the final true cost exceeds the initially estimated cost,” the customer is “required to pay the remaining balance.” COC Ex. 2.01 at 1. However, as Mr. McHugh explained, there is “no mechanism to reconcile or ensure that actual costs incurred are consistent with, and do not vastly exceed, the estimated upfront payment.” COC Ex. 1.0 at 10. Mr. McHugh further stated that “[t]his places all of the risk on a customer for a process that is largely controlled by the Company. Furthermore, there is no obligation for the Company to monitor costs or to communicate when the costs exceed the initial estimate. The City makes clear this is an unfair allocation of risk that should be corrected through additional processes set forth in the tariff.” COC Ex. 2.0 REV. (Public) at 9. Based on its experience, the City crafted narrow revisions to Rider NS to ensure these problems do not persist. _See_ COC Ex. 1.03 at 4 (requiring ComEd to continually monitor costs and immediately notify the customer if the costs incurred are greater than 10% of the estimated initial cost); _see id._ (explaining that the customer will not be responsible for costs that are not properly disclosed).

Fourth, the City argues that little to no disclosure is provided by ComEd when it changes project scopes and schedules during construction. Decisions are made in the field with no official notice or approval from CDA, leading to increased ComEd project costs. In many instances, CDA has become aware of these changes only after receiving unexpectedly high invoices from ComEd. COC Ex. 1.0 at 11. The City states that further compounding these concerns is the fact that the customer must bear all of the risk for scope changes and project delays. As one example of a ComEd invoice provides: “Please keep in mind that any changes associated with this material order may generate additional charges to CDA. All sunk costs associated with CDA project cancelations, project delays and/or scope changes will be passed on to CDA. CDA is expected to cover any costs associated with additional storage and handling for the received material beyond August 2025.” _See_ COC Ex. 1.07 at 1. In the City’s view, this unfair allocation of risk renders ComEd’s administration of Rider NS unjust and unreasonable. The City believes that the informational and notice requirements the City proposes to add to Rider NS would resolve these concerns. COC Ex. 1.03 at 4 (requiring ComEd to continually monitor costs and immediately notify the customer if the costs incurred are greater than 10% of the estimated initial cost).

Fifth, the City argues that the delineation between standard and nonstandard facilities appears to be a continually moving target. ComEd states that it “cannot in the abstract categorize specific equipment as standard or nonstandard at airports as it is the load and the nature of the service that determines standard or nonstandard. For example, a given piece of equipment might be standard when serving a certain level of load at a single point of service, but nonstandard when used to serve less load or a part of a nonstandard design.” COC Ex. 2.03 at 4. When CDA has requested clarity on specific types of equipment such as community transformers, the City points out that ComEd has provided conflicting guidance. COC Ex. 2.0 REV. (Public) at 11.

BOMA/Chicago witness Brookover further validates the City’s concerns: “the definitions of standard services and nonstandard services (as reflected in ComEd’s General Terms and Conditions Sheets No. 155 and 156 and in Rider NS) are unclear and appear to provide ComEd with broad discretion over what constitutes “larger, more, or different” facilities or services while providing customers with little information regarding ComEd’s basis for determining whether a service or facility is “larger, more or different.” BOMA/Chicago Ex. 1.0 at 4. The City states that without further documentation of ComEd’s decision making on this issue, the Commission cannot reasonably conclude that ComEd’s administration of Rider NS is just and reasonable. The City believes its proposed tariff revisions and audit are designed to remedy this untenable situation and provide needed clarity and transparency. _See_ COC Ex. 1.03 at 3 (requiring a written Preliminary Assessment to “detail with specificity all proposed new services and facilities and explain why the nonstandard services and facilities qualify as larger, more, or different services and/or facilities”).

Sixth, the City states that when CDA asked ComEd to explain “how the Company determines the amount of the standard service credit,” ComEd stated that “amount of the standard service credit is proportional to the cost of standard portions of the project.” COC Ex. 2.07 at 1. But all that is provided on the invoice is one line item with a percentage figure. _See_ COC Ex. 1.04 (Public) at 2.

The City believes that without an explanation of how that percentage was calculated and which particular standard facilities were identified as part of that calculation, ComEd’s practice is unjust and unreasonable. COC Ex. 2.0 REV. (Public) at 11. The City argues that its proposed tariff revisions would resolve this problem. _See_ COC Ex. 1.03 at 3 (requiring a written Preliminary Assessment to “detail with specificity all proposed new services and facilities and explain why the nonstandard services and facilities qualify as larger, more, or different services and/or facilities”).

Seventh, the City explains that there are at least eight other entities with infrastructure at the City’s airports. COC Ex. 2.0 REV. (Public) at 19. CDA has repeatedly requested the Company to provide Computer-Aided Design (“CAD”) or Geographic Information System (“GIS”) files with the appropriate georeferenced detail, which would allow CDA to visually “layer” ComEd facilities on top of other layers of infrastructure at the airports. Mr. McHugh explained that this “layering” feature is important because it would allow CDA to better keep track of existing facilities owned by all entities and conduct facility locates in a safe and efficient manner. _Id._ at 19. While ComEd has provided a PDF file of the Midway Airport property (see ComEd Ex. 8.04 (Confidential)), the City argues that format does not enable CDA to perform the “layering” of assets described above, diminishing its usefulness. CDA has repeatedly asked for CAD/GIS files for the O’Hare Airport but this information has not yet been provided. _See_ COC Ex. 2.03 at 4 (“The O’Hare map is still in progress as there have been significant new infrastructure additions. ComEd will supplement this response as soon as the current CAD file for O’Hare Airport is available.”). The City asserts that ComEd never supplemented its response to this data request.

The information CDA is seeking is foundational to its ability to safely and efficiently operate its airports. ComEd’s perpetual refusal to provide this basic information demonstrates that its administration of Rider NS is unjust and unreasonable. The City believes its proposed revisions to Rider NS would provide a formal process whereby customers could request (and ComEd would be required to provide) this type of information. COC Ex. 1.03 at 6 (Miscellaneous General Provision).

The City argues that ComEd’s failure or refusal to provide reasonable information that would enable the City to verify the accuracy of Rider NS costs constrains the City’s ability to develop coherent budgets and conduct business with its partners. COC Ex. 2.0 REV. (Public) at 9. The City stresses that this frustrating situation has persisted for years. Mr. McHugh explained that pursuant to CDA’s Airline Use and Lease Agreement with its airline tenants, capital projects are funded by airport tenants and CDA has an obligation to carefully maintain budgets for all its capital projects and disclose changes to these budgets to its airline tenants. COC Ex. 1.0 at 15. The City underscores that the unpredictability of costs incurred under Rider NS threatens to compromise CDA’s obligations under its Airline Use and Lease Agreement and strain the important business relationships it has with its airline tenants. _Id._ at 15.

Moreover, the City advises that CDA is subject to the Federal Aviation Administration’s grant assurance prohibition on revenue diversion. This means that airport revenues cannot be used to fund non-airport purposes. Thus, the City explains that any shared facilities located at O’Hare must properly correspond only to CDA usage and not to usage by other customers. The City maintains that it needs sufficient detail from ComEd to make these determinations. _Id._ at 15-16. These challenges can be rectified by adopting the City’s proposed set of solutions, described in further detail below.

The City argues that ComEd’s attempts to paint Rider NS issues as a City-specific concern are untethered from the record evidence. ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 66 (“These recommendations are not necessary and would create substantial administrative burden from reporting when only the COC is expressing a concern.”). The City cites the concerns raised by BOMA/Chicago, CTA, and Metra. BOMA/Chicago witness Brookover states, “BOMA/Chicago members have experienced similar issues and frustrations with Rider NS as those described by City of Chicago witness Joseph McHugh.” BOMA/Chicago IB at 6. CTA/Metra witness Mr. Bachman explains that “CTA and Metra believe the Commission should revisit and order ComEd to provide, as the City notes, more accountability, transparency, and notice concerning Rider NS costs and implement the language changes I proposed in my direct testimony.” CTA/Metra Ex. 2.0 at 3; _see also_ CTA IB at 20 (“This lack of transparency and the significant costs Rider NS imposes have caused the CTA to request modifications of Rider NS language not only for the RR Class but also for the transitioning of the CTA’s diesel bus fleet to electric, which requires installation of charging facilities.”); Metra IB at 31 (“It is evident that Rider NS is a point of contention for multiple parties, including not only the RR Class, but also the City of Chicago and the BOMA/Chicago, each of which has advanced proposals regarding Rider NS in this proceeding.”). Thus, the City emphasizes, the issues plaguing Rider NS are pervasive and are much larger than a single customer complaint.

The City states that even more broadly, the use of Rider NS to recover electric vehicle make-ready infrastructure demonstrates that increasing numbers of costs will flow through this tariff provision, particularly as the City achieves the electrification objectives in its Climate Action Plan. COC Ex. 1.0 at 17. In the BE Plan 1 Order, the Commission approved ComEd’s proposal to use Rider NS for cost recovery of make-ready facilities. BE Plan 1 Order at 249. As Mr. McHugh explains, “[p]ursuant to this ruling, ComEd and the retail customer would need to determine the infrastructure facilities needed for electric charging and the costs of this make-ready infrastructure would be recovered through individual customer-specific charges under Rider NS.” COC Ex. 1.0 at 17. The City argues that Rider NS will need to be durable enough to accommodate this increasing volume of upgrades. This further underscores the need for Commission review and scrutiny of Rider NS. _Id._ at 17.

The City argues it presents substantial evidence to support a finding that ComEd’s administration of Rider NS is unjust and unreasonable. The City also argues it presents substantial evidence to support a finding that its proposed solutions to these problems are just and reasonable. Consequently, the City requests that the Commission direct ComEd to: (1) revise Rider NS to include basic customer protections that improve transparency and clarity regarding the costs for providing nonstandard service and the equipment needed to provide that service, as well as establish a formal process whereby customers can submit questions and information requests to ComEd and receive timely responses; (2) engage a third-party auditor to verify the accuracy of the costs ComEd has recovered for Rider NS service in light of ComEd’s inability to substantiate its calculations; and (3) provide an annual report to improve transparency and clarity regarding costs, nonstandard versus standard equipment, service credits, and monthly rental charges. The City details each of these requests below.

The City states that its proposed tariff revisions are set forth in redline in COC Ex. 1.03 and would bring clarity and transparency to Rider NS. The City first recommends that ComEd be required to conduct a Preliminary Assessment before ComEd enters into an agreement with the retail customer, if the total estimated cost of the nonstandard facilities and services exceeds $100,000. _Id._ at 18. The City explains that its Preliminary Assessment would: (1) detail with specificity all proposed new services and facilities and explain why the nonstandard services and facilities qualify as larger, more, or different services and/or facilities; (2) advise the retail customer of the feasibility of alternative solutions to achieving the intended project implementation compatible with ComEd infrastructure, including potential retail customer purchase, installation; and ownership of equipment; (3) explain whether a portion of such nonstandard services and facilities may reasonably be used for other retail customers, and if so, identify such retail customer(s) as well as the estimated total cost and/or monthly payment payable by such other retail customer(s); and (4) provide an estimated, itemized cost breakdown of the cost of furnishing nonstandard services and facilities (i.e., the “NS COST”, which is the amount that must be paid to the Company for the installation or provision of nonstandard services and facilities prior to such installation or provision) consistent with the categories specified in the NS COST equation, including reasonable supporting documentation for each category; and (5) specify whether any costs will be recovered under any other Company riders, an explanation of why those riders are implicated, and the estimated costs to be recovered under the other riders. _Id._ at 18-19.

In addition to the Preliminary Assessment, the proposed tariff revisions include: a process whereby the retail customer is given the choice of whether to proceed with the installation of the nonstandard facilities and services, once an explanation of the estimated costs has been provided; a requirement that the agreement between the retail customer and ComEd must be in writing; an obligation to (1) continually monitor costs and timely communicate to the retail customer if the costs incurred are 10% greater than the initial estimate provided to the retail customer and (2) provide a reconciliation of the estimated cost of the nonstandard services and facilities with the actual costs incurred; a requirement to provide support for all components of the NS COST equation and Nonstandard Cost Monthly Payment in writing prior to payment in reasonable detail with reasonable supporting documentation; and a requirement that the Company provide further information regarding nonstandard services, including a deadline to respond to such requests within 14 business days. _Id._ at 19.

Based on ComEd’s inability or failure to respond to reasonable questions about the propriety of its administration of Rider NS, the City recommends that the Commission direct ComEd to hire an independent auditor to provide oversight of costs recovered under Rider NS. _Id._ at 17. The City maintains that the auditor’s review should culminate in a report to be filed with the Commission, allowing customers impacted by Rider NS to meaningfully respond. _Id._ at 17. The City argues this evaluation is needed so that customers such as CDA, who pay for both standard and nonstandard service, pay rates that are just and reasonable. _Id._ at 17-18. The magnitude of the costs recovered under Rider NS further underscores the importance of this recommendation. _See_ COC Cross Ex. 10 at 2 (showing $424,290,000 in total jurisdictional distribution customer advances for construction in 2023); _see also_ ComEd Ex. 9.0 REV. at 9 (“[i]n the last five years, the collection of these customer advances has avoided at least $500 million of incremental costs that would have been borne by all customers if they had not been collected from these cost-causers.”); _see also_ COC Ex. 1.0 at 11 (noting that the total cost of design and construction invoices CDA has paid to ComEd over the last ten years equals approximately $84 million).

The City has satisfied the standard for the Commission to initiate an audit. Pursuant to Section 8-102, an audit and tariff investigation is commenced when the Commission:

> [H]as reasonable grounds to believe that the audit or investigation is necessary to assure that the utility is providing adequate, efficient, reliable, safe, and least-cost service and charging only just and reasonable rates therefor, or that the audit or investigation is likely to be cost-beneficial in enhancing the quality of service or the reasonableness of rates therefor.

220 ILCS 5/8-102. Here, the City has offered substantial record evidence demonstrating the lack of transparency, notice, and detail that render ComEd’s administration of Rider NS unjust and unreasonable. Hall, 453 U.S. 571, 577 (1981) (explaining that the filed rate doctrine prohibits public utilities from “charg[ing] rates for its services other than those properly filed with the appropriate federal regulatory authority”). As the City explains, the filed rate doctrine provides that once a tariff becomes effective, a customer has no rights to receive service on rates, terms, or conditions different from those in the effective tariff.

As the Commission has already determined, “revisions to a utility’s tariffs cannot be considered in a consumer complaint proceeding, as more than just [the complainant] would be impacted by any change.” Berberick v. MYRP Docket, Order at 500 (Dec. 14, 2023); _see also_ MYRP Docket, Proposed Order at 332 (Oct. 18, 2024) (ALJ Proposed Order confirming the same). For these reasons, the City respectfully asks the Commission to adopt its requests for relief in this case.

BOMA/Chicago expresses support for Rider NS revisions recommended by the City and BOMA/Chicago to ensure a more fair and transparent process for customers incurring Rider NS charges. ComEd will also walk the CDA through Design and Construction invoices and answer any questions from the CDA about charges and credits. _Id._ ComEd communicates that those charges include design and engineering costs, specifically: (1) costs for materials; (2) labor costs; (3) costs for any planned civil work; and (4) other projected costs (including all corporate overhead costs). _Id._ at 3. ComEd also communicates with the City (and all Rider NS customers) that these incremental costs for nonstandard CIAC include direct and indirect costs, such as labor, supervision, and project support costs. _Id._ at 3.

ComEd asserts that some of the other revisions proposed by the City to Rider NS are not appropriate or feasible. For example, the City proposed to revise Rider NS to require ComEd to: “[a]dvise the retail customer of the feasibility of alternative solutions to achieving intended project implementation compatible with ComEd infrastructure, including potential retail customer purchase, installation, and ownership of equipment....” COC Ex. 1.03. It is ComEd’s position that this proposal would require ComEd to research how a customer could provide their own equipment in lieu of taking the nonstandard facilities from ComEd. _See_ ComEd Ex 10.0 at 9. ComEd suggests that this proposal should be rejected as inappropriate and unnecessary. _Id._ ComEd asserts that it does not, and cannot, know the status of every Rider NS customer’s equipment inventory. _Id._ at 9. That is the customer’s, not ComEd’s, responsibility. _Id._ In addition, ComEd’s other customers should not be forced to pay for that comparison shopping in their rates. _Id._

The City also proposed to revise Rider NS to require ComEd to “[s]pecify whether any costs will be recovered under any other Company riders, an explanation of why those riders are implicated, and the estimated costs to be recovered under the other riders.” COC Ex. 1.03. It is ComEd’s position that this proposal is both too vague and too broad. It is ComEd’s position that it is not practical to require ComEd to explain to every Rider NS customer how every Rider NS payment or rental impacts ComEd’s plant and rate determination. ComEd also asserts that it would also significantly increase the administrative difficulty and cost of providing service to Rider NS customers. ComEd Ex. 10.0 at 9.

ComEd asserts that there is no practical or legal basis for the City’s request to add language to Rider NS. The City proposed to revise Rider NS by adding: “Nonstandard services and facilities are, and at all times remain, the sole property of the Company unless otherwise agreed to by the Company and retail customer.” COC Ex. 1.03 (proposed revision emphasized). The proposed revision implies that ComEd would be required to consider selling its assets. ComEd Ex. 10.0 at 9.

It is ComEd’s position that the City’s proposed annual Rider NS Report is unnecessary and should be rejected. ComEd asserts that it already provides much of the information that City witness McHugh suggested should be provided in an annual report to the City on a regular basis, such as in the weekly meetings between CDA and ComEd. _See_ COC Ex. 2.0 REV. at 15-16; ComEd Ex. 13.0 REV. at 16. The City also requested information in the annual report that ComEd does not maintain in the ordinary course of its business or would not necessarily be updated on any regular basis. ComEd Ex. 13.0 REV. at 16. For example, witness McHugh requested that the report contain “the most recent engineering review,” but engineering reviews are typically only done for new customers. COC Ex. 2.0 REV. at 15; ComEd Ex. 13.0 REV. at 16-17. The only other time engineering reviews are conducted is when the customer exceeds their existing standard, and a new engineering review is required. _Id._

It is ComEd’s position that the Commission should reject the City’s proposal for an audit and tariff investigation of Rider NS. It is ComEd’s position that there is no evidence in this case that warrants such a step and that audits and tariff investigations should not be used as tools to address discrete customer questions or concerns. _See_ ComEd Ex. 9.0 REV. at 9-10. ComEd asserts that any concerns regarding disclosure and information presentation, including at intermediate steps in a construction project, are better addressed in other processes, such as workshops (described below), conversations between ComEd and the CDA (or any other customers), and, if the CDA or other customer remains unsatisfied, a customer complaint, if appropriate.

The Commission should also reject BOMA/Chicago’s proposals to modify Rider NS. BOMA/Chicago’s recommendations are unnecessary, vague, and potentially problematic to other customers. Specifically, BOMA/Chicago witness Brookover requested a written agreement, a preliminary assessment, notification of cost increases in excess of 10%, and a reconciliation between estimated and actual budgets. BOMA/Chicago Ex. 1.0 at 4-8.

ComEd argues that its policy is already to implement Rider NS as suggested by BOMA/Chicago, so no additional changes are necessary. ComEd Ex. 10.0 at 11. ComEd provides the type of Rider NS documentation and customer-specific communication that BOMA/Chicago requested. _Id._ at 11. ComEd provides the customer the invoice detailing the charges and requires payment before work can proceed. Rider NS, ILL. C. C. No. 10, 3rd Revised Sheet No. 278. The invoice, plus ComEd’s tariffs and ComEd’s invoicing procedure (see COC Cross Ex. 16.0) constitute the written agreement. It is ComEd’s position that to the extent a customer would like additional information regarding various aspects of their Rider NS projects, ComEd makes efforts to provide the level of detail the customer needs to make an informed decision on how to proceed. _See_ ComEd Ex. 10.0 at 11. Because BOMA/Chicago’s Rider NS modifications are duplicative of ComEd’s current Rider NS implementation and practices, the Commission should reject them. On exceptions, ComEd offered as an alternative to an investigation to file an amendment to Rider NS within 60 days of the final Order in this proceeding.

Staff explains that upon filing, the Commission can decide whether the tariff should go into effect as revised, or to suspend the tariff and open an investigation.

###### (v) Commission Analysis and Conclusion

ComEd argues Rider NS is out of scope; however, the Commission previously directed that revenue-neutral modifications be considered in this proceeding. The Commission’s December 14, 2023, final Order and December 19, 2024, final Order on Refiling in Docket Nos. 22-0486/23-0055/24-0181 (Cons.) instructed parties to address Rider NS in this docket. The City and BOMA/Chicago have raised sufficient concerns to warrant Commission direction regarding Rider NS. The Commission agrees with the City and BOMA/Chicago that workshops are not the appropriate forum to address their concerns with Rider NS due to the nature of the issues and lack of resolution amongst the parties to date. The City offered tariff provisions in this docket to address its identified concerns. _See_ COC Ex. 1.03. Accordingly, the Commission finds certain revisions will enhance the clarity and transparency of Rider NS and adopts the City’s recommendations in part.

The Commission acknowledges that the City is but one of ComEd’s Rider NS customers. However, the record in this case demonstrates that the City is not the only customer to experience similar issues with Rider NS as described by the City. _See_ BOMA/Chicago Ex. 1.0 at 4. While transparent and accurate billing is important to any ratepayer, it is particularly important when that ratepayer is subject to additional rules and regulations that may require informational granularity the Company does not provide. For example, the CDA is subject to the Federal Aviation Administration’s grant assurance prohibition on revenue diversion and needs certain information to comply with the grant requirements. The Commission emphasizes that it is critical for CDA’s grant compliance to verify and ensure proper billing so that taxpayers are not being charged for private entities’ expenses.

The Company is directed to revise its Rider NS tariff to establish a Preliminary Assessment before it enters into an agreement with the retail customer if the total estimated cost of the nonstandard facilities and services exceeds $100,000. The Preliminary Assessment shall: (1) detail with specificity all proposed new services and facilities and explain why the nonstandard services and facilities qualify as larger, more, or different services and/or facilities; (2) explain whether a portion of such nonstandard services and facilities may reasonably be used for other retail customers, and if so, identify such retail customer(s) as well as the estimated total cost and/or monthly payment payable by such other retail customer(s); and (3) provide an estimated, itemized breakdown of the cost of furnishing nonstandard services and facilities (i.e., the “NS COST”) consistent with the categories specified in the NS COST equation, including supporting documentation for each category.

Further, the Commission adopts BOMA/Chicago’s recommendations that this Preliminary Assessment: (1) specify what infrastructure/equipment ComEd is classifying as a standard service for a project, including the costs associated with what ComEd is classifying as standard service; (2) disclose to customers the NS COST in at least as much detail as outlined under the “Determination of Nonstandard Cost Single Payment” section of Rider NS; and (3) provide the numbers or assumption the Company is using to each variable of the NS COST equation and the basis for each of those numbers and assumptions. _See_ BOMA/Chicago Ex. 1.0 at 6.

In addition to the Preliminary Assessment for projects with estimates exceeding $100,000, the Commission directs the Company to incorporate the following components of the City’s proposed tariff revisions in its updated tariff filing.

- **(1)** A process whereby the retail customer is given the choice of whether to proceed with the installation of the nonstandard facilities and services, once an explanation of the estimated costs has been provided.
- **(2)** A requirement that the agreement between the retail customer and ComEd must be in writing.
- **(3)** Obligations for the Company to: (a) continually monitor costs and communicate to the retail customer within 5 business days if the costs incurred are 10% greater than the initial estimate provided to the retail customer and (b) provide a review of the estimated cost of the nonstandard services and facilities with the actual costs incurred.
- **(4)** A requirement to provide support for all components of the NS COST equation and Nonstandard Cost Monthly Payment in writing prior to payment in detail with supporting documentation.
- **(5)** A requirement that the Company provide further information regarding nonstandard services, including a deadline to respond to such requests within 14 business days.

The Commission finds that the record evidence sufficiently justifies an audit. 220 ILCS 5/8-102. The record demonstrates the City repeatedly asked for itemized billing with backup documentation to verify invoice costs but has not received it. _See_ COC Ex. 2.0 REV (Public) at 18; COC Ex. 2.06 at 1. Obtaining additional information is particularly important to understanding ComEd’s Corporate Overhead charges, which vary significantly and can be as high as fifty percent of the total invoice. COC Ex. 2.05. The Commission agrees with the City that this information is needed so that customers that pay both standard and nonstandard rates, like CDA, pay just and reasonable rates. The magnitude of the costs recovered under Rider NS further underscores the importance of the audit. _See_ ComEd Ex. 9.0 REV at 9 (“[i]n the last five years, the collection of these customer advances has avoided at least $500 million of incremental costs that would have been borne by all customers if they had not been collected from these cost-causers”).

Accordingly, the Commission orders Staff to procure an independent auditor to review ComEd’s prior five years of Rider NS administration to begin within 30 days of the procurement of the auditor by Staff. The auditor shall file a report with its findings, as a compliance filing in this docket, within six months of the audit’s initiation. Pursuant to Section 8-102 of the Act, the cost of an independent audit shall be borne initially by the utility but shall be recovered as an expense through normal ratemaking procedures. _See_ 220 ILCS 5/8-102. The scope of this audit shall include, but not be limited to: (1) how line items on prior ComEd invoices align with the NS COST equation specified in Rider NS; (2) whether ComEd has provided reasonable documentation to support each line item; (3) an analysis of whether ComEd failed to adhere to its tariff; and (4) whether the standard service credit is reasonably explained on invoices and how that credit changes over time.

Finally, the Commission grants the City’s request for an annual report to address the identified transparency concerns. ComEd shall provide to the City, and any Rider NS customer that requests, an annual report on Rider NS, including, but not limited to, the following information: (1) the most recent engineering review of the customer’s actual peak usage, including the customer’s electric power and energy requirements calculated pursuant to ComEd’s General Terms and Conditions Sheet No. 155 and, if applicable, an explanation of whether the customer’s tenants’ usage is included in the calculations; (2) the customer’s currently effective Electric Facilities Service Acknowledgement, including a description of where all existing ComEd cabling, equipment, and other infrastructure is located on customer property, supplemented with information about its physical size, whether it is standard or nonstandard, whether it incurs monthly rental charges, and whether it is shared with other entities at the customer’s facilities (e.g., transformers serving both CDA and airline tenants); and (3) the total annual costs ComEd has charged the customer via Rider NS and an explanation of whether, and if so why, a standard service credit was applied to each invoice. The Commission directs ComEd to provide this information annually to the City and any other requesting Rider NS customer by March 1st of each year.

##### b. RR Delivery Class

###### (i) CTA’s Position

Rider NS is used to calculate ComEd charges to customers for what it terms “nonstandard services and facilities.” In short, the CTA explains, this rider applies to all new construction or other work performed by ComEd to provide service. It means that any additional service or changes to the service to the traction power substations means the CTA must pay under Rider NS based upon ComEd’s own calculations. CTA/Metra Ex. 1.0 at 16. In addition, any changes that the CTA makes to its bus garages and bus maintenance facilities to accommodate charging facilities for the new electric bus fleet are subject to ComEd’s Rider NS. As a result, Rider NS makes it more costly to convert traction power substations to eliminate power flow-through and to convert bus garages to charging facilities for electric buses. The CTA asserts Rider NS as it currently exists is contrary to the Commission’s earlier findings concerning the importance of mass transit and its benefits to the area’s economy and environment, and Rider NS is contrary to the intent of the EVA.

The CTA requests language changes in Rider NS in part to assist the CTA in meeting its goal to electrify its more than 1,800 diesel buses. To support the transition to electric buses, the CTA must upgrade and modernize its seven existing garages and its heavy maintenance facility for bus charging and electric bus maintenance. CTA Ex. 1.00 at 8. The modifications at the garages will require up to 10 to 20 MW of capacity to support charging equipment. The total cost for the electric system changes on the CTA side of the meter is estimated to be $20 million to $30 million for each garage. CTA Ex. 2.00 at 11. In addition to the CTA costs, under Rider NS, ComEd will impose an additional charge for upgrades on its side of the meter in the range of $3 million to $7 million per garage. CTA Ex. 2.00 at 11. The ComEd charges should be considered as “make-ready infrastructure” that the utility should pay as required by the EVA.

In enacting the EVA, the General Assembly found that the adoption and use of electric vehicles would benefit the State of Illinois by improving the health and environmental quality of the residents of the State. 20 ILCS 627/5. The General Assembly desired to promote “beneficial electrification” to decrease reliance on fossil fuels and reduce pollution from the transportation sector. 20 ILCS 627/45(a). To accomplish this goal, the General Assembly required electric utilities to prepare “beneficial electrification programs” to include incentives for electrification for all consumers and incentives and infrastructure targeted to vehicles used by transit agencies. 20 ILCS 627/45(a)(10)(5-6). “Make-ready infrastructure” under the EVA is the electrical and construction work necessary from the distribution circuit to the connection point of charging equipment. 20 ILCS 627/45. The EVA further provides that a utility should include as part of its beneficial electrification programs “incentives for electrification and associated infrastructure targeted to medium-duty and heavy-duty vehicles used by transit agencies.” 20 ILCS 627/45. Modification of Rider NS as requested by the CTA would meet this requirement. Because the EVA specifically called-out transit agencies to receive benefits under the EVA, it is reasonable that the Commission promote and not inhibit transit agencies in their electrification transition. Thus, the CTA argues, modifying the language to Rider NS meets the goals and requirements under the EVA as specifically called out by the EVA.

The CTA asserts that the Staff erroneously claims that the CTA is “collaterally estopped” from requesting language changes in Rider NS relating to the definition of “nonstandard services and facilities” that the CTA believes is required to fulfill the utility’s obligations under the EVA. Staff Brief at 25. Staff’s argument is contrary to the law.

In Commonwealth Edison Co. v. Ill. Com. Comm'n, 405 Ill. App. 3d 389, 407-08, 937 N.E.2d 685, 705 (2d Dist. 2010), the court said:

> Illinois courts have consistently held that “decisions of the Commission are not res judicata.” _See, e.g.,_ A. Finkl & Sons Co. v. Illinois Commerce Comm'n, 250 Ill.App.3d 317, 323, 189 Ill.Dec. 824, 620 N.E.2d 1141 (1993). The concept of public regulation requires that the Commission have power to deal freely with each situation that comes before it, regardless of how it may have dealt with a similar or even the same situation in a previous proceeding. Mississippi River Fuel Corp. v. Illinois Commerce Comm'n, 1 Ill.2d 509, 513, 116 N.E.2d 394 (1953). A record containing new evidence or argument that implicates past decisions compels reconsideration on the new record and may require a different result. _See_ 220 ILCS 5/10–103 (West 2006) (“any finding, decision or order made by the Commission shall be based exclusively on the record for decision in the case”). Thus, we agree with IIEC that the decisions the Commission cited as precedent did not compel the Commission to disregard the increase in accumulated depreciation of the embedded plant during the post-test-year period.

405 Ill. App. 3d at 407-08. Staff’s claim that the CTA is estopped from requesting changes to Rider NS is not supported by the law, and that claim has been rejected by the Commission.

Rather than modifying Rider NS to exempt mass transit agencies from certain infrastructure costs charged by ComEd, Staff wants the CTA to “pass infrastructure costs onto their riders.” Staff IB at 25. Staff’s proposal to pass on the costs to mass transit riders contradicts the Commission’s long-standing policy expressed in ComEd cases that rates for mass transit should not unduly burden their riders. Staff’s proposal that mass transit agencies increase their fares in lieu of the Commission modifying Rider NS is rejected.

In Docket No. 05-0597, the Commission stated it “is very concerned that any changes to the provisions of service providers of mass transit will not unduly burden the millions of passengers who depend on public transportation.” Final Order at 189. In Docket No.09-0263, the Commission rejected ComEd’s attempt to pass costs onto the RR Class because it ran “counter to this Commission’s policy of encouraging the use of public transportation for environmental reasons.” Commonwealth Edison Co., Docket No. 05-0597, Order at 43 (Jul. 26, 2006). In the 2017 RDI Order, the Commission again found that the use of mass transit “reduces the level of total energy consumption and provides environmental benefits... The Commission has repeatedly recognized the benefits that the RR Class provides to ComEd’s service area and its customers and affirmed the public policy that, in setting the rates for the RR Class, these societal benefits must be considered.”

The CTA recommends the Commission finds Staff’s recommendation that the CTA simply increase its rates to riders ignores these cases, ignores the benefits of mass transit, and is rejected and instead adopts the language modifications to Rider NS as provided in CTA/Metra Joint Ex. 1.03.

The CTA voices opposition in its RBOE to Staff’s suggested bar on its participation in any Rider NS investigation.

###### (ii) Metra’s Position

Metra requests that the Commission direct ComEd to revise Rider NS to state that service related to Metra’s planned battery-powered train sets shall not be subject to any non-standard service charges or fees under Rider NS, and that service related to Metra’s battery powered train sets will be considered standard services. CTA/Metra Joint Ex. 1.0 at 16-17; CTA/Metra Joint Ex. 1.03. In addition, Metra proposes an additional edit that would require ComEd to reserve, without charge to Metra, distribution system capacity to provide for increased load that may be needed for battery-powered train sets. CTA/Metra Joint Ex. 1.03.

Metra argues that Rider NS as currently drafted is contrary to the EVA, 20 ILCS 627/45. CTA/Metra Joint Ex. 1.0 at 16-17. The EVA makes clear that “[i]t is the intent of the General Assembly to decrease reliance on fossil fuels, [and] reduce pollution from the transportation sector…” 220 ILCS 627/45(a). One of the methods by which the General Assembly plans to reduce pollution from the transportation sector is to encourage “[e]xpanded infrastructure investment” that “will help Illinois more rapidly decarbonize the transportation sector.” 220 ILCS 627/45(a)(5).

Metra contends that it is at the forefront of a decarbonized transportation sector in Illinois. Ms. Ciavarella’s testimony demonstrates the positive environmental impact Metra has in ComEd’s service territory, especially EIEC communities. Metra’s commuter rail lines remove single occupancy vehicles from the road, thereby reducing carbon emissions from the primary contributor to carbon emissions in Illinois. Metra Ex. 2.0 REV. at 4-5. Ms. Ciavarella also demonstrates that the MED serves several EIECs, which are disproportionately vulnerable to the effects of air pollution. _Id._ at 3-4. Metra notes that both ComEd and Staff agree that Metra’s train lines facilitate environmental benefits, reduce traffic congestion and serve EIECs. Metra Cross Exs. 4.0; 5.0; 7.0; 8.0. Metra observes that the Commission has repeatedly made similar findings both in previous ComEd rate increase and rate design cases, as well as in other cases. CTA/Metra Joint Ex. 1.0 at 14-16. Metra’s requested revisions to Rider NS are entirely in keeping with those findings and would expand positive environmental impacts by facilitating Metra’s adoption and use of battery powered trains. Additional battery powered trains will allow Metra to retire old, non-electric locomotives that do not confer as great an environmental benefit as its fully electrified counterparts do.

Accordingly, Metra recommends that the Commission direct ComEd to make the suggested revisions to Rider NS. In the alternative, Metra recommends that the Commission open a separate investigation into Rider NS. CTA/Metra Joint Ex. 2.0. It is evident that Rider NS is a point of contention for multiple parties, including not only the RR Class, but also the City of Chicago and the Building Owners and Managers Association of Chicago (“BOMA/Chicago”), each of which has advanced proposals regarding Rider NS in this proceeding. _See_ ComEd Ex. 10.0 at 1.

ComEd argues that the Commission should reject Metra’s proposed Rider NS revisions. ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 68. First, ComEd argues that the issue was already decided by the Commission in the 2023 BE Plan proceeding. _Id._ However, Metra notes that the language proposed in this proceeding includes the addition of references to Metra’s charging infrastructure, so it is distinct from the language proposed by the CTA in the 2023 BE Plan Proceeding. _See_ Commonwealth Edison Co., Docket Nos. 22-0432/22-0442 (Consol.), CTA and City of Chicago’s Initial Hearing Brief at 27-28 (Jan. 4, 2023). Moreover, in the BE Plan 1 Order, the Commission rejected CTA’s revisions to Rider NS based on “CTA/City’s interpretation of make-ready infrastructure,” which was a subject specific to the EVA. Commonwealth Edison Co., BE Plan 1 Order at 250. Metra notes that in this proceeding the Commission is evaluating revenue-neutral tariff changes.

Metra notes that Staff takes this argument a step further, claiming that the RR Class is “collaterally estopped from raising [the Rider NS revisions] again.” Staff IB at 25. Staff cites Ill. Com. Comm’n v. Ill. Power Co., which stated “[t]he proper means by which to correct perceived errors in Commission orders is through the rehearing and appeal processes. Such corrections should not be sought in other dockets.” Staff IB at 25. To confirm, Metra is not trying to “correct perceived errors” in the BE Plan 1 docket; rather, it is requesting that the Commission direct ComEd to make revenue-neutral tariff changes to Rider NS in the current proceeding—the sole purpose of this proceeding. Moreover, Metra points out that Staff witness Hamidu expressly agreed with the Commission that CTA/Metra should present its arguments in the current proceeding. MYRP Docket, Staff Ex. 21.0 RGP at 9.

Second, ComEd argues that the Rider NS revisions are not revenue neutral because by reclassifying certain nonstandard services as standard services, the cost is collected through ComEd’s base rates rather than Rider NS. ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 68. ComEd argues that the RR Class’s costs would then be socialized to other delivery classes. _Id._ at 69. Staff shares ComEd’s concerns that the RR Class’s revisions to Rider NS would cause costs to be shifted to all other delivery classes. Staff Ex. 7.0 at 21-22. Metra contends that these arguments overstate the cost causation concern. While the Commission does indeed generally adopt a cost causation approach, the Commission also does not strictly adhere to that approach, depending on public policy considerations. _See_ Docket No. 05-0597, Order at 189, 190 (“Accordingly, the Commission finds that minimizing the change to existing contractual terms as necessitated by the post-2006 market changes, as well as avoiding rate shock to the railroad customers, is in the public’s best interest.”). The Commission has repeatedly found that public policy considerations such as electrification, environmental protection, public health issues, and transportation/congestion concerns dictate somewhat modified treatment of the RR Class in terms of rate design. Metra IB at 5-8. Metra’s suggested modifications to Rider NS are justified precisely because of these considerations—facilitating deployment of Metra electric trains will align with all of those public policy considerations. The RR Class’s public transportation is a critical tool to decarbonizing the transportation sector, and ComEd could play an essential role in further incentivizing environmentally positive actions.

Finally, Staff argues that CTA/Metra should pass the costs of electrification infrastructure onto its customers because it is more equitable. Staff IB at 25. Metra states that this position entirely ignores that the Commission has previously recognized the unique public policy considerations associated with passing on additional costs for ComEd service onto public transportation passengers:

> [T]he fact that the CTA and Metra are providers of mass public transportation raises an additional public interest concern. ComEd’s proposal fails to account for the potential impact of increased utility rates for entities providing public transportation on the citizens of Illinois. The Commission is very concerned that any changes to the provisions of service providers of mass transit will not unduly burden the millions of passengers who depend on public transportation. This Commission also believes that it must consider the public policy implications of establishing delivery rates that encourage energy conservation and encourage electric usage during off-peak periods. While the Commission is not prepared to disregard cost of service, the Commission believes that important public policy considerations cannot be ignored.

Docket No. 05-0597, Order at 189. Metra asserts that if the RR Class is forced to pass on additional costs to its passengers, that could impact fares and ridership. The record establishes that Metra’s ridership is expected to return to only 60% of pre-pandemic levels by the end of 2025. Metra Ex. 2.0 at 8. Increased economic pressures on Metra will only exacerbate concerns, undermining the highly positive public policy attributes of increased public transportation that the Commission has previously noted.

Accordingly, Metra urges the Commission to direct ComEd to adopt the RR Class’s revisions to Rider NS.

In its RBOE, Metra notes that while its preference is for the Commission to address changes to Rider NS in this proceeding, it does not object to ComEd’s proposal for a revised Rider NS filing and proceeding so long as Metra and CTA are not restricted in their participation.

###### (iii) ComEd’s Position

ComEd argues that the Commission should reject CTA and Metra’s recommendation to exempt the RR Delivery Class from Rider NS. CTA and Metra propose that Rider NS be modified so that it no longer applies to new construction or other work performed by ComEd to upgrade or provide service to the RR Delivery Class for electric train or bus charging facilities or traction power substations. _See_ CTA/Metra

Joint Ex. 1.0 at 16-17; _see also_ CTA/Metra Ex. 1.03. It is ComEd’s position that the application of Rider NS to CTA bus depots and the RR Delivery Class traction power substations is the same as its application to all other customers: costs for new or additional points of service or other modification, in the absence of increased load requiring an additional standard point of service, will be recovered from the customer under Rider NS. _See_ ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 68-69. The exemption requested by CTA and Metra would only benefit them as the only members of the RR Delivery Class, at the expense of all other customers. _Id._ at 69. Staff witness Ashbaugh agreed with ComEd that allocating the costs to CTA and Metra is best, “rather than being unfairly distributed among all customers.” Staff Ex. 7.0 at 21-22, 23. ComEd argues that CTA/Metra cannot justify their Rider NS exemption request by claiming that public transit agencies cannot upgrade or change their facilities under Rider NS to do things like accommodate charging facilities for the new electric bus fleet or accommodate their planned battery-power train sets. _See_ CTA/Metra Joint Ex. 1.0 at 16-17.

ComEd argues that there are two reasons to reject CTA/Metra’s requests for special exemption to exclude themselves from the application of Rider NS. If adopted, CTA/Metra’s proposed exemption for themselves would shift costs currently collected from them specifically through Rider NS to ComEd’s customer base more broadly through base rates. _See_ ComEd Ex. 10.0 at 12. Second, the Commission already considered and rejected CTA’s similar recommendations seeking exclusion from Rider NS for electric bus charging infrastructure in ComEd’s BE Plan 1 Docket. CTA argued in that proceeding that the lines and equipment to be used for charging electric buses in its garages should be defined as “standard service” and such infrastructure should be carved out from Rider NS’s application. _See_ BE Plan 1 Order at 248 (describing CTA’s position that Rider NS should not impose additional fees on the CTA for make-ready electric bus infrastructure). The Commission, however, rejected CTA’s argument and held that make-ready electric bus infrastructure was not part of CTA’s standard service, and that Rider NS would not be modified to exclude the CTA’s garages. _Id._ at 249-250. The practical effect of the Commission’s holding is that Rider NS applies to electric bus infrastructure installed at CTA garages (or to Metra’s electric trains) if they are not otherwise meeting their standard. _Id._ at 249 (“Under Rider NS, customers would pay for the actual cost of make-ready infrastructure[.]”).

###### (iv) Staff’s Position

The current Rider NS framework correctly requires the Railroad Class, as the direct beneficiaries, to cover the costs of the infrastructure built specifically to serve its needs. Staff IB at 24-25. This approach is consistent with the Commission’s established cost-causation principles, which mandate that customers benefiting from infrastructure investments should proportionately bear the associated costs.

CTA and Metra’s proposed adjustments to reduce Railroad Class contributions (CTA IB at 20-22; Metra IB at 31-33) would disrupt this balance, shifting a financial burden to non-RR customers who neither benefit from nor rely on Railroad infrastructure. Such a shift would create an inequitable cost allocation, essentially subsidizing the Railroad Class at the expense of non-RR customers. Staff opposes the changes to Rider NS proposed by the Railroad class. CTA and Metra claim the Rider, in its current form, places a burden on mass transit agencies because it requires them to fund necessary infrastructure improvements to support electric vehicle charging stations and other needs. CTA/Metra Joint Ex. 1.0 at 17. The changes sought by CTA and Metra would eliminate their financial responsibility for upgrades. They argue that the costs for upgrading infrastructure, such as electric bus charging stations and traction-power substations, should not be solely borne by the transit agencies. _Id._

CTA raised this same argument in ComEd’s first BE Plan 1 Docket, arguing that make-ready infrastructure necessary for charging stations at CTA garages should be considered standard service. The Commission rejected this definition and declined to modify Rider NS. BE Plan 1 Order at 248. Accordingly, this issue has already been decided and the Railroad class is collaterally estopped from raising it again. _See, e.g.,_ Ill. Com. Comm’n v. Ill. Power Co., Docket No. 01-0701, Order at 7 (Feb. 19, 2004) ("The proper means by which to correct perceived errors in Commission orders is through the rehearing and appeal processes. Such corrections should not be sought in other dockets.").

Staff witness Ashbaugh testified that CTA and Metra can pass infrastructure costs onto their riders, “ensuring that those who directly benefit from the services contribute toward the necessary investments.” Staff Ex. 7.0 at 22. Passing costs onto railway riders allocates the costs to the customers who benefit from them, which is more equitable than socializing costs across all ComEd customers regardless of whether they utilize the railroads.

Additionally, Staff agrees with ComEd that modifying Rider NS to shift costs for the two customers in the Railroad class to other customers may not be revenue neutral (ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 68) and thus, for the reasons discussed previously, is outside the scope of this docket.

On exceptions, Staff proposes language prohibiting the railroad class from raising this issue for a third time in any investigation into a revised Rider NS.

###### (v) Commission Analysis and Conclusion

The Commission finds it has previously addressed the proposed Rider NS exemption in the BE Plan 1 Order. While Metra may amplify the CTA’s arguments by joining its request here, the evidence is insufficient for the Commission to revisit its recent ruling in this proceeding. Accordingly, the Commission declines to adopt CTA’s and Meta’s revisions to Rider NS. The Commission declines to specifically bar the CTA or Metra from raising these arguments in any investigation into a revised Rider NS as requested by Staff.

CTA/Metra Joint Ex. 1.0 at 17. BE Plan 1 Order at 251.

The Commission notes that, according to ComEd, customers are likely to have their deposits refunded assuming their estimated demands were reasonable accurate.

### C. Rider CFRA – Carbon Free Resource Adjustment

#### 1. REACT’s Position

REACT identified a problem with ComEd using stale market data to adjust the charges under Rider CFRA – Carbon-Free Resource Adjustment (“Rider CFRA”). That is, during the year forecasted prices will be changing, sometimes significantly, and those changes may not be captured using ComEd’s current methodology. As a result, there is an unnecessary risk that during times of high prices, customers would experience significant CFRA charges. _See_ REACT Ex. 1.0 at 9.

REACT notes that Rider CRFA is one of the ways in which nuclear power is subsidized in Illinois. Specifically, Rider CFRA implements a provision of P.A. 102-0662 designed to provide financial support for the Braidwood, Byron, and Dresden nuclear power plants for a five-year period, through a tariff that became effective on June 1, 2022. _See_ 220 ILCS 5/16-108(k); _see also_ REACT Ex. 1.0 at 12.

P.A. 102-0662 required that the Commission approve a tariff for ComEd that would result in customers subsidizing the nuclear power plants when wholesale prices are “low” and customers receiving a credit when wholesale prices are “high” compared to a base price (the “Base Price”) that was to be set by the Commission. Every May, ComEd calculates the CFR Adjustment for the summer period (June through September billing months) and for the non-summer period (October through May billing months). In September of each year, ComEd updates the CFR Adjustment for the non-summer period. _See_ Rider CFRA, ILL. C. C. No. 10, Original Sheet No. 390.1. ComEd may also update the CFR Adjustment “if the Company determines such revised CFR Adjustment results in a better match between the Company’s applicable incurred and expected costs and its recovery of those costs.” _Id._ ComEd uses forecasted prices to determine the monthly CFR Adjustment, and then includes another true-up adjustment four months after the current month. The four-month true-up adjustment is the difference between the CFRA adjustment and actual prices during the month.

REACT witness Fults testified that customers have been frustrated with the whole Rider CFRA process. _See_ REACT Ex. 1.0 at 16-17; REACT Ex. 5.0 at 9. As he explained: “The way in which Rider CFRA is currently designed, there unnecessarily is a significant risk that when wholesale power prices are high, customers will receive a Rider CFRA charge, and when wholesale power prices are low, customers will receive a Rider CFRA credit. This is exactly opposite of how Rider CFRA should work.” REACT Ex. 5.0 at 9. Mr. Fults proposed a straightforward solution: use the most recent market forecast data to make monthly adjustments. _Id._ at 11.

REACT notes that Staff agreed with Mr. Fults that a closer forecast should be more accurate than one that looks farther into the future and recommended that the Company be required to update the adjustment three times each year. _See_ Staff Ex. 6.0 at 6.

REACT reports that ComEd agreed that adjusting the CFR three times a year would “help produce a forecast that is more reflective of the market price and will also give customers a more consistent CFR Adjustment for four months.” ComEd Ex. 12.0 at 6-7. ComEd proposed tariff language that would provide that the updates would be filed in May, September and December every year and committed to make those revisions as part of a compliance filing in the instant proceeding. _See_ _id._at 7; ComEd Ex. 12.1; REACT Cross Ex. 1.0 at 3.

REACT and Staff agreed with ComEd’s proposal but noted that if ComEd does not update its CFR Adjustment in December 2024, that it should update the forecast in January 2025 as part of its compliance filing in this case. _See_ REACT Cross Ex. 2 at 2.

#### 2. ComEd’s Position

ComEd notes REACT proposed changes to Rider CFRA, recommending that ComEd increase the frequency of its updates to the Carbon-Free Resource (“CFR”) Adjustment. Specifically, Staff witness Hamidu proposed that ComEd update the CFR Adjustment three times a year, to produce a forecast that is more reflective of the market price and to give customers a consistent CFR Adjustment for four months. Staff Ex. 6.0 at 7. In response ComEd agreed with Staff’s proposal and submitted ComEd Ex. 12.01, which contains revisions to Rider CFRA to implement an update to the CFR Adjustment three times a year: in May, September, and December. ComEd Ex. 12.0 at 6-7; ComEd Ex. 12.01. While the REACT proposal is not contested and should be adopted, the Commission should also determine generally that issues pertaining to ComEd’s Rider CFRA are not related to the allocation of ComEd’s delivery service revenue requirement.

#### 3. Staff’s Position

Staff believes a closer forecast period is better than a forecast that is far out in the future. However, a monthly forecast for the CFRA, as proposed by REACT, will lead to a possible monthly change in the CFR Adjustments which will not provide consistency or allow customers to know what to expect. Staff Ex. 6.0 at 6. Staff believes that updates to the CFRA three times a year will help reduce the Carbon-Free Resource Monthly Reconciliation or the “true up” amount and produce a forecast that is more reflective of the actual price and will also give customers a consistent four-month CFRA. Staff Ex. 6.0 at 7. Staff recommended the Company agree to provide three updates a year; with the first update being for the June-September period, the second update, representing the October to January period, and third update, to cover the February – May period. Staff believes this approach will help produce a forecast that is more reflective of the market price and will also give customers a consistent CFRA for four months. Staff Ex. 6.0 at 7. The Company proposed three updates in its surrebuttal: in May, September, and December. ComEd Ex. 12.0 at 7. Staff agrees with this proposal with one caveat. Given the agreed upon schedule for this docket, it is not clear that a final Order will be entered by December of this year. If ComEd does not update the forecast in December 2024, Staff recommends ComEd update the forecast in January 2025 as part of its compliance filing and update the forecast in May, September, and December thereafter. Staff understands this issue is now uncontested. REACT Cross Ex. 2.

#### 4. Commission Analysis and Conclusion

The Commission notes that this issue is uncontested and approves the proposed revisions to Rider CFRA set forth in ComEd Ex. 12.01 to update the CFR Adjustment three times a year, in May, September, and December. Nonetheless, the Commission reiterates that Rider CFRA falls outside of ComEd’s delivery service revenue requirement as noted above.

### D. Rider SPWS – Solar Paired with Storage Rebate

#### 1. REACT’s Position

REACT has identified a flaw in the way in which ComEd is implementing Rider SPWS Rebate – Solar Paired With Storage Rebate (“Rider SPWS”) that results in ComEd failing to provide the Commission-approved incentive that is designed to encourage customer participation in the PJM Interconnection, L.L.C. (“PJM”) frequency regulation market. _See_ REACT Ex. 3.0; REACT Ex. 7.0. Specifically, REACT notes that ComEd’s tariff needs to be revised to ensure that ComEd is including in the rebate the “Part 466 and 467” interconnection costs that are paid to ComEd as it was directed to do in the Solar Paired With Storage Order on Rehearing. _See_ REACT IB at 36 (citing SPWS RH Order at 24); REACT RB at 37.

REACT underscores the importance of this rebate. Frequency regulation is an essential service that helps keep the grid in balance, responding instantaneously to changes that occur to match generation to load. REACT Ex. 3.0 at 6. REACT notes that ComEd witness Perkins recognized this service will become more important in the future with a higher penetration of renewable generation and the retirement of conventional power plants. Tr. at 35-36.

REACT asks that the Commission dismiss ComEd’s claims that this issue is “beyond the scope” of this proceeding. There is no legitimate basis to contest that REACT is requesting a revenue-neutral rate design change and thus this issue is properly considered within this proceeding. There also is no legitimate basis for ComEd to assert that REACT’s position is an improper collateral attack on the Commission’s prior Order. The statute that governs this proceeding authorizes the Commission to “consider revenue-neutral tariff changes related to rate design.” 220 ILCS 5/16-105.5(c)(1). This language requires that the Commission revisit its prior decisions that created the rate design contained in the existing tariffs. Moreover, far from “attacking” the prior Order, REACT is requesting that the Commission compel ComEd abide by its express terms.

Further, REACT explains that to accept ComEd’s position would require that a new complaint be filed regarding ComEd’s implementation of Rider SPWS, testimony would have to be presented again, and briefs would have to be rewritten, in a proceeding with fewer parties to review the testimony and arguments. This issue has been fully litigated and briefed in the instant proceeding. To the extent that the Commission finds there is any ambiguity in the statute, REACT explains the Commission should reject ComEd’s proposed reading of the Act because such a result would undermine the fact-finding process and would be less expeditious, less convenient and more costly. _See_ 83 Ill. Adm. Code Part 200.25. Issues related to the scope of the proceeding are further addressed supra in Section III.

Regarding the merits of this issue, REACT explains that the Commission has directed ComEd to use a “differential approach” to quantify the interconnection costs that are to be rebated under Rider SPWS. _See_ REACT IB at 36 (citing SPWS RH Order at 25). Essentially, the differential approach is a math equation:

> B–A=C

B are the interconnection cost assuming the battery is participating in the

frequency regulation market;

A are the interconnection cost assuming the battery is not participating in the

frequency regulation market; and

C are the interconnection cost that are to be rebated to the customer. _See also_ Tr. at 61. As REACT explained, the way in which ComEd is performing this calculation is improper because it results in ComEd refunding none of the “Part 466 and 467” interconnection costs that are paid to ComEd. _See_ REACT IB at 37 (citing SPWS RH Order at 25).

REACT maintains that ComEd improperly asserts that REACT’s proposed revisions ignore the costs of interconnection. According to REACT, ComEd has it wrong. REACT does not “ignore” the fact that there are interconnection costs that ComEd must recover if ComEd assumes that the owner of the facility is utilizing the full extent of its technical and operational capabilities. Rather, REACT explains that by making that assumption both for purposes of calculating what interconnection costs would be for a battery that is participating in the PJM frequency regulation market as well as for purposes of calculating what interconnection costs would be for a battery that is not participating in the PJM frequency regulation market, ComEd’s methodology ignores the “use case” and ends up failing to refund any “Part 466 and 467” interconnection costs that are paid to ComEd.

Thus, the result under ComEd’s current methodology is precisely the result that the Commission rejected in the proceeding that authorized the creation of Rider SPWS. _See_ SPWS RH Order at 24 (“the clearest reading of ‘interconnection facilities’ in Section 16-107.6(b-5) includes costs identified during the Part 466 or 467 interconnection process”).

REACT explains that ComEd should only assume that the owner of the facility is utilizing the full extent of the battery’s technical and operational capabilities for purposes of calculating what interconnection costs would be for a battery that is participating in the PJM frequency regulation market (“B” in the equation above). When ComEd is calculating what interconnection costs would be for a battery that is not participating in the PJM frequency regulation market (“A” in the equation above), ComEd should be required to assume that the use of the battery is to reduce the customer’s load or support the functions of the solar panels. _See_ REACT Ex. 3.0 at 11-12. This methodology will appropriately consider the “use case” of the battery and should result in ComEd appropriately refunding “Part 466 and 467” interconnection costs that are paid to ComEd as was directed by the Commission.

ComEd admitted that it has only received twelve Rider SPWS applications since the tariff was approved more than two years ago. ComEd also confirmed that in none of these cases has ComEd identified any incremental cost for upgraded transformers, switch gear, or line upgrades that would be included as part of the rebate. _See_ REACT Ex. 7.0 at 4; Tr. at 68. REACT witness Marhoefer presented uncontroverted evidence that ComEd’s failure to include these costs in the rebate calculation is preventing projects from moving forward. _See_ REACT Ex. 7.0 at 7.

REACT notes that Staff agreed that ComEd is not properly taking into consideration the “use case” of customers’ batteries as required by the Commission’s prior Orders, and requests that the Commission direct ComEd to revise its methodology “since the use case drives the amount of this component of the Rider SPWS rebate.” Staff IB at 28-29.

#### 2. ComEd’s Position

ComEd argues REACT’s proposal to modify ComEd’s Rider SPWS is both a collateral attack on the SPWS RH Order, and out of scope for this RDI. The proposal should be rejected. REACT’s proposed changes to ComEd’s Rider SPWS would alter the Commission-approved way ComEd calculates Incremental Interconnection Costs, which are a subset of the costs rebated under Rider SPWS. _See_ REACT Ex. 3.0 at 10, 12. The Commission already explicitly evaluated and rejected REACT’s proposal in SPWS RH Order. _See generally_ SPWS RH Order. Indeed, the same witness, REACT witness Marhoefer, who offered the testimony at issue in this proceeding offered testimony on behalf of Magid Glove (a member of REACT) in the Rider SPWS proceeding. REACT Ex. 3.0 at 4. Furthermore, costs rebated under Rider SPWS are not part of ComEd’s distribution revenue requirements and the mechanics are not related to ComEd’s delivery service rate design. ComEd Ex. 4.0 at 6. Therefore, the Commission should find that REACT’s proposal also falls outside the scope of this delivery service rate design investigation.

ComEd explains that it currently calculates the incremental interconnection costs by: (1) calculating what interconnection costs would be for an energy storage system (“ESS”) assuming it would not participate in the PJM frequency regulation market; (2) calculating what the interconnection costs would be for the same ESS assuming it intends to fully participate in the PJM frequency regulation market; and (3) subtracting the non-frequency regulation costs from the frequency regulation costs. SPWS RH Order at 25; _see also_ ComEd Ex. 4.0 at 14. This calculation was proposed by Staff and approved in the Rider SPWS RH Order.

ComEd notes that it evaluates the battery in the context of participation in the PJM frequency regulation market when considering the frequency regulation costs portion of the rebate under Rider SPWS. Tr. at 64. Therefore, it argues, the Commission should not change its decision from the SPWS Final Order or SPWS RH Order regarding the methodology with which ComEd calculates Incremental Interconnection Costs.

ComEd should not be “required to assume that the use of the battery is to reduce the customer’s load or support the functions of the solar panels[,]” and that ComEd be “required to determine the costs necessary to prevent distribution grid issues to the way in which the battery will operate,” as requested by REACT. REACT IB at 39. ComEd explained that it evaluates the proposed device for all potential adverse system impacts, as defined by Parts 466 and 467, such that ComEd can mitigate those adverse impacts and prevent disruption to grid operations. _See_ ComEd Ex. 9.0 REV. at 15; _see also_ 83 Ill. Adm. Code 466 and 467. Requiring that ComEd assume any specific use case for a battery when evaluating interconnection costs would artificially limit ComEd’s study of the battery and its adverse system impacts and is not reasonable, ComEd argues. ComEd urges the Commission to reject REACT’s proposal to artificially limit ComEd’s study of the battery when calculating interconnection costs.

#### 3. Staff’s Position

In addition to the incremental cost calculation recommended by Staff, the Commission’s SPWS RH Order noted that:

> Staff also recommends that the Commission require ComEd to provide a good faith estimate of costs that will be eligible for the rebate under Rider SPWS after the customer applies for the tariff. _Id._ at 4-5. The Commission adopts Staff’s proposal because it will allow for the most accurate identification of incremental interconnection costs.

SPWS RH Order at 25.

Staff notes REACT’s recommendation that the Commission direct ComEd to revise this calculation “to give meaning to each component of that rebate.” REACT Ex. 3.0 at 5. Staff originally disagreed with REACT, as there is no new information demonstrating or explaining why the Commission should reconsider a decision on an issue about which it has already definitely ruled. Staff Ex. 6.0 at 5. Staff agrees with the Company that the Commission should retain the incremental interconnection cost calculation methodology approved by the Commission in the SPWS RH Order. _Id._ However, Staff now understands that REACT is not requesting the Commission change its decision on this issue, but rather that the Commission direct ComEd to revise its calculation as its current approach does not fully align with the Commission’s decision in that case. REACT Ex. 7.0 at 6; REACT Cross Ex. 2. Staff agrees that use case should be considered when reviewing an interconnection application for ESS, as stated in Commission conclusion in the SPWS RH Order at 24. REACT Cross Ex. 2. The Commission also concluded “that both the bright-line and differential cost approaches must be performed on a case-by-case basis, just as interconnection studies performed pursuant to Parts 466 and 467 are performed on a case-by-case basis.” SPWS RH Order at 24.

As noted above, the Commission adopted Staff’s recommendation to require consideration of the specific use case in each application, since the use case drives the amount of this component of the Rider SPWS rebate. REACT Cross Ex. 2. Accordingly, the Commission should clarify this in its final Order in this proceeding and direct ComEd to comply with these provisions.

#### 4. Commission Analysis and Conclusion

The Commission finds the way in which ComEd is implementing Rider SPWS is troubling. The Commission was clear when it previously directed ComEd to calculate the “incremental interconnection costs” component of this rebate in a manner that results in ComEd including in the rebate those costs that are paid to ComEd for facilities that are identified during the Part 466 or 467 interconnection process. ComEd’s current methodology for calculating this rebate does not comply with that prior direction.

To be clear, the Commission directs ComEd to retain the incremental interconnection cost calculation methodology and to consider the use case when reviewing an interconnection application for ESS, as stated in the Commission conclusion on page 24 of the SPWS RH Order. To the extent this omission from the Rider SPWS Rebate is impeding projects from moving forward, the Commission emphasizes that its goal was and continues to be to incent solar projects, not to inhibit them. While Rider SPWS falls outside the delivery service requirement, this conclusion is merely restating the Commission’s position and correcting the Company’s misinterpretation of it. The Commission directs ComEd to provide Staff with an update regarding how it plans to comply with the Commission’s Order moving forward.

### E. Distribution Time of Use Rate

#### 1. Overview

##### a. ComEd’s Position

Pursuant to Commission directives in the BE Plan 1 Order, ComEd proposed a DTOU rate and presented corresponding data and analyses. BE Plan 1 Order at 258; _see generally_ ComEd Ex. 3.0. No party disputes that ComEd has fulfilled these directives, and the Commission should find that ComEd met all its obligations stemming from the BE Plan 1 Order. _Id._ ComEd also notes that the Commission is not required to approve a TOU rate proposal at this time.

Nonetheless, ComEd notes CUB/EDF propose that ComEd deduct from its distribution revenue requirement projected deferred distribution system investments that could result from adoption of ComEd’s proposed DTOU rate. CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 20-21. The Commission should reject this proposal because it is not revenue neutral and is therefore outside the statutory scope of Section 16-105.5. ComEd Ex. 4.0 at 16-17.

##### b. AG’s Position

ComEd asks that the Commission approve its proposed DTOU, which contains four pricing periods and rates and would require customers who sign up for time-variant supply rates to also enroll in the DTOU. This proposal arises from BE Plan 1 Order, in which the Commission ordered ComEd “to develop and propose a [DTOU] rate in its next rate design investigation proceeding.” ComEd Ex. 3.0 at 4; BE Plan 1 Order at 258. A DTOU “is a rate structure where cost of electric delivery charges varies depending on the time of day it is delivered. Under this structure, delivery charges are higher during peak demand periods and lower during off-peak periods.” ComEd Ex. 1.0 at 10.

Like any rate, ComEd has the burden to prove that its proposed DTOU is just and reasonable. 220 ILCS 5/9-201(c). The AG iterates that ComEd has failed to meet this burden because it did “not provide any evidence that it has developed a program that will achieve reasonable goals, or provide customers enough value to justify the costs.” ELPC/VS/UCS IB at 1-2. ComEd witness Kremer did not provide any cost-benefit analysis or other evidence to support the reasonableness of its proposed program costs, which she quantified as between 5 and 10 million dollars for the first time during the evidentiary hearing. _Id._ at 3.

The AG explains that ComEd’s lack of support for its proposed costs is particularly concerning, given that the record shows that ComEd’s proposed DTOU time periods are inconsistent with its own peak usage data, that it does not account for significant usage differences between the summer months and the rest of the year, and that it seeks to require all residential customers who enroll in a time-variant supply rate from an ARES or ComEd’s Residential Real Time Pricing Program (“RRTP”) to enroll in the DTOU—even though the savings periods between the supply side and distribution side programs may be inconsistent with each other. While the AG does not oppose the implementation of a voluntary opt-in DTOU, it urges the Commission to adopt CUB/EDF’s modifications to ComEd’s proposal as discussed below.

##### c. ELPC/VS/UCS’ Position

The purpose of TOU rates is to encourage customers to shift their usage from peak times to off peak to limit the need for new power plants, grid investment, and reduce pollution from fossil fuel. ComEd witness Kremer notes:

> A TOU rate must contain differences between on-peak and off-peak periods that are significant enough to encourage customers to shift usage without deterring customers from signing up for the rate.

ComEd Ex. 3.0 at 4. ComEd proposes to offer only a delivery rate, and Ms. Kremer estimates savings at only $2.31 per month, or approximately 5% of an average customer’s delivery cost. Tr. at 84. Given that delivery rates make up only about 1/3 of the customer’s bill, offering a supply TOU rate would greatly enhance this differential.

The Commission must address whether ComEd’s proposal accomplishes that goal of shifting enough customer behavior that the program meets just and reasonable standards. ComEd’s proposal and its testimony do not provide any evidence that it has developed a program that will achieve reasonable goals or provide customers enough value to justify the costs.

ELPC/VS/UCS allege that ComEd fails to provide any persuasive argument that its program will induce customers to shift usage off-peak. ComEd correctly states that its DTOU rate, “must contain differences between on-peak and off-peak rates that are significant enough to encourage customers to shift usage without deterring customers from signing up for the rate.” ComEd IB at 49 (citing BE Plan 1 Order at 258). ComEd’s IB claims it demonstrates this incentive and cites to witness Kremer’s direct testimony. ComEd Ex. 3.0 at 12; ComEd IB at 50. Ms. Kremer’s testimony lacks any substantive analysis that demonstrates she or anyone at ComEd has any idea whether the program will shift enough behavior to make a difference in customers actually using energy off-peak when they rely more on renewable supply. Ms. Kremer merely states the conclusion that the program includes financial incentives to encourage customers to enroll. ComEd Ex. 3.0 at 12. That conclusory statement does not demonstrate that this program is likely to produce results.

ELPC/VS/UCS assert ComEd has the burden of proof to demonstrate that its program will shift behavior and that the costs provide actual benefits. 220 ILCS 5/9-201(c)). ComEd fails to meet that burden. ComEd provides no estimates about how many customers it anticipates will sign up for TOU, how many suppliers it anticipates will offer supply TOU, or any data from other states that allow the Commission to reach a finding on the merits of its proposal. ComEd offers only details on the time periods and prices it intends to offer. That is not enough to demonstrate ComEd’s TOU offering will shift customer behavior.

Ideally, ELPC/VS/UCS hope the Commission will conclude it has legal authority to order ComEd to offer TOU rates. If the Commission determines it lacks that authority or no party has sufficiently made the case for ComEd to order Supply TOU rates, then it must reject ComEd’s filing and order it to design a better program.

##### d. Commission Analysis and Conclusion

The Commission approves ComEd’s DTOU rate, subject to the modifications discussed below. Through the development and implementation of its DTOU rate, and its recent RTOUPP, the Commission expects ComEd to use the data and lessons learned to refine and improve incorporation of TOU rates in the near future. The Commission finds that a well-designed TOU rate can cost-effectively deliver benefits to customers, including bill savings resulting from shifts in customer load and the deferral of distribution system investments. ComEd should examine how its DTOU rate delivers savings to customers and consider further modifications or expansion of TOU rates to enhance these benefits, leveraging AMI meter investments and capabilities for all customers. Data from the DTOU and ComEd’s RTOUPP could inform the development of a bundled supply and distribution TOU rate, as contemplated by ELPC/VS/UCS in Section E.3.f of this Order.

The Commission acknowledges ELPC/VS/UCS’s position regarding an STOU and further addresses it in Section VI.F.4.

#### 2. Time Periods

##### a. ComEd’s Position

ComEd’s DTOU rate proposal includes four pricing periods: Morning (6 AM to 1 PM), Mid-Day Peak (1 PM to 7 PM), Evening (7 PM to 10 PM), and Overnight (10 PM to 6 AM). ComEd Ex. 6.0 at 10. The DTOU rate is meant to provide a pricing structure that is flexible enough to provide for different rates in four distinct time periods to account for changing profiles in the future due to factors such as solar or EV charging habits. _Id._ at 13-14. These time periods are based on the feeder peaking data of ComEd’s distribution system and thus correlate with times when demand on ComEd’s distribution grid is highest. ComEd Ex. 3.0 at 15. ComEd also chose the names because they are intuitive, using common terms that customers can easily remember. _Id._ at 16.

ComEd argues that a seasonal factor, whereby the DTOU rate being artificially higher in the summer and lower in non-summer, is not logical for a distribution rate because it defeats the efficiency of the DTOU rate design. ComEd Ex. 6.0 at 14. ComEd explains that its DTOU rate is designed to recover costs throughout the year in a way that ensures customers will pay their fair share of delivery costs under either the flat rate or the DTOU rate based upon average customer usage for each delivery class and a seasonal factor would disrupt this design. ComEd Ex. 11.0 2nd CORR. at 7. ComEd argues that ultimately, a seasonal factor is not necessary for the DTOU rate because the costs that ComEd incurs to build and maintain its distribution grid do not follow the same seasonal profile as supply rates. _Id._ at 15.

##### b. Staff’s Position

In compliance with the Commission’s BE Plan 1 Order, ComEd worked with stakeholders in advance of this proceeding to develop the DTOU that ComEd proposes in this docket. ComEd Ex. 3.0 at 4. The DTOU would apply to the DFC which includes distribution costs. ComEd witness Kremer testified that distribution costs “are generally related to the purchase and maintenance of equipment like poles, conduit, wire and cable, and transformers.” _Id._ at 7. Currently for ComEd residential customers, the DFC is a per kWh charge, multiplied by the total kWh usage during the month; there is no distinction based on the day of the week or time of day. _Id._ at 11. ComEd’s DTOU proposes four different DFCs for four different time periods of the day. Collectively, the DFCs would recover the same amount of revenue the existing fixed-price DFC recovers. _Id._ at 15.

Staff is supportive of ComEd’s DTOU proposal generally. However, Staff recommends the Commission adjust ComEd’s proposed DFC scheduling blocks. The four scheduling blocks are Morning, Mid-Day Peak, Evening, and Overnight; ComEd initially proposed the Overnight period start at 11:00 PM and continue until 6:00 AM. _Id._ at 16. Staff witness Martin testified that the Overnight period as proposed by ComEd is too short to create real incentives for non-EV customers to change their behavior. Staff Ex. 1.0 at 6. Staff recommends the overnight period begins two hours earlier, at 9:00 PM instead of 11:00 PM, to be more attractive to the entire residential customer base. _Id._ at 3. In rebuttal testimony, ComEd modified the Overnight period in response to Staff, proposing to start it at 10:00 PM. ComEd 6.0 at 10. While Staff appreciates this change, the 9:00 hour should be included in the Overnight period if the objective is to incentivize customers to shift load; Staff notes that, according to ComEd’s data, only 0.6% of total feeders reach maximum load during the 9:00 hour, meaning over 99% of feeders have capacity. Staff Ex. 4.0 at 12. ComEd should have no issues serving customers who are incentivized to shift load without impacting peak load. Accordingly, Staff continues to recommend the Overnight period start at 9:00 PM.

To ensure DTOU scheduling blocks remain revenue neutral, Staff recommends rates in other time periods be adjusted to reflect the shift in hours from the Evening period to the Overnight period. Staff Ex. 1.0 at 11-13. Specifically, Staff witness Martin recommends that the difference in rates from the adjustment from the Evening and Overnight Periods be added onto and spread across the thirteen hours of the Morning and Mid-Day Peak Periods while keeping the rate in the Morning Period below the DFC rate. Staff Ex. 1.0 at 12. Staff’s recommendation in total is:

**Staff’s recommended DTOU time-period adjustments:**

| ---------------- | ---------- | -------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Morning          | 6:00 AM    | 1:00 PM  | `$0.03747 < “New Morning Rate” <`    |
| Mid-Day Peak     | 1:00 PM    | 7:00 PM  | `“New Mid-Day Peak Rate” > $0.10276` |
| Evening          | 7:00 PM    | 9:00 PM  | `$0.03747`                           |

**Note:** This table is using Single Family No Electric Space Heat Rates as an example, but the same suggestive adjustments are for the other residential rate classes relative to their individual respective rates mentioned in ComEd Ex. 3.0.

Staff’s recommendation is intended to make the DTOU rate attractive to all residential customers, not just EV owners. Specifically, Staff believes this change will financially incentivize non-EV owners to shift usage of heavy electric load appliances to later in the evening when electric demand is lowest.

Addressing the needs of non-EV customers is important, as currently ComEd’s residential customer base is approximately 3.3 million customers but only around 80,000 (about 2.4%) own an EV. Staff Ex. 1.0 at 11. Accordingly, most residential customer electricity consumption is related to heavy electric load appliances. _Id._ at 10. Since household appliances are not replaced as often as smaller-use items such as light bulbs, even if customers are incentivized to transition to energy efficient appliances, that transition will happen over a long period. _Id._ Accordingly, Staff’s proposal for a DTOU schedule with four time periods, three of which are below the Flat DFC, and the least expensive of which, the Overnight Period, begins at 9:00 PM, will be attractive to all residential customers and provide them with the financial incentives necessary to change their electrical consumption behaviors. _Id._ at 13. CUB/EDF witness McCann proposed matching the proposed DTOU periods with the time periods used in the RTOUPP. CUB/EDF 1.0 at 15. Staff examined the direct testimonies of the intervenors relating to DTOU program and their suggestive changes to ComEd’s proposed time periods. Staff witness Martin testified that consistency with RTOUPP time periods is unnecessary, as that pilot program has ended. Staff Ex. 4.0 at 6. However, Staff does not oppose the changes CUB/EDF proposes to other time periods, so long as those changes can be implemented consistent with Staff’s proposal to change the start time of the Overnight period. _See_ CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 14, 16. Specifically, Staff is not opposed to CUB/EDF’s proposed shifts in the “Morning” and “Mid-Day” periods which are also supported by the AG (CUB/EDF IB at 9; AG IB at 5-6), provided that any such changes: (1) are implemented concurrent with Staff’s proposal to change the start time of the Overnight period to 9 PM; (2) there are no changes to the evening period rate, and (3) the rate in the Morning Period stays below the DFC rate. Staff Ex. 1.0 at 12.

CUB/EDF witness McCann also recommended a DTOU should reflect seasonal differences between June to September (“summer”) and October to May (“non-summer”). ComEd opposed this change, arguing that seasonal patterns do not impact distribution costs in the same way they might affect supply costs. ComEd Ex. 6.0 at 14-15. Staff agrees with this assessment. Staff agrees with ComEd that delivery rates do not vary significantly with the seasons, making a seasonal rate unnecessary. Staff Ex. 4.0 at 7. ComEd testified that CUB/EDF’s specific proposal, which would result in a summer Mid-Day Peak rate almost 4.5 times the flat rate, has the potential to disproportionately benefit the limited number of residential customers with solar taking service under Rider Purchase of Generation Net Metering; this would unfairly shift revenues to these net metering customers and the cost shift would be borne by non-solar customers. ComEd Ex. 6.0 at 15-16. Staff agrees that CUB/EDF’s proposal would result in an unfair advantage to customers who own solar systems. Staff Ex. 4.0 at 7.

##### c. AG’s Position

The AG notes TOU rates “divide each 24-hour day into two or more pricing periods, and a specific, pre-determined price applies to all consumption within each period.” ComEd Ex. 3.0 at 8. CUB/EDF witness McCann raised several concerns with ComEd’s proposal. First, ComEd “does not quantify the criteria used to segment hours by load period” and neither Ms. Kremer “nor anyone else with the utility performed any analysis considering alternative time periods.” CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 12. To remedy these deficiencies, Dr. McCann analyzed ComEd’s feeder peak data and proposed modifications to ComEd’s pricing periods based on lower the peak rate during the hours of 1-2PM. _Id._ at 12-14. He then recommended that the Commission adopt the same pricing period names that ComEd used in its supply-side RTOUPP, as shown on Table 3 below, because this “is more consistent with what other utilities use and, of course, what Commonwealth Edison used in its own TOU pilot.” _Id._

**Table 3: CUB/EDF’s Proposed DTOU Time Periods**

| Period name | Start time | End time |
| ----------- | ---------- | -------- |
| Peak        | 6:00 AM    | 2:00 PM  |
| Super Peak  | 2:00 PM    | 7:00 PM  |
| Peak        | 7:00 PM    | 10:00 PM |
| Off Peak    | 10:00 PM   | 6:00 AM  |

_Id._ at 13-14. The AG asks that the Commission adopt CUB/EDF’s proposed DTOU time periods, shown on Table 3 above.

Dr. McCann also explained that ComEd’s proposal erroneously fails to account for seasonal differences in peak loads—particularly between summer months (June– September) and the rest of the year. _Id._ at 16, 17. A figure used to demonstrate these differences shows, “[n]o hours during the October to May Season approaches the Super Peak loads” from the summer and “the highest average load in the non-summer evening is in the bottom third of summer loads.” _Id._ at 17. Given these clear usage differentials, Dr. McCann asked that customers not be expected to pay Super Peak rates (like those proposed for ComEd’s mid-day period) during non-summer months.

ComEd argues that differentiating between seasons “is not pragmatic for a distribution rate” because “ComEd’s DTOU rate is designed to recover costs throughout the year in a way that ensures customers will pay their fair share of delivery costs under either the flat rate or DTOU rate based on average customer usage for each delivery class.” ComEd IB at 51. ComEd also claims that while supply rates “are driven by the cost of the kWh load in the wholesale supply market and are thus higher in summer months and lower in non-summer months,” ComEd’s costs “to build and maintain its distribution grid do not follow the same seasonal profile.” _Id._ While ComEd may incur these costs throughout the year, Dr. McCann demonstrates that customers’ usage patterns vary significantly between summer and non-summer months and ComEd’s proposed rate should reflect this variance. CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 17.

The AG states that if the purpose of the DTOU is to encourage ratepayers to use less energy during system peaks, then it only makes sense for the Company to provide ratepayers a reprieve from high super peak rates during months where there is less demand on ComEd’s system. “Charging customers a Super Peak rate during non-summer months is not reflective of cost causation and the benefits of load-shifting are far stronger in the summer than in the non-summer” and “Summer Peak does not reflect cost of service in the non-summer months.” CUB/EDF IB at 11. Given these clear usage differentials, the AG asks that the Commission adopt CUB/EDF’s proposal to only charge customers a Super Peak (or in ComEd’s proposal “Mid-Day Peak”) rate during summer months.

##### d. CUB/EDF’s Position

CUB/EDF agree with some aspects of ComEd’s proposed DTOU. CUB/EDF generally agree with four time periods over the course of a 24-hour day, and strongly encourage the implementation of a TOU rate. Further, CUB/EDF witness McCann suggested in his direct testimony a shift to the start time of the Overnight time period from the Company’s initially proposed 11 PM to 10 PM, in order to align with the Company’s successful TOU pilot and because of the lack of feeder peaks during the hour ending at 11 PM. CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 12-13. CUB/EDF continues to support this shift and appreciates the Company’s decision to make this adjustment.

CUB/EDF disagree with the Company on other aspects of the TOU proposal. First, CUB/EDF argue the 1pm hour should be in what ComEd proposes to call the “Morning” time period instead of the highest peak rate period. _Id._ This aligns with the RTOUPP the Company conducted. CUB/EDF contend, and no party disputes, that RTOUPP was a successful pilot, and it proved that its hourly periods are effective in achieving customer load-shifting and savings. _Id._ at 11. CUB/EDF contend this proven success indicates the initial TOU rate should follow the time periods of the pilot, with the understanding that in the future the time periods may shift to reflect data demonstrating future system load pattern changes. ComEd admitted that it did not analyze or consider any other time periods when developing its proposed DTOU. CUB/EDF Ex. 1.2. ComEd further admitted the time periods were based solely on percentage of feeder peak, and not on any analysis of customer behavior. _See_ _id._; Tr. at 81-83, 85-88, 93-95, & 97-99.

Second, CUB/EDF propose the TOU should only have three rates. CUB/EDF argue the two “shoulder” periods – the time periods between the highest-cost peak period and the lowest-cost overnight period – should share the same name and rate. CUB/EDF Ex. 2.0 at 10-11. This matches the RTOUPP and simplifies the rate for customers. CUB/EDF pose having different prices, especially a difference less than $0.005 per kWh as the Company proposes, will only cause confusion for customers. _Id._ Further, ComEd’s initial proposal on direct was for the two shoulder periods to have the same rate. _Id._ It was only after adjusting the 10 PM to 11 PM hour to appropriately be in the overnight period that the shift in rates occurred. _Id._ CUB/EDF contend the Company cannot credibly claim there is some inherent need for the rates to be different, as ComEd originally intended them to be the same. CUB/EDF propose revenue neutrality can be achieved through other shifts, not arbitrarily charging very slightly more for one off-peak shoulder period than the other. CUB/EDF argues the difference is too small to warrant separate treatment.

Dr. McCann also suggested naming the time periods to be clearer as to which rates are the highest and lowest. Dr. McCann proposed Off-Peak, Peak, and Super-Peak should be used as it matches ComEd’s own pilot and typical industry practice. CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 13-14. Dr. McCann suggested that if the Commission ultimately approves four different rates, Off-Peak, Pre-Peak, Post-Peak, and Peak is an appropriate naming convention, because it conveys more about relative prices than listing the time of day does. CUB/EDF Ex. 2.0 at 12. CUB/EDF posits customers know when times of day from the hours and do not need to be told by the name what to consider, for example, morning or afternoon, but they do not typically know how that correlates to the price they face.

In surrebuttal, ComEd suggested Super-Off Peak, Morning Off-Peak, Evening Off-Peak, and Peak. ComEd Ex. 11.0 2nd CORR at 15-16. CUB/EDF consider the addition of the “Off-Peak” language an improvement, but objects to ComEd still proposing to name the rates for specific time periods that, in future rate design cases, would be subject to change. To avoid the need for customer reeducation when load patterns change, CUB/EDF recommend naming the rate periods based on their relative cost and relationship to peak. Otherwise, ComEd may be in the position of having to explain to customers in the future that, for example, the peak is now in the morning instead of the afternoon and customers will have to reorient their usage accordingly. CUB/EDF consider Dr. McCann’s suggestions more adaptable and note his proposed rate period names would not require renaming if the peak periods change in future years. CUB/EDF argue this reduces customer confusion and conveys to customers the most important information they need: price.

Finally, because ComEd has two distinct load profiles between the “summer” (June to September) and “non-summer” (October to May) months, CUB/EDF contend the time of use periods should reflect seasonal differences in cost causation. CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 16-17; CUB/EDF Ex. 2.0 at 13-17. The highest hourly average load in the non-summer evening is in the bottom third of summer loads, and no hours during the non-summer months approaches Super-Peak loads. _Id._ CUB/EDF explain charging customers a Super Peak rate during non-summer months is not reflective of cost causation, and the benefits of load-shifting are far stronger in the summer than in the non-summer. _Id._ In other words, CUB/EDF argue, the Super Peak does not reflect cost of service in the non-summer months. CUB/EDF state applying the Super Peak rate to usage in the non-summer months unduly punishes customers for usage that does not impose a commensurate cost on the system. CUB/EDF argue peak usage during the summer months drives system costs for ComEd customers, and the TOU rate should reflect that reality.

ComEd claims seasonality of the rate is not necessary because distribution costs do not vary by season. ComEd Ex. 6.0 at 14-15. CUB/EDF argue this is a result of mistakenly applying accounting depreciation principles to physical reality. As Dr. McCann explained, assets experience physical wear and tear differently throughout the year depending largely on load and thermal stress. CUB/EDF Ex. 2.0 at 15. Delivery infrastructure experiences more stress when demands are placed on them, and more demands are placed on them in the summer. _Id._ Dr. McCann testified this physical depreciation is relevant for determining costs by time period and is why seasonality is important when considering the necessity of load shifting. _Id._ CUB/EDF therefore conclude the TOU rate must consider this physical depreciation cost.

In testimony, Ms. Kremer also presented a rate arbitrage concern for the seasonality proposal: If a customer is free to switch back and forth between rates, they can game predictable seasonal rate variation patterns to avoid paying their full share of cost of service. ComEd Ex. 11.0 2nd CORR at 6-7. To address Ms. Kremer’s rate arbitrage concern, CUB/EDF propose ComEd simply apply the same 12-month stay off provision to the TOU rate that it already applies to Rate BESH. CUB/EDF Cross Exs. 1.0, 2.0 & 4.0. The tariff for ComEd Rate BESH requires, “Following a termination of service hereunder for a residential retail customer, such residential retail customer is not eligible to receive service hereunder for a period of at least twelve (12) months following the date of such termination.” CUB/EDF Cross Ex. 12.0. In response to discovery, ComEd confirmed that this solution to seasonable rate arbitrage applies to several of its tariffs. ComEd Ex. 11.0 2nd CORR at 6-7. Identical language exists in ComEd’s RTOUPP tariff. CUB-EDF Cross Ex. 10.0 at 15 (Ill. C.C. No. 10 1st Rev’d Sheet No. 28.13). Moreover, the Company proposes to incorporate language implementing the DTOU into the tariff for Rate BESH, while retaining this 12-month stay-out requirement, therefore applying it to the DTOU as well. _See_ CUB/EDF Cross Ex. 12.0. ComEd confirmed that incorporating this language would mitigate rate arbitrage. CUB/EDF Cross Ex. 2.0. CUB/EDF thus concludes Ms. Kremer’s concern is without merit, and a seasonal approach to a TOU should be implemented.

CUB/EDF note ComEd failed to engage in its IB Brief with CUB/EDF witness McCann’s proposal to include the 1 PM hour (ending at 2 PM) in the “Morning” time period. ComEd IB at 50-51. Dr. McCann noted this shift would adjust the DTOU to match the successful RTOUPP time periods. CUB/EDF IB at 9. CUB/EDF maintain that the RTOUPP program was successful and remains the only time period proposal with any data support or analysis. _Id._ With the exception of the 1 PM hour, the time periods proposed by ComEd are otherwise already identical to RTOUPP. CUB/EDF post the success of RTOUPP should be weighed heavily when determining the appropriate time periods for the first iteration of the DTOU. Dr. McCann’s proposal does so.

Second, CUB/EDF state ComEd appears to misunderstand Dr. McCann’s argument regarding seasonality of the time periods. The Company, relying on their accounting witness Ms. Kremer, points to the cost to purchase the needed equipment for distribution not varying throughout the year. ComEd IB at 51-52. Dr. McCann never disputed this fact. CUB/EDF contend that what the Company did not address, because it cannot without conceding Dr. McCann’s point, is that the amount of use on the equipment increases during high use seasons causing physical depreciation. CUB/EDF IB at 10-12. CUB/EDF argue this physical depreciation goes unaccounted for in ComEd’s proposal. Dr. McCann testified that while accounting practices may reflect a standard rate of depreciation across the year, the reality is the physical depreciation fluctuates based on use and should be accounted for in a DTOU. _Id._ Dr. McCann added that encouraging less use during peak periods reduces wear and tear on the distribution equipment and will prolong the useful life of infrastructure. _Id._ Also, CUB/EDF point out, reducing peak demand reduces the need to invest in capacity-expanding infrastructure, thereby reducing system costs long-term. CUB/EDF takes issue with ComEd’s insistence on applying accounting principles to physical realities, rather than adjusting to real-world impacts. CUB/EDF conclude the DTOU should account for seasonality to properly consider all costs on the distribution system.

Staff pushes for the 9 PM hour, (ending at 10 PM) to be included in the “Overnight” time period. Staff IB at 29-32. CUB/EDF consider Staff’s support for this conjecture devoid of analysis or empirical basis. _Id._ In fact, CUB/EDF note, Staff witness Martin admitted to having done no analysis in arriving at his proposal. CUB/EDF Cross Ex. 6.0. To maintain revenue neutrality from this hour shift, Staff would increase the cost in the Morning and Mid-day peak periods, instead of increasing the cost in the Evening period (where the hour is removed) or the Overnight period (where the hour is placed), or maintaining the originally proposed three rates for the four periods by equally increasing the Morning and Evening periods. CUB/EDF point out Mr. Martin provided no analysis to support this proposal, and when asked for additional information in discovery, Staff was unable to provide anything beyond a restatement of testimony. CUB/EDF Cross Ex. 5.0. CUB-EDF urge the Commission not to adopt Staff’s proposed revenue shifting in this manner.

Staff joins the Company in opposing a seasonality component to the DTOU. Staff IB at 32-33. CUB-EDF argue much of Staff’s reasoning is merely stating an agreement with ComEd’s misunderstanding of Dr. McCann’s proposal and should be ignored for the reasons stated above. _Id._ Staff also raises a concern that a seasonal approach would disproportionately benefit certain rooftop solar customers. _Id._ CUB/EDF note Staff provides no analysis in support of this claim, and no explanation for why it is concerning a proposal may benefit some customers more than others. CUB/EDF point out there is no requirement that optional programs or services available to customers must equally benefit all potential customers. CUB/EDF argue there is certainly no logical reason to oppose a rate structure on the grounds that it that could potentially aid Illinois in reaching renewable energy goals by compensating rooftop solar customers for the system benefits they contribute. Further, CUB/EDF note, Staff witness Martin provided no analysis in support of this claim, nor had he determined to any degree what portion of ComEd’s customers participate in rooftop solar. CUB/EDF Cross. Ex. 7.0. CUB/EDF conclude Mr. Martin’s concerns are entirely based on his own, unsupported assumptions and the Commission should ignore Staff’s position accordingly.

##### e. Commission Analysis and Conclusion

The DTOU proposed by ComEd includes four scheduling blocks with corresponding rates. Staff supports naming these blocks as initially proposed by ComEd: Morning, Mid-Day Peak, Evening, and Overnight. CUB/EDF counter that labeling the scheduling blocks as Off-Peak, Peak, and Super-Peak would be easier for consumers to remember because the names would be tied to price. The Commission appreciates CUB/EDF’s consumer friendly perspective but agrees with ComEd that the names it initially proposed are intuitive, using common terms that customers can easily remember. The Commission declines to adopt CUB/EDF’s modifications.

Staff recommends the Commission adopt ComEd’s DTOU scheduling blocks with one modification: shifting the overnight period to begin at 9:00 PM. In response to concerns, ComEd already shifted this block from 11:00 PM to 10:00 PM, a shift CUB/EDF also supported. Staff argues that this earlier time would be more attractive to the entire residential customer base, not just EV customers. The AG supports CUB/EDF’s proposal to reject this change, which they argue is unsupported and instead suggest including the 1 PM hour in the Morning block to better align with the RTOUPP. Staff states that these changes are unnecessary because the RTOUPP has ended but does not oppose the changes.

There are three considerations in developing a TOU which the Commission previously set forth in the BE Plan 1 Order: (1) that any TOU rate developed contain difference between peak and off peak that are significant enough to encourage customers to shift usage without deterring customers from signing up for the rate; (2) the rates on-peak and off-peak periods should coincide with the distribution system on-peak and off-peak periods; and (3) the Company’s rate proposal should include an incentive for EV owners (i.e., monthly/annual bill credits) to go on the rate, but the rate should not be available exclusively to EV owners. BE Plan 1 Order at 258. The Commission finds that Staff’s proposal to move the Overnight block to 9:00 PM is supported by ComEd’s data indicating only 0.6% of total feeders reach maximum load during the 9:00 hour, meaning over 99% of feeders have capacity. _See_ Staff Ex. 4.0 at 12. Moving the block to this hour also aligns with the directives set forth above. In particular, the main objective of a TOU is to incent customer usage to off-peak period. By shifting the Overnight period earlier, it is possible that customers who may not own EV or appliances that have timing capabilities may be more inclined to manually start their appliances at 9:00 PM if they can take advantage of off-peak pricing. The Commission therefore adopts Staff’s modification to shift the Overnight block to start at 9:00 PM and make its recommended pricing changes to maintain revenue neutrality.

The Commission declines to adopt CUB/EDF’s proposal to include the hour of 1 PM in the Morning block because it is unnecessary to maintain consistency with the RTOUPP. Furthermore, in an extension of the reasoning supporting the scheduling blocks names, its inclusion may be confusing to customers.

Finally, CUB/EDF suggest the DTOU should account for seasonality to properly consider all costs on the distribution system. Staff agrees with ComEd’s position that seasonal patterns do not impact distribution costs in the same way they might affect supply costs and that a seasonal DTOU would result in an unfair advantage to customers who own solar systems. ComEd also expressed concerns about rate arbitrage, which CUB/EDF suggested could be resolved with a 12-month stay off provision. Nonetheless, the Commission rejects CUB/EDF’s proposal in the interest of maintaining simplicity and fairness to all customers.

#### 3. Pairing with Time-Variant Supply Rates

##### a. ComEd’s Position

ComEd proposes that the DTOU rate should be automatically applied when a customer chooses to be on a time-variant supply rate because the DTOU rate design is flexible and maximizes the value of a customer’s load-shifting decisions at minimal risk to the customer. Staff witness Martin concurred that ComEd customers should either elect a time variant supply rate coupled with ComEd’s DTOU rate, or that customers should elect to be on a fixed rate for both supply and distribution. Staff Ex. 4.0 at 9-10. ComEd’s proposed DTOU rate is designed to be automatically applied to customer bills for those who elect a time-variant supply rate and is not available as a separate rate for individual election. ComEd Ex. 3.0 at 13. ComEd argues that pairing of the DTOU rate with time-variant supply is intended to reduce confusion and maximize benefits to customers that wish to make decisions to shift their load. _Id._ at 13-14.

In support thereof, ComEd argues that under its proposed structure, DTOU rate customers have lower DFCs than ComEd’s flat delivery rate as long as they shift load out of the Mid-Day Peak Period. _See, e.g.,_ ComEd Ex. 3.0 at 350; ComEd Ex. 6.0 at 12. At most, customers on the DTOU rate would only see a difference of less than 5 cents per kWh between the flat rate and the Mid-Day Peak period on the DTOU rate. ComEd Ex. 6.0 at 8. ComEd states that this rate design is low risk and makes the DTOU rate flexible enough to accommodate customer behavior in response to a wide variety of supply-side price signals. ComEd Ex. 11.0 2nd CORR. at 8-9.

##### b. Staff’s Position

The Commission should reject ICEA/RESA’s and CUB/EDF’s suggestions to make DTOU rates voluntary rather than compulsory for customers who are on an STOU rate. ICEA/RESA Ex. 60 at 3; CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 10. ComEd witness Kremer testified that the delivery service price risk is low compared to the price risk for the supply rate, so an STOU, rather than a DTOU, will incentivize a customer to switch. ComEd Ex. 11.0 at 11. Staff witness Martin also opposed making the DTOU rate voluntary for a customer on an STOU because the customers will retain the ability to choose their electric supplier and thus their supply rate; pairing the STOU with the DTOU will reduce customer confusion. Staff Ex. 4.0 at 5.

##### c. AG’s Position

ComEd proposes that all residential customers who enroll in a supply rate from an ARES or ComEd’s RRTP be required to enroll in the DTOU. ComEd Ex. 3.0 at 13. According to Ms. Kremer, this is because “[i]f a customer would like to find a rate that rewards shifting their energy usage, it would be counterintuitive for ComEd to offer a non-time-variant distribution option to go with the time-variant supply election the customer just made.” _Id._ at 13-14. Ms. Kremer further notes that “[w]ithout this pairing, ComEd would need to program its systems to allow for several different rate combinations... rather than assigning each supply election to one corresponding distribution election.” _Id._ at 14. The AG points out that customers who are currently enrolled in ARES or RRTP variable rates are not required to enroll in a DTOU and “[f]orcing many of the existing [R]RTP and other time variant rate customers onto a new rate schedule could be disruptive for them” and require them to take part in a program that they neither anticipated nor wanted to enroll in. CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 10. In addition, the AG notes that ComEd’s proposal would add unnecessary complexity and competing incentives to the DTOU and make it harder for customers to understand, assess, and take advantage of the potential benefits of these programs.

ComEd argues that “[u]nder time-variant rates of any kind, the price a customer pays for electricity is based upon when the customer uses electricity.” ComEd IB at 52. The AG explains that ComEd fails to recognize that the time periods of these varying ARES and RRTP rates may not match the time periods of its proposed DTOU, and that its proposal will likely lead to situations where supply rates are higher during periods where distribution rates are lower and vice versa. CUB/EDF IB at 15. ComEd’s proposal would require customers “to track two separate, and at times conflicting price signal streams” which “are likely to blunt the intended customer response.” CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 8. For example, ComEd’s RRTP varies unpredictably with changes in the wholesale electricity market rather than a customer’s time of use. _Id._ at 7-8. This means that on a sunny afternoon in spring, a customer might pay less for electricity supply because it is in abundant supply due to solar power on the grid. _Id._ By contrast, DTOU rates always vary on a strict schedule. Customers would always find it cheaper under DTOU rates to set their dishwasher to run at 3:00 AM as opposed to 3:00 PM, because the DTOU rate is much lower at 3:00 AM. _See id._ However, the STOU is more variable and may offer opportunities to use lower-priced supply at other times of the day. Adding to the confusion, Ms. Kremer testified that she was unaware if any ARES in ComEd’s service territory currently offers an STOU, or whether any ARES will offer an STOU in 2026— when ComEd’s proposal would go into effect. ELPC/VS/UCS IB at 3. The AG maintains that this situation creates a clear catch-22 for the consumer: on a sunny afternoon with abundant solar energy available, the same electricity would be both cheaper (from the RRTP supply perspective) and more expensive (from the DTOU perspective). _Id._

To grapple with these concerns, Ms. Kremer called for consumers to research supply-side rate options to maximize affordability under ComEd’s new proposal—but her responses missed the point. _See_ ComEd Ex. 11.0 2d Corr. at 9. Ms. Kremer admitted that the volatility in supply-focused rates would create risk for the consumer, stating “[t]he primary risk to the customer’s bill lies with the customer’s supply election, as it always has.” _Id._ at 9. The AG iterates that building ComEd’s rate around the assumption that consumers will research supply elections goes against its alleged goal of being “customer-friendly.” ComEd Ex. 3.0 at 14. The AG further points out that while ComEd’s RRTP coupled with the DTOU could create competing incentives, as discussed in the paragraph above, ARES supply TOU rates pose additional problems that cannot be solved with “customer research.” There are currently 87 ARES operating in ComEd’s service territory. Illinois Commerce Commission Office of Retail Market Development, Annual Report to the Illinois Commerce Commission, the General Assembly, and the Governor, Submitted Pursuant to Section 20-110 of the Illinois Public Utilities Act at 11 (July 2024). [2024 ORMD Annual Report](https://icc.illinois.gov/api/web-management/documents/downloads/public/icc-reports/2024%20ORMD%20Section%2020-110%20Report.pdf). Residential customers who receive their supply from an ARES currently pay approximately 3 cents more per kWh than customers who receive their supply from ComEd. _Id._ at 29. As a result, ARES customers in ComEd’s service territory pay $13.9 million more per month, or $167 million more per year, than customers who receive their supply from ComEd. _Id._ The AG notes that asking customers to “research” the best supply rate to couple with the DTOU is unreasonable given the excessive rates charged by ARES, particularly when it would be simpler for ComEd to offer its own STOU, as discussed below.

The AG asserts that a more “customer-friendly” rate would provide customers with a straightforward STOU that matches the time periods of the DTOU, thus eliminating the complexity and competing incentives of ComEd’s proposal. And that if ComEd’s goal is, as Ms. Kremer claims, to create “[t]he most straightforward and customer-friendly offering... one where our customers can maximize the effects of any actions they will take to shift load,” then the Commission should adopt CUB/EDF’s modifications to ComEd’s DTOU and direct ComEd to offer an STOU with corresponding pricing periods.

The AG explains that the danger of competing and often high supply rates and the need to coordinate the supply and DTOU rates apply to all customers but are particularly concerning for low-income customers who are unable to absorb unexpectedly high bills. RTOUPP Order at 11. In ComEd’s own supply RTOUPP, early reports showed that low-income participants paid more in the aggregate than they would have paid under default rates. RTOUPP Docket, Residential Time-of-Day Pricing Pilot Semi Annual Compliance Filing #6 at 23 (April 12, 2023). However, ComEd “proactively monitored income eligible customers” and communicated directly with those “showing a significant loss in any single month, or those demonstrating a trend of losses across two or more months.” RTOUPP Docket, Residential Time-of-Day Pricing Pilot Semi Annual Compliance Filing #8 at 23 (April 12, 2024). Complicating the supply election process for consumers makes it less likely that they will be able to maximize affordability, putting low-income consumers at risk of higher bills that they should not be asked to shoulder. Low-income customers are less likely to take advantage of cost-saving benefits when they must proactively select an alternate supply rate from a market that, as demonstrated above, often charges customers significantly more than they would pay under ComEd’s default rate. As such, under ComEd’s proposal, consumers cannot and should not be required to take the time needed to research strategic supply elections to utilize the DTOU. _See_ _id._; ComEd Ex. 11.0 2d Corr. at 9. Rather, many customers are more likely to stick with their default or preexisting supply election, even when they could save more with a simple STOU paired with the DTOU. _Id._ This could lead to significantly higher rates for some customers, despite the potential benefits of the DTOU.

To maximize cost-saving potential, the AG asks that the Commission order ComEd to offer an STOU rate that customers can choose to combine with the DTOU rate. _See_ CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 3. Given outstanding uncertainties about the savings potential of TOU rates and the effect on ratepayers, the AG emphasizes that both the STOU and DTOU rates should be offered solely on a voluntary opt-in basis. Further requiring customers who select a time-varying supply rate to also accept a DTOU rate magnifies the effect of the customers’ ability to control when they use electricity. A customer may want to experiment with either a supply or delivery TOU to see how it affects the bill, or to hedge the effect of TOU pricing. For these reasons, the AG asks that the Commission adopt CUB/EDF’s proposed STOU, paired with their recommended modifications to ComEd’s DTOU on a voluntary opt-in basis.

##### d. CUB/EDF’s Position

CUB/EDF oppose ComEd’s proposal to automatically apply the DTOU rate to all residential and small commercial customers taking service on any time-variant supply rate and to offer it to no other customers. Instead, CUB/EDF propose that the DTOU be its own separate rate offering, available on an opt-in basis to ComEd distribution customers regardless of the customer’s choice of supplier or supply rate, with one exception. CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 5-6. CUB/EDF argue that provided the Commission adopts CUB/EDF’s proposal for ComEd to offer an STOU and DTOU with matching hourly rate periods, the identical designs of these rates mean it is most sensible for any customer who signs up for ComEd’s supply TOU to be enrolled as well in the DTOU, on an opt-out basis. _Id._ at 6, 24. CUB/EDF maintain this approach balances ComEd’s stated interest in minimizing election points that customers face to sign up for a simple combined TOU rate without forcing any customers into taking service on a confusing hodgepodge of misaligned rates. _See_ ComEd Ex. 3.0 at 13-14. Further, because ARES cannot offer distribution rates, default-enrolling a customer who has selected ComEd’s STOU rate in a DTOU, while also making this DTOU available on an opt-in basis for any ARES customer, does nothing to inhibit retail competition.

CUB/EDF contend making the DTOU available as its own separate rate offering is necessary to: (1) conform with the clear intent of the Commission’s order approving the Company’s Beneficial Electrification Plan, (2) ensure the DTOU is an available and appealing option for ComEd customers who do not necessarily wish to also enroll in Rate BESH, and (3) avoid undue discrimination against ARES and their customers. Further, while CUB/EDF agree with ComEd that Illinois’ net energy metering law makes the pairing of a time-variant distribution rate with a flat supply rate unappealing to net metering customers, CUB/EDF argue the Company need not automatically pair time-variant supply rates with the DTOU to account for this contingency. CUB/EDF propose ComEd instead simply include in the sign-up process for net metering and time-variant rates a disclaimer explaining the financial implications of their options. Thus, CUB/EDF conclude ComEd’s proposed constraint on customer choice is overbroad and wholly unnecessary to address the concerns the Company raises.

In approving ComEd’s BE Plan 1, the Commission “require[ed] ComEd to develop and propose a TOU distribution rate in its next rate design investigation proceeding,” which is the immediate docket. BE Plan 1 Order at 258. Further, “the Commission direct[ed] ComEd to work with Staff and stakeholders in the development of the new proposed distribution rate.” _Id._ at 263. The Commission consistently referred to the required proposal as a new rate offering. _Id._ at 258, 263. In response, CUB/EDF argue, ComEd has not proposed a new rate offering; rather, the Company proposes only adding terms to its existing Real Time Pricing rate offering, Rate BESH, and its Rate RDS for ARES customers. ComEd Ex. 3.02 at 1-2. CUB/EDF explain that no new rate offering would be created, and customers still would not have the option to enroll in a DTOU rate by itself. There are at present no ARES time-variant rates available to these customers. CUB/EDF Cross Ex. 3.0. Thus, for the foreseeable future, to take service on a DTOU rate, customers would have to enroll in ComEd’s Rate BESH for supply service as well, thereby subjecting themselves to unpredictable variable and fixed charges that undermine the point of offering a predictable TOU rate design. _See_ CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 7-9. Under ComEd’s proposal, there still would be no such thing as a straightforward TOU rate available to its customers. CUB/EDF conclude this arrangement would fail to satisfy the Commission’s clear intent in the BE Plan 1 Order for ComEd to propose, for the Commission’s consideration and potential adoption, a TOU rate per se.

CUB/EDF argue automatic pairing, especially with Real Time Pricing, unnecessarily increases complexity and risk, likely dissuading customer participation. Further, automatically pairing the DTOU with Rate BESH makes it significantly less attractive to customers. Dr. McCann testified, “Time variant rate schedules must be simple for customers to understand and use to be widely adopted.” CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 7. Accordingly, CUB/EDF propose that ComEd offer customers a TOU rate that applies the same price per kilowatt hour each day to usage during each of an establish range of hours. CUB/EDF states whether a customer elects to take service on an STOU rate, a DTOU rate, or both, their experience is simple. CUB/EDF argue this simplicity is especially evident if, as CUB/EDF proposes, ComEd’s STOU and DTOU rate offerings feature matching hourly interval ranges. CUB/EDF contend this design feature would maximize ease of use by providing customers a unified price signal, seamlessly integrating the STOU and DTOU rates to where, from the customer’s perspective, they are indistinguishable from a single combined TOU rate. Thus, CUB/EDF conclude their proposal maximizes simplicity and customer choice.

CUB/EDF contrast their own straightforward approach to ComEd’s rigid, convoluted proposal to automatically enroll every residential or small commercial customer taking service on a time-variant supply rate to also take service on a DTOU, regardless of whether these rates are even remotely consistent with each other. CUB-EDF point out ComEd’s proposal fails to provide customers with the option to simply take service on a TOU rate. Instead, unless and until ARES develop other time-variant supply rates, the only option for a ComEd customer to take service on a TOU rate is a DTOU, which is then automatically paired with ComEd’s Real Time Pricing rate, Rate BESH. Dr. McCann testified, “having one portion of a customer’s bill determined on an hourly basis and another on a multipart time period basis complicates a customer’s decision rather than simplifies it.” CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 7. Dr. McCann further explained that Rate BESH applies real time prices in the PJM wholesale market, which often diverge drastically from the preset DTOU prices. _Id._ at 7-8. “For example, higher solar power production in the early afternoon in the spring can push PJM prices below nighttime prices while the distribution TOU price would be higher at the same time.” _Id._ at 8. In this common situation, and many others like it, a customer would be forced to perform a multivariate analysis considering multiple price signals, one of which is known in advance while the other is not. _Id._ at 147-148; CUB/EDF Ex. 2.0 at 7. In fact, Rate BESH’s energy price for each hour does not settle until the end of the hour. _Id._ at 141-145. Thus, CUB/EDF explain, Rate BESH customers are not responding to price signals so much as they are attempting to predict future shifts in volatile energy markets. Dr. McCann observed, “This situation will be challenging to a computer scientist much less a typical customer.” _Id._ at 7. Dr. McCann concluded, “These confusing signals are likely to blunt intended consumer response. A single unified overall price, such as one delivered through a combined TOU rate, avoids that problem.” CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 8.

Pairing the DTOU with ComEd’s Rate BESH imposes another layer of complexity, and an additional source of risk: the Capacity Charge. CUB/EDF Ex. 2.0 at 8. The Capacity Charge is a fixed monthly charge accounting for roughly 40% of participants’ supply costs, resetting once every twelve months. _Id._ at 8; _see also_ CUB/EDF Cross Ex. 12.0. The Capacity Charge is calculated as the PJM Net Load Price for the PJM Planning Year, adjusted by uncollectible factors, multiplied by the customer’s volumetric usage during the five highest peak systemwide usage hours across ComEd’s service territory, and the same for PJM’s service territory, with overlapping hours counting double, for the previous year. CUB/EDF Ex. 2.0 at 150-153; CUB/EDF Cross Ex. 12.0 at 13. When ComEd proposed to incorporate the same capacity charge mechanism into a supply TOU rate (RTOUPP), the Company’s own witness admitted ComEd “cannot precisely predict which days will be the system peak demand days.” CUB/EDF Ex. 2.0 at 8. A witness for the AG further testified that ComEd cannot know in advance which 5 to 10 of the 8,760 hours in a year will matter in determining the amount of the Capacity Charge, thereby providing not so much a price signal as a guessing game with significant financial consequences. _Id._ at 8. In response, the Commission rejected ComEd’s previous attempt to automatically pair the Capacity Charge with a TOU rate, finding it likely that “customers will never make the connection between a fixed Capacity Charge on their current bill to their usage during a few peak hours the summer before.” RTOUPP Order at 27-28. CUB/EDF ask the Commission to do so again here.

CUB/EDF maintain, given the complexity and inherent uncertainty of determining the cost of using electricity at any given time for Rate BESH customers, it is no surprise that, as of this past December, only 1.1% of ComEd residential customers and 1.3% of ComEd small commercial customers take service on the rate. _See_ ComEd Ex. 3.0 at 8. CUB/EDF argue it stands to reason customers’ hesitance to enroll in Real Time Pricing does not stem from the rate being too simple. Now, the Company proposes to add another layer of complexity to what CUB/EDF consider an already difficult program for customers to comprehend. If a customer prefers to take service on both the DTOU and Rate BESH or an ARES time-variant rate, CUB/EDF suggest they should be at liberty to do so, but on an opt-in basis. CUB/EDF Ex. 2.0 at 9. CUB/EDF argue offering the DTOU only as a package deal with a rate that is either already established to be repellently risky (Rate BESH) or as of yet strictly hypothetical (an ARES time-variant rate) would undermine the effort to introduce ComEd’s customers to the benefits of a TOU rate. _Id._ at 9; CUB/EDF Cross Ex. 3.0.

CUB/EDF argue that automatic pairing would unnecessarily hinder retail competition and choice. ICEA witness Wright testified that Illinois law reflects a policy preference to allow customers to choose their preferred ARES. ICEA/RESA Ex. 3.0 at 6. Mr. Wright elaborated:

> Not all time-variant supply rates or products are the same because they will cater to different customer risk tolerances and ability to reliably shift load. Each customer will make independent evaluations of risk tolerance along with potential upside. While some customers may (as [ComEd witness] Ms. Kremer suggests) seek to maximize the value of load-shifting, other customers will be less able to consistently shift load and thus will not want to take on the additional risk on their delivery bill. This should be the customer’s choice and not ComEd’s.

_Id._ (emphasis in original). Accordingly, Mr. Wright arrives at the same conclusion as CUB/EDF: The Commission should reject ComEd’s proposal to automatically pair the DTOU rate with any time-variant supply rate and instead should offer the DTOU as its own separate tariff. _Id._ at 8.

CUB/EDF contend ComEd’s proposal further threatens retail competition by extending an automatic 12-month stay off provision to the DTOU and pairing the DTOU, and by extension this requirement, with all time-variant supply rates. In discovery, the Company explained that its proposed automatic pairing of the DTOU with all time-variant supply rates means, “the application of such a requirement would prevent a customer from taking service under any time-of-use rate, including the DTOU rate, for a period of twelve months.” CUB/EDF Cross Ex. 2.0. Thus, if a customer were to unenroll from Rate BESH, they would have to wait 12 months to enroll in a time-variant rate offered by an ARES. CUB/EDF agree with ComEd that rate arbitrage is a concern for customers leaving and returning to the same time-variant rate within 12 months, but offer that it would be problematic to apply the same stay off requirement to switching from one time-variant rate to another, as ComEd proposes. CUB-EDF suggest the Commission can avoid this problem by ordering ComEd to offer the DTOU as its own separate tariff, so the associated stay off provision does not adversely impact retail supplier choice.

CUB/EDF argue the net metering credits problem ComEd points to does not require automatic pairing to solve. Ms. Kremer explained in testimony that Illinois’ net energy metering law is structured to prevent a net metering customer who takes service on both a flat supply rate and the DTOU from accessing a potentially large portion of the customer’s net metering bill credits. ComEd Ex. 11.0 at 12-13. Ms. Kremer elaborated that net metering customers can avoid this problem if they enroll in either (1) a time-variant supply rate and a flat distribution rate or (2) a time-variant supply rate and a time-variant distribution rate. _Id._ at 13. She further noted the net metering statute requires ComEd to offer the same rates to net metering customers that it offers to non-net metering customers, and therefore, the Company cannot offer the DTOU rate as a standalone tariff without also offering it as such to net metering customers. _Id._ at 14.

CUB/EDF agree with ComEd’s reading of the statute. However, CUB/EDF explain ComEd’s proposed solution – prohibiting all customers from pairing a time-variant distribution rate with a flat supply rate or vice versa – is unnecessary. CUB/EDF point to a simpler solution: ComEd can offer the DTOU rate as a standalone tariff but provide net metering customers the necessary information during the sign-up process to make an informed choice. If, after receiving the necessary information about how their rate options affect their bill credits, the customer still insists on signing up for the DTOU and a flat supply rate, that is their own choice. They would retain the option to enroll in a time-variant supply rate or to unenroll from the DTOU or net metering, depending on their preference. It is simply not necessary to impose the aforementioned negative consequences of automatic pairing to address this issue. ComEd knows which of its customers take service on which of its tariffs and can provide the necessary disclaimers to any customers who find themselves in this situation. Merely offering a rate does not force a customer to enroll, and net metering customers are not the only ones who may not benefit from enrolling in this rate. CUB/EDF maintain to design around just one small subset of customers to only mitigate their risk is unnecessary. CUB/EDF argue state policy encouraging customer choice supports the approach to provide customers with the relevant information and let them make their own decisions. CUB/EDF conclude purporting to protect customers by taking away rate choices is the type of paternalistic ratemaking that customer choice was intended to move away from. For the foregoing reasons, CUB-EDF request the Commission should reject ComEd’s proposal to automatically pair the DTOU with time-variant supply rates.

CUB/EDF oppose ComEd’s proposal to automatically and exclusively apply the DTOU rate to every residential and small commercial customer who is on a time-variant supply rate. _See_ ComEd IB at 52-53. The Company claims this is appropriate because the DTOU rate is “flexible and maximizes the value of a customer’s load-shifting decisions at minimal risk to the customer.” _Id._ at 52. CUB/EDF note ComEd offers no record support for this statement.

CUB/EDF agree there are many potential benefits for customers who take service on a DTOU rate. However, CUB/EDF argue requiring that all time-variant supply rate customers also take service on the DTOU rate, and not offering the DTOU rate to anyone else, certainly is not a policy choice in the direction of flexibility. CUB/EDF maintain a rate offering cannot be said to be flexible if it limits, rather than increases, customer choice.

Further, CUB/EDF, ICEA/RESA, and the AG note, while taking service on a DTOU can maximize customer benefits, not every customer on a time-variant supply rate is necessarily better off also taking service on the DTOU, and vice versa. ICEA/RESA IB at 20-22; AG IB at 7-11. CUB/EDF explain because individual customers’ circumstances vary, some customers’ benefits from taking service on either a time-variant supply rate or a DTOU would actually be reduced by being forced to also take service on both.

For example, a Rate BES customer who previously made no effort to shift their usage away from peak hours, as they had no incentive to do so, may have had high usage during system peak hours the previous year. Thus, their past behavior would lock them into a high Capacity Charge were they to take service on ComEd’s Rate BESH. Absent a better-suited ARES time-variant supply rate offering that currently does not exist becoming available and known to the customer by then, this customer would be unable to take service on the DTOU rate without also taking on a large monthly fixed Capacity Charge under Rate BESH that would reduce, perhaps even entirely offset, the benefits that customer can reasonably expect to otherwise achieve. For this customer to save, they would have to shift load during the year’s system peak hours without any compensation at the time, avoid guessing wrong about when those hours are, and then sign up for both the DTOU and Rate BESH the following year.

Without automatic pairing, that same customer would be able to take service on the DTOU, be rewarded for shifting load that then produces system benefits for others, and only sign up for Rate BESH if and when it makes sense for that customer to do so. CUB/EDF argue this approach far better conforms to any coherent definition of the word “flexible” and allows the customer to unlock benefits that otherwise would not be available to them.

Finally, ComEd attempts to minimize the risk impact of automatically pairing the DTOU rate with any time-variant supply rate by noting, under the Company’s proposed design, the difference between the highest DTOU hourly price and Rate BES would be “only” roughly 5 cents per kWh. _Id._ at 53. CUB/EDF state this attempt to downplay the implications of forced pairing of time-variant rates ring hollow. CUB/EDF contend a nearly 5-cent per kWh price difference is actually quite significant. The current price to compare for ComEd, which combines supply and transmission, is 6.47 cents per kWh. PLUGIN ILL., Understanding the Price to Compare, [ComEd Price to Compare](https://plugin.illinois.gov/understanding-the-price-to-compare/price-to-compare-comed.html) (last accessed Nov. 5, 2024). CUB/EDF explain adding almost 5 cents per kWh via the DTOU would virtually eliminate any potential room for net savings from a time-variant supply rate. Moreover, CUB/EDF propose to apply the highest peak DTOU price only during the summer months, which would make the difference between this highest peak DTOU price and Rate BES larger. _See_ CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 16-17. CUB/EDF conclude a customer cannot safely ignore the cost impacts of the DTOU rate, as ComEd suggests.

For these reasons, CUB/EDF urge the Commission to reject ComEd’s proposal to automatically pair the DTOU and time-variant supply rates together and instead allow customers to decide their choice of rates for themselves.

##### e. ICEA/RESA’s Position

ICEA/RESA register opposition to ComEd’s proposed requirement that any customer taking a time-of-use supply option must take ComEd’s proposed optional time-of-use delivery rate. ICEA/RESA instead urge the Commission to allow any residential or small commercial customer to take or refuse the proposed DTOU rate.

According to ICEA/RESA, the primary reason that the Commission should allow all residential or small commercials to take or refuse ComEd’s proposed DTOU rate is preserving and enhancing customer choice. ICEA/RESA highlight that Mr. Wright explained that the Act empowers customers to make their own energy choices through voluntary real-time pricing utility supply rates for residential customers and allowing ARES to receive authorization to access critical customer interval data. _See_ ICEA-RESA Ex. 3.0 at 6. According to ICEA/RESA, not all customers will benefit from all TOU options because of each customer’s different risk tolerance and ability to shift load according to the specific design of any time-variant rate. _See id._ at 6. ICEA/RESA argue that some customers may seek to maximize their upside, but others may, despite the potential upside, not wish to expose themselves to the risk of both an STOU and DTOU rate. _See id._ at 6.

According to ICEA/RESA, there are also many reasons that even customers with a higher risk appetite that is reflected in their STOU option may not want ComEd’s DTOU option: the supply option and ComEd’s delivery rate may have inconsistent or conflicting incentives. ICEA/RESA highlight that according to Mr. Wright, a particular time-variant supply option may incentivize customers to shift or reduce load in a way that is not

advantageous under ComEd’s proposed delivery time-of-use tariff. _See id._ at 6; ICEA/RESA Ex. 6.0 at 7; _see also_ ICEA/RESA Ex. 3.01. ICEA/RESA continue that Mr. Wright identified the specific example of a free nights and weekends supply product, which would incentivize very different behavior than ComEd’s three-tiered time-of-use delivery rate. _See_ ICEA-RESA Ex. 6.0 at 7. ICEA/RESA urge that the Commission should not artificially limit a customer’s ability to choose their supply and delivery options by requiring that a time-of-use supply rate must be paired with ComEd’s DTOU and vice versa.

ICEA/RESA highlight that CUB/EDF summarized several compelling reasons for the Commission to not link ComEd’s DTOU rates with necessarily taking a supply time-of-use ComEd rate or ARES product:

> Making the DTOU available as its own separate rate offering is necessary to (1) conform with the clear intent of the Commission’s order approving the Company’s Beneficial Electrification Plan, (2) ensure the DTOU is an available and appealing option for ComEd customers who do not necessarily wish to also enroll in Rate BESH, and (3) avoid undue discrimination against ARES and their customers.

CUB/EDF IB at 13. According to ICEA/RESA, CUB-EDF also agree that requiring a customer to take an STOU rate or ARES product with delivery time-of-use is confusing to customers. _See id._ at 14. ICEA/RESA note that while they disagree that the solution is Commission approval of a new ComEd STOU rate (which is prohibited under Section 16-103(e) for reasons addressed in Section VI.F.1 infra), ICEA and RESA fully agree with CUB/EDF’s reasoning about why a required pairing is not appropriate.

ICEA/RESA further highlight that CUB/EDF oppose pairing because of the impact it will have on customers’ time-of-use ARES supply choices. _See_ CUB/EDF IB at 17-18. According to ICEA/RESA, CUB/EDF in particular highlights the switching restrictions ComEd proposed under its DTOU rates. _See id._ at 18. ICEA/RESA agree that ComEd’s proposed switching restrictions would further inhibit retail competition and negatively impact customer time-of-use choices.

According to ICEA-RESA, the AG, like ICEA and RESA, notes the disconnect between price incentives on ComEd’s DTOU rates and an STOU rate, in their case ComEd’s Rate BESH. ICEA-RESA note the AG’s explanation that: “This situation [pairing ComEd’s Rate BESH with ComEd’s delivery time-of-use rate] creates a clear catch-22 for the consumer: on a sunny afternoon with abundant solar energy available, the same electricity would be both cheaper (from the RRTP supply perspective) and more expensive (from the DTOU perspective).” AG IB at 8. ICEA/RESA agree, noting this is one example from the wide range of potentially available time-of-use supply products and services that ARES may offer.

On reply, ICEA/RESA note that unlike CUB/EDF and the AG, Staff and ComEd support requiring a customer selecting the DTOU rate to take an STOU rate or ARES product and vice versa. According to ICEA/RESA, ComEd provided little support for its position in briefing, relying solely on the unsupported contention from Ms. Kremer that customers on ComEd’s DTOU rate will modify usage patterns to optimize savings under both ComEd’s DTOU rate and the customer’s selected STOU option. _See_ ComEd IB at 52-53. ICEA/RESA reiterate that ComEd is ignoring the misalignment of incentives between ComEd’s DTOU rate and available or potential STOU options. _See_ supra at VI.E.2 (agreeing with AG argument).

Similarly, ICEA/RESA continue, Staff provided little basis for its position, simply restating Ms. Kremer’s positions and argument that required pairing is acceptable because “the customers will retain the ability to choose their electric supplier and thus their supply rate.” Staff IB at 33. ICEA/RESA note that they agree that a customer will be able to choose an ARES time-of-use supply product under ComEd’s proposal— subject of course to the switching limits CUB/EDF raised that ICEA and RESA agree with supra—nevertheless the ability to choose an ARES is different from the ability for the customer to choose their product. ICEA-RESA point out that once the technical changes for Rider Interval Meter Usage Data (“IMUD”) as ordered in Docket No. 23-0773 are in place, ICEA witness Mr. Wright expects more ARES TOU options to be available. _See_ ICEA/RESA Ex. 3.0 at 3. However, ICEA/RESA respond, that does little to relieve the misalignment between ComEd’s proposed DTOU rate and the myriad possible STOU rates as highlighted by ICEA and RESA and the AG and described in response to ComEd’s similar argument. ICEA/RESA urge that the Commission should thus not adopt Staff’s position.

ICEA/RESA note that ComEd raised concerns that programming the ability for a customer to elect a time-variant supply with the current delivery rate or the time-variant delivery rate with a fixed-rate supply option would be challenging. _See_ ComEd Ex. 3.0 at 14. ICEA/RESA respond that given that ComEd’s challenges with their Customer Care & Billing (“CC&B”) system upgrades have directly and seriously impacted all ARES serving customers in the ComEd service territory since February 2024, ICEA and RESA do appreciate ComEd’s candor about programming challenges. However, ICEA/RESA counter that Mr. Wright noted that ComEd will be upgrading its systems twice (once for Rider IMUD as ordered in Docket No. 23-0773 and again for the proposed DTOU rate). _See_ ICEA/RESA Ex. 3.0 at 8. ICEA/RESA note that Mr. Wright further warned that he is “highly concerned that ComEd will experience similar defects and failures in fully executing critical systems that ARES need to serve their customers (all customers, not just customers on time-variant products and services).” _Id._ at 8. On the other hand, ICEA/RESA continue, ComEd admitted in discovery that it does not know whether its proposed DTOU rate will have conflicting or inconsistent incentives as any particular ARES TOU option. _See_ ICEA/RESA Ex. 3.01.

According to ICEA/RESA, the record has clearly established that ComEd’s justification that customers want to maximize their load shifting potential (see ComEd Ex. 3.0 at 13-14) is mere speculation. ComEd admitted in discovery that ComEd “does not have the information to estimate the number of customers that would take an additional step to elect the DTOU in combination with a ComEd or RES time-variant supply rate.” ICEA/RESA 3.01. ICEA/RESA contend that ComEd also does not know whether any customers would actually benefit from requiring its time-variant delivery rate to solely and exclusively be paired with STOU options. _See id._ In other words, ICEA/RESA note, ComEd is attempting to force customers into a choice that ComEd admits it has no idea whether customers actually want or would benefit from.

ICEA/RESA note that while ComEd does highlight the very real risks posed to customers and ARES from ComEd’s billing system upgrades, the proper remedy is greater oversight and accountability rather than artificially restricting customer choice. ICEA/RESA argue that none of ComEd’s other voluntary options require a specific or different delivery rate structure—ComEd’s proposed DTOU rate would be the outlier. _See_ ICEA/RESA Ex. 6.0 at 5-6. ICEA/RESA conclude that ComEd provided no convincing justifications for why its DTOU rate should be treated differently than any other of its voluntary programs.

##### f. ELPC/VS/UCS’ Position

ComEd proposes to offer only a TOU delivery rate, and its plan requires that customers must combine its DTOU rate with an STOU rate. Given that ComEd offers no supply rate, customers must sign up for an STOU rate from a competitive supplier to get the TOU delivery rate. In support of this, ComEd argues:

> If a customer would like to find a rate that rewards shifting their energy usage, it would be counterintuitive for ComEd to offer a non-time variant distribution option to go with the time-variant supply election the customer just made. The most straightforward and customer-friendly offering for a time-variant rate is one where our customers can maximize the effects of any actions they will take to shift load. Pairing time-time variant supply rates with a time-variant distribution rate accomplishes this goal.

ComEd Ex. 3.0 at 13. ELPC/VS/UCS agree with Kremer’s assessment. However, “the most straight forward customer friendly offering” is for ComEd to offer customers an STOU and DTOU as a bundled rate. ComEd presents no evidence that customers will find it appealing to shop for TOU rates under the split framework it proposes.

CUB/EDF witness McCann testifies extensively in support of ComEd aligning its supply and distribution time variant rate tariffs. CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 5. He notes, “[g]iven that the utility is arguing that matching supply and distribution time variant rates is required for customers to have simpler ‘customer friendly’ choices, the lack of such a proposal is surprising and inconsistent with the utility’s reasoning.” _Id._ at 7. ELPC/VS/UCS agree with this analysis.

ELPC/VS/UCS highlight that Ms. Kremer testifies that she does not know how if any competitive suppliers in its territory currently offer an STOU rate, and ComEd has no estimate of how many suppliers will offer an STOU rate in 2026. Tr. at 95. Nor does ComEd have any projections for how many customers will sign up for TOU rates. Tr. at 91. Most importantly, ComEd has no requirement that supplier TOU periods match up with ComEd’s four TOU periods. Ms. Kremer acknowledges that the TOU offerings could have eight or more time periods for customers to remember depending on the supplier offerings. “They could potentially have many more than eight time periods, in the example that we provide with the residential time of -- sorry, Residential Real-Time Pricing program, it's a different price in every hour.” Tr. at 96. According to Ms. Kremer, “[a] fully ‘unified’ price signal is not necessarily desirable, because staggered periods allow for more flexibility in customer behavior.” ComEd Ex. 11.0 at 8. But she also could not answer a question about how she thinks having so many time periods may affect the number of customers who sign up for TOU. Tr. at 97. ELPC/VS/UCS strongly agree with CUB/EDF witness McCann, who emphasizes that ComEd should offer a bundled TOU rate to make it easy for customers to understand and achieve wide adoption. CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 7.

For any program, the Commission should look at the cost-benefit analysis. ComEd’s direct testimony provides no estimate for the cost of this program, which raises concerns about the cost-benefit analysis that the utility should do. Then on cross, Ms. Kremer estimates the cost at $5 to 10 million dollars. Nowhere does ComEd look at how those costs compare to the benefits.

##### g. Commission Analysis and Conclusion

The Commission will not require ComEd’s DTOU to be mandatory if a customer elects to participate in a time-variant supply rate. Instead, the Commission agrees with the AG and CUB/EDF and approves ComEd’s DTOU as a separate rate offering available on an opt-in basis. In arriving at this conclusion, the Commission agrees with the AG that ComEd’s proposal would add unnecessary complexity and competing incentives to the DTOU and make it harder for customers to understand, assess, and take advantage of the potential benefits of these programs. The Commission also emphasizes the risk to low-income customers as highlighted by the AG and illustrated by ComEd’s own supply RTOUPP. The Commission also agrees with CUB/EDF that if the DTOU was paired with Rate BESH, the capacity charge would add complexity and customer risk, while diminishing the effectiveness of the price signal that the DTOU is intended to transmit.

The Commission notes that ICEA/RESA also joins CUB/EDF’s reasoning in opposing the pairing and emphasizes the Commission’s priorities in preserving and enhancing customer choice. CUB/EDF suggest that ComEd offer its own STOU. The AG supports this proposal along with ELPC/VS/UCS, which do not oppose a pairing of a DTOU and STOU so long as it is proposed by ComEd. The Commission addresses the STOU proposal below in Section F.4.

#### 4. Revenue Requirement Allocation to DTOU Customers

##### a. ComEd’s Position

ComEd states neither CUB/EDF’s proposal nor Staff’s proposal for tracking the impact of DTOU rate adoption and providing those benefits back to customers is appropriate for consideration. ComEd states that it cannot track any deferred distribution system investments as a result of DTOU load shifting, nor pass on any itemized or calculated benefits to the customers enrolled in the DTOU rate. _See_ ComEd Ex. 9.0 REV. at 20-21.

ComEd asserts that its DTOU rate already provides immediate, tangible benefits to customers by allowing them to shift load to a DFC below what they would be charged on the flat delivery rate, thus saving money on their bill. _Id._ It also benefits them in the future, to the extent that ComEd can avoid investments as a result of DTOU load-shifting and reduce its revenue requirement. _Id._ Therefore, the benefits of DTOU rate participation are already inherently and automatically passed through to customers and no additional benefits need be returned. _Id._

First, ComEd argues that it does not have the ability to capture data to prove out the assumption that DTOU customers will identifiably shift load because of the DTOU rate. It is not possible to accurately isolate deferrals of investment caused by a customer’s load shifting under the DTOU rate because many factors could influence a change in a particular customer’s usage. _Id._ at 21. ComEd proposes that its DTOU rate be paired with time-variant supply rates, whether from ComEd or a RES so is not possible to isolate whether a customer’s subsequent load shifting was driven by the DTOU rate or by their time-variant supply rate, let alone apportion benefits achieved between the two. _Id._ at 21-22.

In addition, ComEd argues, there would be administrative and operational costs associated with attempting to track the supposed benefits of DTOU rate participation, and those costs would be borne by all customers. ComEd Ex. 9.0 REV. at 20-21. Existing, approved ratemaking structures do not provide for the pass-back of unspecified future grid savings to individual customers. _Id._ at 21.

ComEd notes that proposals also assume customers will shift load on the DTOU rate, which they may not. ComEd Ex. 9.0 REV. at 21. Furthermore, ComEd argues, Staff’s and CUB/EDF’s proposals suggest that ComEd return “additional” measured benefits to DTOU rate customers, irrespective of whether any of those customers is individually shifting load. _See_ CUB/EDF Ex. 2.0 at 23; Staff Ex. 6.0 at 14. Therefore, because some DTOU rate customers make decisions to shift load, all DTOU rate customers would receive a pass-back of benefits that are paid for by other ComEd customers—including other customers on the DTOU rate.

Finally, ComEd argues that these supplemental benefits would blunt the clear price signals inherent to TOU rate design. _See_ ComEd Ex. 6.0 at 22 (explaining the DTOU rate’s price signals and the negative impact of additional compensation). Ultimately, these proposals do not advance the “fairness” advocated for by their proponents and, even if it were possible to implement them, these proposals fundamentally undermine the design of the DTOU rate. As ComEd explained, all benefits from participation are already automatically and directly provided to DTOU rate customers through reduced charges on their bill, so additional credit would result in double counting benefits for those DTOU rate customers.

ComEd states in its RBOE that it does not object to Staff’s proposed next steps for additional discussion and data collection on this issue.

##### b. Staff’s Position

Staff recommends that the Company should use actual usage data for customers who enroll in the DTOU rate, after the fact, to calculate any impacts. The Company should determine the avoided or deferred investment due to the change in customer behavior within the year and pass on such benefits to the customers that adopted the DTOU. Using the actual benefits realized will help reduce the issue of over or under forecast and provide customers with the actual benefits that their adoption of DTOU provides them. Staff Ex. 6.0 at 14.

CUB/EDF recommend that projected system cost savings from reduced peak period use should be deducted when calculating the benchmark for allocating revenue requirements for STOU and DTOU schedules. CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 3. Staff disagrees with CUB/EDF because the proposed benefits are contingent on customers’ decisions to opt into a DTOU and any resulting change in electricity usage and it is not clear the forecast accurately predicts customer adoption rates or behavior change. Staff Ex. 6.0 at 14. Instead, ComEd should use actual data on customer adoption rates and behavior changes to determine the benefits of DTOU adoption. _Id._ The Company does not yet have such data available. ComEd Ex. 4.0 at 17. The lack of actual data may cause potential issues with the forecast which can make it difficult to tell if the Company forecast of benefits of the DTOU is exaggerated. While Staff does not support CUB/EDF’s methodology to calculate savings, Staff agrees that customers should benefit. Staff recommends that, within a year of implementing a DTOU, ComEd determine the amount of avoided or deferred investments attributable to adoption of a DTOU and pass those savings on to customers. Staff Ex. 6.0 at 14.

ComEd disagrees with both Staff and CUB/EDF, saying the calculations are unnecessary. ComEd Ex. 9.0 REV. at 20. Staff disagrees with the Company and recommends that the Company should use actual data to determine the avoided or deferred investment due to the change in customer behavior both because this will help reduce the issue of over or under forecast and because it will ensure customers realize the actual benefits associated with adopting a DTOU rate. Staff Ex. 6.0 at 14.

As requested by the Proposed Order, on exceptions Staff stated that future discussion and data are necessary prior to implementation and provided suggestions on how to achieve those objectives.

##### c. CUB/EDF’s Position

As Dr. McCann testified, “[t]he point of adopting TOU rates is to create system wide savings for all customers,” yet in allocating revenues between DTOU customers and other customers, ComEd “assumes there are no such savings.” CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 20. ComEd bases this assumption on a misapplication of the requirement that the Company’s rate design be “revenue neutral.” CUB Ex. 2.0 at 5. As Dr. McCann explained, “Revenue neutrality, which is required specifically of distribution rates, is intended to avoid creating windfall profits for the utility and to minimize cost shifting among customers.” _Id._ at 5. CUB/EDF explain the principle of revenue neutrality prevents ComEd from knowingly deviating from its revenue requirement via rate design, but it does not preclude the Company from allocating revenues differently to subsets of its customers that take service on a different tariff. In fact, CUB/EDF argue, intentional allocation of revenues between customers who take service under different terms is a core purpose of a rate design investigation.

CUB/EDF contend the Company calculates the DTOU’s hourly period rates as if customers taking service on the DTOU will ignore the time-variance of these prices and behave the same, causing the same costs, as a flat-rate customer would. CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 20-21. Dr. McCann summarized his concerns about this approach as follows:

> the sole purpose of implementing TOU rates is reduce overall utility costs by lowering investment and power purchase requirements. To be revenue neutral requires that ComEd recover distribution revenues equal to its authorized distribution revenue requirement. To achieve this, any utility that serves customers on a TOU rate must anticipate the likely change in customers’ usage induced by taking service on a TOU rate and assign billing determinants accordingly in determining TOU and non-TOU customers’ appropriate contributions to overall revenues. If ComEd simply assumes the cost of service will be identical for these customers regardless of which rate they are on, then ComEd is assuming customer behavior will be unchanged, that utility revenue requirements will be unchanged, and that state policy to reduce energy costs will fail. Also ComEd would consistently overcharge TOU customers, and as a result, overcollect revenues.

CUB/EDF Ex. 2.0 at 5.

To remedy these flaws, CUB/EDF propose that the Commission order ComEd to determine TOU and non-TOU customers’ respective distribution revenue responsibilities to reflect the change in relative costs of service for the two groups, based on reasonable assumptions regarding DTOU customers’ load patterns. _Id._ at 6. One resource that can inform such expectations is the results of ComEd’s RTOUPP program. CUB-EDF Ex. 2.0 at 70-79. This pilot featured a TOU rate designed similarly to ComEd’s proposed DTOU, and even more similarly to CUB/EDF’s proposed DTOU, and documented significant load-shifting by participating customers. _See_ CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 11.

CUB/EDF further recommend the Company and the Commission revisit assumptions about DTOU customers’ load patterns and their resulting contribution to cost of service in subsequent rate design investigations, to reflect updated data. _Id._ at 6. In doing so, Dr. McCann testified, the Commission “can create a virtuous cycle where customers are attracted to the rate schedule which then creates more savings which are then passed on to those customers.” _Id._ at 7.

CUB/EDF argue the Commission should reject all of ComEd’s attempts to defend the Company’s refusal to assume load-shifting when allocating ComEd’s revenue requirement between DTOU and flat distribution rate customers. First, CUB/EDF contend ComEd misapplies the concept of revenue neutrality to suggest CUB/EDF’s proposal is out of scope. ComEd IB at 53. ComEd’s case in chief on this point consists of two sentences, reproduced here in their entirety: “CUB-EDF’s first proposal for ComEd to deduct projected system cost savings due to DTOU-motivated reduced peak period use from its distribution revenue requirement amount must be rejected. This is not a revenue neutral proposal and is thus inappropriate to suggest in this proceeding.” _Id._ at 53 (citations omitted). CUB/EDF note the Company made no attempt to define revenue neutrality. CUB/EDF addressed this matter in the IB:

> As [Dr.] McCann explained, “Revenue neutrality, which is required specifically of distribution rates, is intended to avoid creating windfall profits for the utility and to minimize cost shifting among customers.” [CUB Ex. 2.0] at 5:90-92. The principle of revenue neutrality prevents ComEd from knowingly deviating from its revenue requirement via rate design, but it does not preclude the Company from allocating revenues differently to subsets of its customers that take service on a different tariff. In fact, intentional allocation of revenues between customers who take service under different terms is a core purpose of a rate design investigation.

CUB/EDF IB at 20. CUB/EDF contend the Company’s IB offers no substantive arguments in support of the outcome ComEd desires. CUB/EDF implore the Commission to reject the Company’s request to dismiss CUB/EDF’s proposal for lack of basis.

Second, ComEd claims “it is not possible to match deferred investments to DTOU rate adoption.” ComEd IB at 54. The Company bases its position on the insistence of ComEd witness Perkins that the Company cannot verify that “DTOU rate customers will actually shift load in a detectable way solely because of the DTOU rate.” _Id._ (citing ComEd Ex. 9.0 REV. at 20, 21). CUB/EDF explain this premise is disproven by ComEd’s own reports detailing the results of its years-long TOU rate pilot (RTOUPP) and Rate BESH and measuring load-shifting by participants. CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 10-11; CUB/EDF Ex. 2.0 at 4-5. CUB/EDF assert the point of conducting RTOUPP, a 4-year pilot program, in particular was to collect this type of data and use it to inform future rate design. _See_ RTOUPP Order at 38-41. CUB/EDF consider it puzzling ComEd refuses to utilize the fruits of its own labor to reasonably allocate revenue responsibilities between rate classes in this rate design investigation.

Third, ComEd argues “DTOU customers will already receive all benefits generated by their load-shifting” without the Company intentionally reallocating revenue responsibility between DTOU and non-DTOU customers based on expected load-shifting. ComEd IB at 54-56. While CUB/EDF agrees that DTOU customers would stand to benefit from load-shifting even under ComEd’s proposal, Dr. McCann explained DTOU customers as a group would overpay:

> If ComEd simply assumes the cost of service will be identical for these customers regardless of which rate they are on, then ComEd is assuming customer behavior will be unchanged, that utility revenue requirements will be unchanged, and that state policy to reduce energy costs will fail. Also, ComEd would consistently overcharge TOU customers, and as a result, overcollect revenues.

CUB/EDF Ex. 2.0 at 5.

The Company then rehashes its claim that rate design cannot reasonably assume DTOU customers will shift load in response to price signals. ComEd IB at 55. In response, CUB/EDF point again to ComEd’s own reports documenting the Company’s extensive experience with time-variant rate customers responding to such price signals by shifting load. CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 10-11; CUB/EDF Ex. 2.0 at 4-5. ComEd further argues that it would be unfair to allocate DTOU rate customers’ collective revenue responsibility based on the group’s aggregated load shape. ComEd IB at 55-56. CUB/EDF express disbelief that the Company can possibly believe such a practice is inherently inappropriate, seeing as allocating revenues to rate classes based on assumptions about the total cost to serve each of those rate classes is the fundamental basis for much of ComEd’s entire approach to rate design in this proceeding. CUB-EDF point out each customer does not get their own personalized revenue allocation either way. CUB/EDF argue rate design necessarily requires the application of informed assumptions regarding the cost of service and magnitude (and, for time-variant rates, timing) of billing determinants for each rate class as a whole, which then provide the foundation for designing the pricing formula that applies the same to customers within each respective rate class. CUB/EDF maintain that none of these considerations are unique to a DTOU rate. CUB/EDF and Staff ask that ComEd not exempt the DTOU rate from the cost-causation principle that generally applies to rate design.

CUB/EDF suggest on exceptions that RTOUPP data could be used to implement these benefits, while the parties wait to obtain actual data.

##### d. Commission Analysis and Conclusion

The Commission agrees with Staff and CUB/EDF that customers should realize the actual benefits associated with adopting a DTOU rate. To calculate those benefits, the Commission directs the Company to use actual data to determine the avoided or deferred investment due to the change in customer behavior as suggested by Staff. Therefore, the Commission directs the Company to take the following steps as recommended by Staff in an effort to determine the amount of avoided or deferred investments attributable to adoption of a DTOU.

First, the Commission directs ComEd to track the amount of avoided or deferred investments attributable to adoption of a DTOU. Second, the Commission directs ComEd to coordinate with Staff, CUB/EDF, and any additional stakeholders that notify the Company of an interest to coordinate on this issue to select methodologies to be used to identify investments that are avoided or deferred due to DTOU adoption, including quantifying changes in customer energy usage patterns to determine peak demand reduction, prior to the implementation of the DTOU rate in January 2026. Third, the Commission directs ComEd to calculate savings attributable to the DTOU after implementation of the DTOU rate starting no later than January 2026. Fourth, the Commission directs ComEd to apply the selected methodologies to track data on deferred and avoided investments and report out on the progress related to this issue, including any identified savings, in its annual Grid Plan report so that the information may be available to Staff and interested stakeholders. Whether there are tangible savings and how those savings are passed on to ratepayers should be discussed among the parties. The Commission notes that ComEd does not object to this approach. The Commission declines to utilize RTOUPP data in the interim as suggested by CUB/EDF.

#### 6. Required Tariff Revisions

##### a. ComEd’s Position

ComEd proposes to implement its DTOU rate proposal through modifications to its existing rates. ComEd Exs. 3.01 and 6.01; _see also_ ComEd Exs. 3.02 and 6.02. ComEd states that this proposal is consistent with how distribution rates are implemented in ComEd’s ratebook, i.e., not as separate tariffs, but as DFCs in other rates. ComEd Ex. 3.0 at 10 (explaining that a customer’s delivery class determines the DFC applicable to the customer’s Delivery section of their bill). ComEd notes that Staff concurs with ComEd that the DTOU rate does not need to be a separate tariff. Staff Ex. 4.0 at 11.

##### b. Staff’s Position

ICEA/RESA recommends ComEd offer a new DTOU, through a separate tariff, rather than make changes to Rate BESH and Rate RDS to include that new rate. ICEA/RESA Ex. 3.0 at 8. Staff recommends the Commission reject ICEA/RESA’s proposal. ICEA/RESA witness Wright expressed concerns that residential customers participating in Rider DG Rebate for storage facilities are currently prohibited from taking ARES supply service pursuant to Section 16-107.6(c)(2). _Id._ ComEd responded to this issue by making revisions to Rider DG Rebate; Staff recommends the Commission approve the revised tariff changes to Rider DG as they resolve ICEA-RESA's concern. ComEd Exs. 6.01 and 6.02. ICEA/RESA witness Wright also claimed that ComEd’s proposed tariff changes would cause a divergence in Rate BESH and Rate RDS with future changes to either tariff. _Id._ at 9. ComEd testified these proposed changes would not intentionally cause a divergence and any changes to Rate BESH and Rate RDS would have to be filed with the Commission, giving parties the opportunity to review the proposed modifications and investigate formally if it decided that was appropriate. ComEd Ex. 6.0 at 6. Staff agrees.

##### c. CUB/EDF’s Position

CUB/EDF propose that ComEd file the DTOU as its own separate tariff rather than as a revision to Rate BESH.

##### d. ICEA/RESA’s Position

#### 7. Rider EVEN – Electric Vehicle Elective Notification

##### a. ComEd’s Position

ComEd proposes Rider EVEN, which provides for a $2 per month credit for a total of 24 months, so long as the customer is enrolled on a time-variant supply rate paired with the DTOU rate and notifies ComEd that they own an EV. ComEd Ex. 3.0 at 44-45; ComEd Ex. 3.05. ComEd states that the purpose of this credit is to provide an incentive for EV owners to adopt time-variant rates, as instructed, and ComEd’s proposal does that. BE Plan 1 Order at 258.

CUB/EDF proposed an incentive level of either $463 per year or $9 per month for an indefinite period. CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 22. ComEd explains that this is approximately 18 times greater than ComEd’s proposal and CUB/EDF has not justified such a substantial increase to the credit. ComEd Ex. 6.0 at 22-23. ComEd urges the Commission to reject this unnecessary increase to the Rider EVEN credit and approve its proposal of $2 per month for 24 months.

##### b. Staff’s Position

Staff supports ComEd’s proposed Rider EVEN, which provides a $2 per month credit for up to 24 monthly billing periods to customers who sign up for a supply rate paired with the DTOU rate and identify themselves as an EV owner. ComEd Ex. 3.0 at 44-45. Rider EVEN provides an additional $2 per month for an additional EV that is registered at the same address while the customer is on a supply rate paired with the DTOU rate. _Id._ Customers could receive an additional 24-monthly billing period credits if they move to a new residence and remain in an eligible supply rate paired with the DTOU rate. ComEd Ex. 3.0 at 45. The only obligation for the customer to continue to receive the rebate provided under Rider EVEN is to remain on the DTOU rate. Customers can voluntarily leave the DTOU rate without penalty and simply forego any remaining monthly credits. ComEd Ex. 3.0 at 22.

Staff recommends the Commission reject CUB/EDF’s proposed alternative enrollment incentive payment for EV owners in Rider EVEN, which would be based on the project resource savings from the customer’s participation and offer either a payment of $463 a year for two years or a monthly payment of $9 a month for as long as the customer is enrolled in Rider EVEN. CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 22. ComEd witness Kremer opposed this program because Rider EVEN is not intended to offset the purchase of an EV nor to provide compensation based upon the “system value.” ComEd Ex. 6.0 at 21. Staff agrees with ComEd. Staff Ex. 4.0 at 8.

##### c. CUB/EDF’s Position

In the Commission’s BE Plan 1 Order, the Commission ordered the Company to include in its proposal “an incentive for EV owners (i.e., monthly/annual bill credits) to go on the [DTOU] rate.” BE Plan 1 Order at 162-163. In response, ComEd proposes in this docket Rider EVEN, which provides a $2 bill credit each month ($4 for a 2-EV household), for a maximum of 24 months, to EV owners who are enrolled in the DTOU. ComEd Ex. 3.0 at 44-45. CUB/EDF argue ComEd’s Rider EVEN falls short of meeting the Commission’s mandate, as the amount of the bill credit is so small as to be unlikely to induce additional enrollment. CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 21. Dr. McCann testified an EV owner already will have spent thousands of dollars more than the cost of an equivalent internal combustion engine vehicle and received up to $11,500 in state and federal incentives. _Id._ at 21. Dr. McCann noted they also likely will have paid a substantial sum to install a home charger and received a ComEd rebate of up to $3,750, which also requires time-variant rate enrollment. _Id._ at 21. For a customer who already has gone through this series of hefty transactions, Dr. McCann determined a $2 per month bill credit, capped at a total of $48 over 2 years, is unlikely to sway any decisions. _Id._ at 21. CUB/EDF conclude the record does not demonstrate that allocating $2 a month in bill credits to customers adds any system benefit, let alone enough to justify the administrative costs. _Id._ at 22.

CUB/EDF contend ComEd provides no supporting analysis for this apparently arbitrary $2 amount. _Id._ at 21-22. Staff witness Martin voiced support for the Company’s approach but admitted in testimony that he had performed no research or analysis to support that conclusion. Staff Ex. 4.0 at 8; CUB/EDF Cross Ex. 8.0. In contrast, CUB/EDF witness McCann presented what CUB/EDF consider the only approach supported by empirical evidence. CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 22.

CUB/EDF recommend that the Commission adopt an incentive closer to Dr. McCann’s proposed system benefit-based incentive of $463 per year for 2 years or $9 per month indefinitely. _Id._ at 365-370. To determine this valuation, Dr. McCann cited the findings of ComEd’s report on the results of the RTOUPP, which found participant EV charging increased 1.03 kilowatts during the overnight “Off-Peak” period and attributed this load-shifting to the TOU rate design. _Id._ at 22. Accordingly, assuming the Super Peak reduction is equivalent to the Off-Peak increase, Dr. McCann proposed that the incentive payment can be based on compensating the value of a 1-kilowatt reduction in required capacity per PJM Interconnection’s reliability capacity benchmark (i.e., the cost of a new combustion turbine). _Id._ at 22.

Dr. McCann noted the ComEd pilot results on which he based this calculation are consistent with other empirical studies of TOU rates across several jurisdictions. CUB-EDF Ex. 2.0 at 19-20. Moreover, he explained his use of a combustion turbine resource as a proxy for reducing peak demand conformed with industry standards. _Id._ at 20. In fact, it matches PJM’s valuation of battery storage as a capacity resource, thereby recognizing that an EV is battery storage on wheels. _Id._

CUB/EDF consider ComEd witness Kremer’s response to Dr. McCann a strawman argument. Ms. Kremer claimed Dr. McCann’s proposed bill credits would be overly costly to apply to one million EVs. _Id._ at 21. There are less than 100,000 EVs across all of Illinois, ComEd serves only 58.6% of Illinois households, and thus, CUB/EDF conclude, there is no credible basis for basing a program cost estimate on the assumption that one million ComEd customers would receive Rider EVEN bill credits at any time in the foreseeable future. _Id._ at 21-22. Applying Ms. Kremer’s calculation method to what Dr. McCann considered a more accurate count of ComEd customers with EVs brings the estimated program cost to $216,000, less than half the $480,000 figure Ms. Kremer admitted she considered reasonable. _Id._ at 22.

For the foregoing reasons, CUB/EDF urge the Commission to adopt a Rider EVEN incentive amount more in line with Dr. McCann’s proposal. CUB/EDF assert theirs is the only EV incentive recommendation in the record that is supported by empirical evidence, and that the foregoing analysis demonstrates its reasonableness.

CUB/EDF note Staff expresses agreement with ComEd on Rider EVEN. Staff IB at 36-37. After summarizing the Company’s proposal, Staff offers one substantive argument in favor of its position. Staff quotes ComEd witness Kremer’s opinion that Rider EVEN is not intended to compensate for system value created or offset the cost of purchasing an EV. _Id._ at 36. To clarify, CUB/EDF never suggested that Dr. McCann’s proposed adjustment to Rider EVEN would “offset the purchase of an EV.” _See id._ Dr. McCann’s testimony referenced the cost to purchase an EV not to suggest that Rider EVEN should somehow seek to “offset” that cost, but rather merely to illustrate how trivial an amount ComEd’s proposed $2 per month bill credit is in that context. _See_ CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 21-22. This describes Ms. Kremer’s conception of her own proposal, but CUB/EDF note nothing in the Commission’s BE Plan 1 Order that gave rise to Rider EVEN suggested the Commission shares this view.

CUB/EDF consider ComEd’s proposal that Staff supports entirely arbitrary. CUB/EDF note neither the Company nor Staff provides any reason the amount should be $2 per month. Staff witness Martin admitted he conducted no analysis on the subject. CUB/EDF Cross Ex. 8.0. In contrast, CUB/EDF propose for there to be a relationship between the amount of the Rider EVEN incentive payment and the value contributed to the system when an EV owner elects to take service on a time-variant rate.

##### d. Commission Analysis and Conclusion

The Commission finds that the proposed incentive level of $2/month for 24 months contained in Rider EVEN is reasonable given the information available and need not be increased as suggested by CUB/EDF. Furthermore, it complies with the Commission’s directive in the BE Plan 1 Order. In its next rate design proceeding, the Commission directs ComEd to reexamine the $2/month incentive level with the most recently available data to ensure it is functioning appropriately.

### F. Supply Time of Use Rate

#### 1. Commission Authority

##### a. ComEd’s Position

ComEd did not propose an STOU rate in this proceeding. Therefore, ComEd argues that the Commission must reject any of CUB/EDF’s, ELPC/VS/UCS’, and the AG’s requests that ComEd be ordered to propose or adopt an STOU rate.

The Commission cannot require ComEd to adopt a new service that is not otherwise required by statute, and the Commission has consistently held this perspective across multiple proceedings. _See_ 220 ILCS 5/16-103(e); see also, e.g., BE Plan 1 Order at 258 (stating that Section 16-103(e) contains a plain limitation on the Commission’s authority to order new rates other than as required in 16-103(e)). Section 16-103(e) of the Act states that “[t]he Commission shall not require an electric utility to offer any tariffed service other than the services required by this Section.” ComEd has not offered an STOU in this case and argues that the Commission does not have the statutory authority to require ComEd to do so. 220 ILCS 5/16-103(e). Because CUB/EDF’s proposals for an STOU cannot be granted by the Commission, and because they are not relevant to the cost allocation of ComEd’s delivery service revenue requirement, the proposals must be rejected as out of scope for this proceeding.

##### b. Staff’s Position

CUB/EDF recommend the Commission direct ComEd to offer an STOU rate. CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 3. Staff strongly disagrees for three key reasons.

First, as discussed in detail in Section III.B., this docket is a revenue-neutral investigation into the design of ComEd’s delivery service rates. Supply service rates are, by definition, outside the scope of the docket.

Second, and perhaps most important, a ComEd STOU is contrary to the Commission’s Rules. Part 452, promulgated pursuant to Section 16-119A of the Act, Functional Separation, states in relevant part that “[a]n Integrated Distribution Company shall not promote, advertise or market with regard to the offering or provision of any retail electric supply service.” 83 Ill. Adm. Code 452.240(a). While Part 452.140 states a utility “may” petition for a waiver of certain provisions of Part 452, that provision is discretionary rather than mandatory and it is not clear the Commission can order ComEd to provide a supply rate should it not seek to do so. Moreover, the waiver requires a showing of six specific things by ComEd and a determination by the Commission that the waiver will not cause undue discrimination and will promote or create efficient competition. _See generally_ 83 Ill. Admin. Code 452.140. There is no record evidence adduced in this docket to make the necessary showing by ComEd or to support the necessary conclusions by the Commission. Moreover, the Commission’s BE Plan 1 Order explicitly states that it “is a creature of statute and is limited to its authority provided in the statute. Section 16-103(e) contains a plain limitation on the Commission’s authority to order new rates other than as required in Section 16-103(e).” BE Plan 1 Order at 258.

CUB/EDF argue that authority can be found in Section 16-103(a) which allows the Commission to “approve, allow or order modifications in the rates, terms and conditions… for continued bundled electric service.” CUB/EDF IB at 25. CUB/EDF correctly note, “No part of a statute should be rendered superfluous, and no provision should be interpreted so as to render another provision meaningless” (_id._ at 24 (citing City of Chi. v. Ill. Com. Comm’n, 233 Ill. App. 3d 992, 1001 (1st Dist. 1992))), and yet CUB/EDF do exactly that. Section 16-103(e) clearly states the Commission cannot require ComEd to “offer any competitive service.” 220 ILCS 5/16-103(e). ICEA/RESA and ComEd agree with Staff that the Commission cannot not approve an STOU in this docket. ICEA/RESA IB at 26; ComEd IB at 58.

Comparison to the RTOUPP is not persuasive. CUB/EDF suggest the primary difference between RTOUPP and its proposed STOU in this proceeding is the suggestion that the STOU be paired with a DTOU rate. CUB/EDF IB at 25. While this is a difference, the primary difference is that the STOU in this proceeding was proposed by CUB/EDF while the RTOUPP was proposed by ComEd. _See_ generally, RTOUPP Order. Importantly, the Commission limited participation in the pilot program precisely because of concerns with competition. _See, e.g.,_ RTOUPP Order at 13. While ComEd requested a waiver of Part 452 to implement RTOUPP, it is not clear that the Commission can direct the Company to offer an STOU. Because ComEd did not propose an STOU in this proceeding, the Commission lacks authority to consider CUB/EDF’s proposal. _See_ also, ICEA/RESA IB at 26; ComEd IB at 58.

The AG argues that “[t]he Commission has the authority to order ComEd to institute a companion supply-side TOU rate” based on the premise Section 16-103 does not apply to rates but only to services. AG IB at 11-12. Even assuming this is correct, it is irrelevant as STOU rates are strictly beyond the scope of this proceeding, as explained above because, even if ComEd wanted to pursue an STOU, it would still have to seek and be granted a waiver of Part 452 which it has not done. The AG’s arguments blatantly ignore the Commission’s rules and negative retail competition consequences and implications of a Commission decision on this matter at this time.

##### c. AG’s Position

The AG states that ComEd insinuates that an STOU rate would be a massive undertaking beyond the scope of this proceeding, in large part because it would involve the creation of a new “service” as opposed to merely a new “rate.” ComEd Ex. 9.0 REV. at 17; 18. ComEd witness Perkins stated “I am not aware that the Commission has ever taken the position that it can require ComEd to offer any competitive service or rate or any tariffed service or rate other than the services required by statute.” ComEd Ex. 9.0 REV. at 17 (citing 220 ILCS 5/16-103(e)). The AG asserts that Mr. Perkins mischaracterizes both the Act and Dr. McCann’s proposal.

The Act requires the Commission “to approve, allow or order modifications” to a utility’s rates and conditions of service, but prevents the Commission from requiring a utility to “to offer any tariffed service other than the services” or “require an electric utility to offer a competitive service.” 220 ILCS 5/16-103(a), (e). Importantly, this Section only limits the Commission’s authority to require the Company to provide additional “services;” it does not limit the Commission’s authority to modify a Company’s proposed “rates.” The AG asserts that Mr. Perkins tries to sidestep this distinction by claiming that “[s]aying that there is a difference between a service and a rate is like saying a dog is not a dog because it is on a leash.” ComEd Ex. 9.0 REV. at 17. However, an Illinois appellate court has found that rates and services are distinct when the issue at hand is what customers will pay for electricity, not whether the utility will continue to supply electricity to customers. Commonwealth Edison Co. v. Ill. Com. Comm'n, 398 Ill. App. 3d 510, 523-24 (2d Dist. 2009) (citing 220 ILCS 5/16-103(a) (finding that the Commission did not exceed its authority when it terminated a special rate for BOMA because “there is no protected interest in the continuation of a favorable utility rate” and eliminating a specific rate is not the same as eliminating a service)). That is precisely the issue here. Section 16-103(c) provides that “the electric utility shall... offer... bundled service options at rates which reflect recovery of all cost components for providing the service.” 220 ILCS 5/16-103(c). Dr. McCann does not claim that ComEd should provide additional services to its customers, but simply proposes that ComEd formulate a rate for existing electric supply services that permits customers to pay an STOU rate that is consistent with the proposed DTOU rate. CUB/EDF Ex. 2.0 at 3. Therefore, the AG concludes that Dr. McCann’s proposal is consistent with the statute and within the Commission’s authority. For these reasons, the AG respectfully requests that the Commission require ComEd to adopt CUB/EDF’s proposed STOU and allow it to be paired with its modified DTOU on a voluntary basis.

##### d. CUB/EDF’s Position

CUB/EDF contend the Commission has authority to approve CUB/EDF’s proposal for a residential and small commercial TOU rate for bundled electric service, which are the same bundled electric services as ComEd’s successful RTOUPP. CUB/EDF reason just as the Commission’s authority to approve RTOUPP was lawful, so is its authority to approve CUB/EDF’s TOU proposal.

Rate design and cost allocation are “uniquely a matter for the Commission’s discretion.” Coal. to Request Equitable Allocation of Costs Together v. Ill. Com. Comm’n, 28 N.E.3d 223, 241 (2d Dist. 2015). Here, CUB/EDF consider Sections 16-105.5 and 16-103 as governing the Commission’s rate design authority. In construing Sections 16-105.5 and 16-103, the Commission must ascertain and give effect to the intent of the legislature. Bus. & Pro. People for Pub. Int. v. Ill. Com. Comm’n, 146 Ill. 2d 175, 205-11 (1991) (citing People v. Beam, 74 Ill. 2d 240 (1979); MCI Telephone Cor. v. Ill. Com. Comm’n, 168 Ill. App. 3d 1008 (1988)). The Commission is bound by the plain meaning of unambiguous statutory language. _Id._ No part of a statute should be rendered superfluous, and no provision should be interpreted so as to render another provision meaningless. City of Chi. v. Ill. Com. Comm’n, 233 Ill. App. 3d 992, 1001 (1st Dist. 1992) (citing In re Application of County Collector, 132 Ill. 2d 72 (1989)).

Under Section 16-103(a), the Commission retains its authority to “approve, allow or order modifications in the rates, terms and conditions… for continued bundled electric service.” Utilities like ComEd are required to provide “bundled electric service” to all residential customers as a tariffed service “consistent with the bundled utility service provided by the electric utility on the effective date of this amendatory Act of 1997.” A “tariffed service” means services provided to customers, “but shall not include competitive services.” 220 ILCS 5/16-102. CUB/EDF conclude because “bundled electric services” must be provided as a “tariffed service,” they therefore cannot also be a “competitive service.” _Id._

Here, CUB/EDF propose that ComEd offer to residential and small commercial customers a TOU rate for bundled electric service. CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 2. The bundled electric service in CUB/EDF’s proposal is the same bundled electric service in ComEd’s RTOUPP tariff. CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 3. The primary difference between ComEd’s RTOUPP and CUB/EDF’s proposed TOU offering in this case is that CUB/EDF’s proposed TOU for bundled electric service would be paired with ComEd’s proposed TOU for delivery service. CUB/EDF contend this distinction is not material, as the Commission’s authority to approve ComEd’s proposed TOU for delivery service is not disputed. For the avoidance of doubt, CUB/EDF note, the Commission has already approved ComEd’s RTOUPP tariff stating that TOU for bundled electric service satisfies ComEd’s obligation under Section 16-103(c):

> Bundled electric service provided to retail customers receiving service with time of use pricing satisfies the Company’s requirements to provide bundled electric power and energy service in according with the terms of this Bundled Electric Service subsection.

CUB-EDF Ex. 10.0 at 4 (Ill. C.C. No. 10, 1st Rev’d Sheet No. 28.2).

To reiterate, CUB/EDF maintain they do not propose any new services in its TOU rate for bundled electric service. CUB/EDF note the TOU rates that CUB/EDF propose here would apply to the same bundled electric services in ComEd’s RTOUPP. The bundled electric services under ComEd’s RTOUPP tariffs include:

> direct procurement through PJM-administered markets of all the component services the Company requires to meet retail customer instantaneous electric power and energy requirements at any given time under the Company’s tariffs, applicable tariffs on file with the FERC, and other applicable laws, including, without limitation, all required electric energy, energy to satisfy losses, electric generation capacity, volumetric risk management, transmission services, ancillary transmission services, administrative services, and other necessary services procured by the Company.

CUB/EDF Ex. 10.0 at 4 (Ill. C.C. No. 10, 1st Rev’d Sheet No. 28.2) (under “NATURE OF SERVICE. Bundled Electric Service.”). The bundled electric services provided under RTOUPP are the same bundled electric services provided under ComEd’s Rate BES. CUB/EDF Ex. 9.0 at 1 (Ill. C.C. No. 10). They are also the same bundled electric services provided under ComEd’s Rate BESH. CUB/EDF Ex. 12.0 at 2 (Ill. C.C. No. 10, 1st Rev’d Sheet No. 30).

CUB/EDF argue because their proposal satisfies an obligation under 16-103(c), it cannot simultaneously be a violation of 16-103(e). Moreover, CUB/EDF contend Section 16-103(e) must be read in harmony with other provisions of P.A. 102-0662, several of which authorize the Commission to approve an STOU rate (i.e., for bundled electric services) by incumbent electric utilities. For example, Section 108.18 authorizes the Commission to incentivize “time of use rates for delivery and supply” through performance metrics. 220 ILCS 5/16-108.18(e)(2)(A)(v).

CUB/EDF argue the Commission should not literally extend apparently unintentional phrasing in its BE Plan 1 Order to this case, as suggested by two parties in testimony. _See_ ICEA/RESA Ex. 6.0 at 2; Staff Ex. 8.0 at 4-5. First, the BE case was filed under the EVA, 20 ILCS 627/45. In that context, the Commission found that it was a creature of statute, and limited to its statutory authority, and declined to adopt a whole house time of use rate that went beyond the promotion of electric vehicle adoption. BE Plan 1 Order at 258. The Commission’s reasoning does not apply to this case, where Section 16-105.5 is not limited to electric vehicles, and where the Commission retains general discretionary authority over all issues of rate design.

Second, CUB/EDF point out Staff and ICEA/RESA’s testimony contain no analysis or explanation. To the extent they argue that the Commission cannot order new rate designs for existing services, CUB/EDF argue they are misapplying dicta in the BE Plan 1 Order. Where Section 16-103(e) says the Commission “shall not require an electric utility to offer any tariffed service other than the services required by this Section,” the BE Plan 1 Order replaced the words “tariffed service” with the word “rate” in concluding that “Section 16-103(e) limits the Commissions’ authority to order new rates other than as required in Section 16-103(e).” BE Plan 1 Order at 258. The word “rate” does not appear in Section 16-103(e). The distinction between “services” and “rates” for purposes of the Act was not raised as an issue by any party in the Beneficial Electrification case, it was not explicitly addressed in the BE Plan 1 Order, and it made no difference in the outcome of that case, which turned on the Commission’s authority under the EVA Section 45.

In this case, however, the distinction is material, and failing to recognize the difference between a service and a rate would go against the plain meaning of the law and lead to absurd results.

The Act distinguishes between “services” and “rates.” “Rates” are:

> every individual or joint rate, fare, toll, charge, rental or other compensation of any public utility or any two or more such individual or joint rates, fares, tolls, charges, rental or other compensation of any public utility or any schedule or tariff thereof, and any rule, regulation, charge, practice or contract relating thereto.

220 ILCS 5/3-116. “Services,” in contrast, are:

> used in its broadest and most inclusive sense, and includes not only the use or accommodation afforded consumers or patrons, but also any product or commodity furnished by any public utility and the plant, equipment, apparatus, appliances, property and facilities employed by, or in connection with, any public utility in performing any service or in furnishing any product or commodity and devoted to the purposes in which such public utility is engaged and to use and accommodation of the public.

220 ILCS 5/3-115.

More colloquially, CUB/EDF explain services are provided to a customer, and rates are the prices paid by the customer for that service. CUB/EDF Ex. 2.0 at 3. Section 16-103(e) prohibits new tariffed services, not new rates. As explained above, bundled electric services are nothing new, and CUB/EDF proposes that the tariffed services for bundled electricity here are the same tariffed services under ComEd’s RTOUPP, Rate BES, and Rate BESH tariff sheets. CUB/EDF therefore conclude they propose new rates, not new services. The “Nature of Service” provision under CUB/EDF’s proposal would be the same as in all other bundled electric service tariffs (Rate BES, Rate BESH, RTOUPP); only the “Monthly Charges” (i.e., rates) would be unique. _See_ CUB/EDF Cross Ex. 10.0 at Sheet No. 28.2 through 28.3 (“Nature of Service”); and _id._ at Sheet No. 28.4 through 28.12 (“Monthly Charges”).

In summary, CUB/EDF urge the Commission to reject Staff and ICEA/RESA’s overly restrictive reading of Section 16-103. CUB/EDF contend the Commission has authority to consider and approve CUB/EDF’s TOU rate proposal for bundled electric services.

CUB/EDF argue for the reasons stated above, CUB-EDF’s proposals are within the scope of this proceeding, contrary to Staff’s assertion. The AG concurs with CUB/EDF on this issue. AG IB at 11-12.

CUB/EDF argue both ComEd and Staff misapply Section 16-103(e) and dicta, as explained above. _See_ ComEd IB at 58; Staff IB at 38. CUB/EDF contend both ComEd and Staff misquote Section 16-103(e) to say that “Section 16-103(e) contains a plain limitation on the Commission’s authority to order new rates other than as required in Section 16-103(e).” ComEd IB at 58; Staff IB at 38. CUB/EDF claim the first problem with ComEd’s and Staff’s citation is that Section 16-103(e) does not refer to rates. CUB/EDF point out it prohibits new services, not new rates. _Id._ As explained above, CUB/EDF maintain an electric STOU rate is not a service, but rather simply the pricing formula applied to customers who utilize an existing bundled electric supply service. CUB/EDF IB at 24-29.

CUB/EDF’s second problem with ComEd’s and Staff’s citation is that Section 16-103(e) does not require anything. The services required by Section 16-103 are identified in subsections 16-103(a), (b), and (c). Citizens Util. Bd. v. Ill. Com. Comm’n, 2016 Ill. App. (1st) 152936 ¶ 10, 55 N.E.3d 736 (2016). That case involved a virtual net metering proposal, and therefore raised questions of what constituted a “new service” under subsection 16-103(a), which required “distinct and identifiable” services provided in 1997. _Id._ In contrast, the present case involves “bundled electric service” required under subsection 16-103(c). CUB/EDF IB at 25. CUB/EDF do not propose any new services, only a new rate design for services ComEd is already required to provide under Section 16-103(c).

Third, Staff alleges issues with Part 452 of the Commission’s regulations on Integrated Distribution Companies (“IDC”). Staff IB at 38-39. Staff relies on 83 Ill. Adm. Code 452.240(a), which prohibits IDCs from promoting, advertising, or marketing with regard to the offering of any retail electric supply service. Once again, ICEA/RESA state that they agree.

##### f. ELPC/VS/UCS’ Position

ComEd and Staff interpret Section 16-103(e) as prohibiting ComEd from offering TOU supply rates. ComEd Ex. 4.0 at 7; Staff Ex. 8.0 at 4. ELPC/VS/UCS disagree with their legal conclusions. In a perfect world, the legislature would have made it clearer that ComEd can offer a bundled STOU and DTOU, but when read carefully, the Act does give ComEd authority to offer an STOU rate. First, Section 16-103 outlines the service obligations of electric utilities. Section (a) directs utilities to continue “offering to retail customers each tariffed service that it offered as a distinct and identifiable service” on the date of the 1997 amendatory Act. 220 ILCS 5/16-103(a). Then Section 16-103(b) authorizes the utility to offer real-time pricing under Section 16-107. 220 ILCS 5/16-103(b); 220 ILCS 16-107. While the statute references real time pricing and not supply side TOU rates, the purpose of real time pricing is to send the proper price signals and shift customer behavior. ELPC/VS/UCS note CUB/EDF witness McCann’s compelling point that ComEd has already offered two time variant rates, real time pricing, and RTOUPP. CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 6. STOU is essentially a version of real time pricing, in that the TOU rates reflect supply costs in a comparable way to real time pricing which offers customers rates that vary by the hour.

Importantly, when the Commission approved ComEd’s RTOUPP it interpreted that TOU rate for delivery and supply service is a bundled service consistent with the Act under Section 16-103(c):

> Bundled electric service provided to retail customers receiving service with TOU pricing satisfies the Company’s requirements to provide bundled electric power and energy service in according with the terms of this Bundled Electric Service subsection.

CUB/EDF Cross Ex. 10.0 at 4 (Ill. C.C. No. 10, 1st Rev’d Sheet No. 28.2).

Despite the sections outlined above, ELPC/VS/UCS recognize that in the BE case the Commission rejects the proposed DTOU and STOU stating, “220 ILCS 16-103(e) contains a plain limitation on the Commission’s authority to order new rates other than as required in Section 16-103(e).” BE Plan 1 Order at 258. However, as argued above, TOU for supply and delivery is a different pricing of the same bundled service ComEd offered in 1997 and is simply a variation of real time pricing. While the Commission generally follows precedent, it has the flexibility to revisit its past decisions and make an alternative finding. Docket No. 01-0614, Order on Remand (Phase II) at 1–2 (Nov. 22, 2005) (citing Ill. Bell Tel. Co. v. Wright, No. 02 C 6002, Court Order (N.D. Ill. May 17, 2004)). ELPC/VS/UCS urge the Commission to reconsider its previous interpretation of Section 16-103(e) to clarify that a bundled STOU and DTOU rate is consistent with the Act under Section 16-103(c).

Interestingly, in response to CUB/EDF, ComEd witness Perkins states, “[w]ithout waiving its rights under Section 16-103(e) on what services it will offer, ComEd is open to engage and collaborate with stakeholders outside this proceeding to discuss supply TOU rates.” ComEd Ex. 4.0 at 18. If ComEd is willing to do this in the future, it should fix this problem now.

Because of the success of the RTOUPP, CUB/EDF’s proposed STOU follows its design, as indicated by the proposed adjustments to the DTOU outlined above. Dr. McCann explained research has shown that TOU rates can accomplish 60% to 70% of the load shifting effects of an RTP [real-time pricing rate], and the gap can be closed with the addition of either critical peak pricing or peak time rebates. CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 9. Dr. McCann testified, with higher enrollment rates than a Real Time Pricing rate and little resistance even to being implemented on an opt-out default basis, a TOU rate has a “much higher potential overall impact on reducing ComEd’s Super Peak loads.” _Id._ Under ComEd’s RTOUPP, Super Peak loads decreased 0.10 kilowatts, or 6.7%, while loads increased in Peak and Off-Peak periods, as customers shifted usage from the former to the latter. _Id._ at 11. ComEd found customers in ComEd’s TOU pilot “appear to have reduced peak loads about eight times more than those on the RTP tariff and 75% more even for the most responsive subset.” _Id._ at 9. Dr. McCann also explained that the RTOUPP rate seemed to “offer at least the same level of load reduction benefits with much simpler rate structure for customers to follow.” _Id._ at 11.

CUB/EDF contend ComEd’s analysis of its distribution-only TOU rate proposal cannot match the proven performance of its RTOUPP. ComEd admits that the purpose of a TOU rate is to incentivized customers to shift usage from peak to off-peak periods and lower stress on the grid. Tr. at 78-79. Yet ComEd provides no estimate of how many customers it thinks will end up on ComEd’s proposed new TOU rate. _Id._ at 86. While ComEd is trying to incentivize customers to shift load, it did not forecast the load that might be shifted. _Id._ at 88. ComEd claims it cannot tell how much additional shifting behavior it expects from its distribution-only TOU proposal; it believes “the customer is already shifting their load in response to the supply price signal.” _Id._ at 91. Thus, the Company’s reasoning suggests, the distribution TOU is merely additional savings on top of behavior that already exists. _Id._ Similarly, ComEd did not analyze how much its program could save in grid investments. _Id._ at 93. As for complexity, ComEd’s proposal could result in eight or more time periods, and that, “From customer ease of review, [Ms. Kremer] would agree that the unified would perhaps be easier to remember.” _Id._ at 96-97.

For the above reasons, CUB/EDF’s proposal builds on ComEd’s successful RTOUPP to achieve demonstrable customer load shifts, environmental benefits, and customer savings. CUB/EDF point out that ComEd’s proposal is not backed by any analysis showing any such benefit.

ComEd argues that CUB/EDF’s proposal, which is based on reinstating and expanding access to the RTOUPP “should not be reinstated wholesale without an analysis of the final learnings and an opportunity for the Commission to weigh the evidence on such a design.” ComEd IB at 59. CUB/EDF respond by noting they have already provided the Commission with the analysis of the final learnings of the RTOUPP. ComEd’s own report found RTOUPP had positive effects on shifting load, and customers overwhelmingly saved money on the program. Specifically, under RTOUPP the Super Peak Period loads decreased 0.10 kilowatts, or 6.7%, while loads increased in Peak and Off Peak Periods as customers shifted usage. CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 11. In contrast, the control group on the standard Rate BES increased their peak load contribution by 7.0%. _Id._ at 11. Consequently, the net total reduction was close to 10.4%. _Id._ That translates to an average of 0.26 kilowatts per participant. _Id._ at 11.

Dr. McCann observed the RTOUPP also appears to offer at least the same level of load reduction benefits with a much simpler rate structure than ComEd’s real time pricing. CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 11. He found customers in the RTOUPP appear to have reduced peak loads by about eight times (8X) more than those on the hourly rate BESH, and 75% more even for the most responsive subset. CUB/EDF Ex. 1.0 at 11.

Neither Staff nor ICEA/RESA took a position on CUB/EDF’s proposed design. Staff IB at 38; ICEA/RESA IB at 25.

For the reasons stated above, CUB/EDF request that the Commission adopt their proposal to adopt an STOU design based on ComEd’s successful RTOUPP, which has been shown to lower peak demand and save customers money.

Staff highlights ELPC/VS/UCS make a number of dubious arguments, perhaps the least meritorious of which is its argument regarding public policy. ELPC/VS/UCS IB at 6-7. ELPC/VS/UCS assert that, … on cross[-examination Staff witness Tanya Capellan] fails to explain or support her statement [that “offering innovative supply rates, such as a supply TOU rate, should be left up to the retail market”] in terms of whether that will produce results. (citations) … Ms. Capellan testifies that the ComEd bundled offer is not a barrier to competition. (citations). Then when ELPC, VS, and UCS asked Ms. Capellan, “is it possible that ComEd offering a supply-side TOU rate would encourage competitive suppliers to offer a better supply side TOU rate than ComEd?” Staff objected to the question and Ms. Capellan never answered it. ELPC/VS/UCS IB at 6-7 (citations omitted).

Ms. Capellan’s failure, if it can be characterized as such, to answer the question posed to her regarding whether ComEd’s offering of an STOU rate would encourage competitive suppliers to offer a better supply rate is readily explained by a fact ELPC/VS/UCS conveniently omitted – that Staff counsel’s objection to the question was sustained, on the basis that it sought information outside the scope of the proceeding. Tr. at 196-199. Sustaining an objection constitutes a finding that the question is improper and should not be considered by the trier of fact. Allen v. Sarah Bush Lincoln Health Ctr., 2021 IL App (4th) 200360, ¶213. Accordingly, it was highly improper for ELPC/VS/UCS to even reference this question in its IB, let alone urge the Commission to draw adverse inferences from the fact that Ms. Capellan did not answer it.

ELPC/VS/UCS’s assertion that Ms. Capellan’s opinion is somehow defective because she offered no estimate of how many customers would sign up for (unknown) STOU rates from (unknown) competitive suppliers, subject to (unknown) terms and conditions, is almost equally farcical. While it is true that Ms. Capellan sensibly offered no such estimate, she explained that neither she nor anyone else knowledgeable about such matters would or could offer such an estimate because competitive suppliers have, until recently, been effectively prohibited by Section 16-122 of the Act from obtaining individual customers’ AMI interval data. Tr. at 201, 202-4; _see also_ 220 ILCS 5/16-122 as it existed prior to June 30, 2023. Ms. Capellan stated, with respect to such data, that “I think we're looking at a completely different landscape in 2026, again given that suppliers will barely have access to the data needed in order to develop [TOU] rates come June of 2025, so I think it's premature to even come up with an estimate.” Tr. at 202-3.

Competitive suppliers agree with Ms. Capellan on this point, having long argued that the lack of access to individual customer AMI interval data has prevented them from offering TOU rates. _See, e.g.,_ Rate RTOUPP Final Order at 56 (ICEA states that it “strongly believes greater access to [AMI] interval data would remove one of the primary barriers to new ARES time-variant products.”). Accordingly, Ms. Capellan gave a sensible, circumspect, and well-informed answer to a question that would have required her to offer an estimate of customer behavior in response to as-yet nonexistent TOU offerings from competitive suppliers that are not at issue in this proceeding, especially when the competitive suppliers themselves admit that they lack sufficient information to even formulate such offerings.

ELPC/VS/UCS suggest that a ComEd STOU would “not preclude competitive suppliers from offering a better rate.” ELPC/VS/UCS IB at 6. However, this statement conveniently ignores the fact, discussed above, that competitive suppliers do not yet possess the information necessary to offer an STOU. As Ms. Capellan noted, “ComEd is at a distinct…advantage to offer a TOU rate given…that they hold the customer data, whereas at the present moment supplie[r]s are not able to access that type of data to the point where they can make it useful.” Tr. at 195-196. As noted, Ms. Capellan explained that ARES will not have this data until June 2025. _Id._

ELPC/VS/UCS further suggest that Staff offered no evidence to support its contention that a ComEd STOU would negatively impact competition. ELPC/VS/UCS IB at 7. However, it is self-evident that ComEd would have a distinct competitive advantage so long as it alone has access to the customer data necessary to design an STOU. Moreover, ELPC/VS/UCS offer no evidence that a ComEd STOU would shift customer usage off peak other than a general statement that they “believe” it would. ELPC/VS/UCS IB at 7.

Even if ComEd were to seek authorization to offer an STOU in future proceeding, it should not be allowed to do so until ARES have the information they need to develop STOU rates. Once ARES can develop and offer STOUs, the Commission will be in a position to evaluate the impact of ComEd’s possible entry into the competitive supply market.

Staff IB at 38-39. CUB/EDF contend Staff’s arguments cannot be reconciled with the Commission’s Order in the RTOUPP docket, where the Commission found that a TOU rate with a supply element included lowers risk to residential customers compared to real time pricing, saves customers money, promotes grid resilience, lowers greenhouse gas emissions, positively impacts distribution system investments, provides for flexible rate design, and promotes value, choice and competition. Docket Nos. 18-1725/18-1824 (Consol.), Order at 8.

CUB/EDF offer it is not clear that Staff suggests the Commission should prohibit ComEd from offering an STOU in the future, but to the extent that is Staff’s position, CUB/EDF argue it lacks evidentiary support. Staff’s witness on this issue, Tanya Capellan, is the Director of the ORMD. Tr. at 184. Ms. Capellan testified that she does not believe that ComEd should offer a supply TOU “at this time.” Tr. at 195-196. While ComEd currently holds the customer usage data needed to develop STOU rates, “there was a recent law passed where ComEd will now be required to provide ARES [alternative retail electric suppliers] with that information as of June 2025.” Tr. at 195-196. CUB/EDF point out Ms. Capellan merely testified that an STOU offering from ComEd was “premature” at this point in time. Tr. at 196. CUB/EDF explain that nothing in Ms. Capellan’s testimony or cross-examination disputes that the barrier to entry for ARES to offer competing supply TOU rates will be resolved before ComEd’s STOU offering would become available in 2026.

In conclusion, CUB/EDF support a Commission order requiring ComEd to offer an STOU rate in this docket for residential and small commercial customers. CUB/EDF argue in no case should the Commission’s order prohibit ComEd from offering an STOU rate in future proceedings, because such an order would be based on flawed legal analysis and lacks any evidentiary basis in the record in this case. Therefore, CUB/EDF ask in the alternative that if the Commission were to decline CUB/EDF’s proposal to require an STOU rate in this proceeding, the Commission’s order should direct ComEd to work with stakeholders to develop an STOU rate that the Company would offer in the future.

##### e. ICEA/RESA’s Position

ICEA/RESA contend that while the Commission lacks authority to consider CUB/EDF’s proposal in this docket, the Commission may encounter a future proceeding where the prerequisites under Section 16-103(e) to its authority to approve an STOU rate have been met. ICEA/RESA caution that the Commission should not approve a new STOU rate at all because it will harm the nascent competitive retail market for time-variant supply products and services.

According to ICEA/RESA, the primary reasons that the Commission should not approve a new ComEd STOU rate is because ARES are currently facing substantial but temporary barriers to entry. ICEA/RESA note that Mr. Wright testified:

> I expect that ARES will develop and market time-variant products and services despite substantial costs (collectively $2.15 million, excluding carrying costs) imposed on ARES in ICC Docket No. 23-0773 for access to the basic information that ComEd should have been providing to ARES for many years.

ICEA/RESA Ex. 3.0 at 3. ICEA/RESA note that, in addition, ComEd’s CC&B system “have not worked for at least five months” at the time of ICEA/RESA witness Wright’s direct testimony (_see id._ at 3-4) and the record does not contain evidence that the CC&B system is currently fully functional for ARES TOU products and services.

ICEA/RESA observe that CUB/EDF suggest that because Rider IMUD is expected to be operational in June 2025 that ARES will get a competitive head start and thus a ComEd supply time-of-use rate is appropriate. _See_ CUB/EDF IB at 32. ICEA/RESA respectfully disagree. First, ICEA/RESA argue, given the troubles that ComEd has had with implementing CC&B—which remain ongoing for ARES nearly nine months after the changeover—ICEA and RESA urge the Commission and other stakeholders to not assume the June 2025 deadline is a guarantee. Second, ICEA/RESA continue, because ARES face risk in designing time-of-use products and services, marketing those products and services, and dealing with compliance and contracting issues, the “head start”—even if Rider IMUD is on time—will be negligible with all of the work that must go into new product offers.

##### f. ELPC/VS/UCS’ Position

ComEd’s proposal emphasizes that the “public policy” here is to shift customer electricity usage off peak. Staff opposes ComEd offering an STOU rate because, “allowing ComEd to offer a supply TOU rate would be in direct conflict with the Commission’s mandate to promote retail competition in Illinois and its final Order in Docket Nos. 22-0432/22-0442.” Staff Ex. 8.0 at 3. While promoting competition bears consideration, the Commission has many public policies to consider here including reduction of carbon emissions. The General Assembly specifically cites the need to, “reduce energy usage generally and especially during times of greatest reliance on fossil fuels, and enhance customer engagement opportunities.” 220 ILCS 5/16-105.17(a)(2). Shifting usage off peak directly relates to this goal.

Ms. Capellan testifies that, “offering innovative supply rates, such as an STOU rate, should be left up to the retail market.” Staff Ex. 8.0 at 5. However, in testimony and on cross she fails to explain or support this statement in terms of whether that will produce results. _See_ Staff Ex. 8.0; Tr. at 186-210. Certainly, ComEd offering an STOU rate does not preclude competitive suppliers from offering a better rate—the essence of competition. In fact, ComEd currently provides customers a standard offer of bundled services, and competitive suppliers compete to offer a better rate. Ms. Capellan testifies that the ComEd bundled offer is not a barrier to competition. Tr. at 190. Then when ELPC/VS/UCS asked Ms. Capellan, “is it possible that ComEd offering a supply-side TOU rate would encourage competitive suppliers to offer a better supply side TOU rate than ComEd?” Staff objected to the question and the objection was sustained. Tr. at 199-201. Similarly, when ELPC/VS/UCS asked Ms. Capellan if she had an estimate of how many customers would sign up for TOU supply rates from competitive suppliers, she had no estimate. Tr. at 202-203. The bottom line is that Ms. Capellan fails to support her premise that ComEd offering an STOU hurts the competitive market, or more importantly that it hurts customers.

#### 4. Commission Analysis and Conclusion

The Commission agrees that there are many benefits to an STOU. In the RTOUPP docket, the Commission found that a TOU rate with a supply element lowers risk to residential customers compared to real time pricing, saves customers money, promotes grid resilience, lowers greenhouse gas emissions, positively impacts distribution system investments, provides for flexible rate design, and promotes value, choice, and competition. The Commission acknowledges the arguments pertaining to the Commission’s statutory authority and the lack of sufficient record evidence to formulate such a rate in this case. The Commission recognizes ComEd’s willingness to collaborate with stakeholders to explore the development of an STOU or a bundled TOU rate. Accordingly, the Commission directs ComEd to engage with stakeholders on the potential structure and mechanics of an STOU and a bundled TOU rate. Within 120 days of this Order, ComEd shall submit a compliance filing in this docket that details the issues and topics raised by parties and provides a strawman proposal for both an STOU and a bundled TOU rate. The Commission further encourages ComEd to consider developing and proposing an expanded TOU tariff in the near future as a means to advance the objectives of P.A. 102-0662 and enhance its response to changing grid needs.

## VII. MISCELLANEOUS CHARGES AND ADJUSTMENTS

### A. Kilowatt-based Recovery of Transmission Charges for Rate BESH

ComEd’s Rate BESH is a bundled service rate that is available to ComEd retail customers who are not served by an ARES. REACT Ex 8.0 at 2. PJM bills ComEd for some costs on a demand basis (per kW), but ComEd recovers these costs from customers on a volumetric (per kWh) basis. _Id._ at 3. Initially, the Company wanted to maintain the per kWh recovery mechanism. ComEd Ex. 12.0 at 9. Staff and REACT recommend ComEd bill for Transmission Service for customers on Rider BESH in the same way it is billed by PJM, on a cost per kW basis to encourage consistency in how costs are incurred and how they are recovered. REACT Ex 8.0 at 9; Staff Ex. 6.0 at 12-13. ComEd agrees with Staff and REACT’s recommendation and agrees to make a tariff

filing by January 31, 2026, and subsequently to make program changes to Rate BESH by January 2027. ComEd Ex. 12.0 at 8.

This issue is uncontested. The Commission approves the recommended change to bill for Transmission Service for customers on Rider BESH on a cost per kW basis and ComEd’s proposal to make a tariff filing by January 31, 2026, and implement the changes by January 2027.

### B. Electric Vehicle Charging in the Watt-Hour Delivery Class

Staff recommended ComEd provide an updated ECOSS that displays revenues from EVs in the Watt-Hour Delivery Class. Staff Ex. 5.0 at 25. ComEd opposed Staff’s proposal because modifying the ECOSS would take significant effort but would not change the revenue allocation. ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 22. As an alternative, ComEd proposes providing load profiles related to the kWh and customer count data informally on an annual basis. _Id._ at 23. Staff agrees this is a practical short-term response to the need for better insight into how EV charging customers within the Watt-Hour Delivery Class impact the overall class load. Staff states that providing this data on an annual basis allows for regular updates that can inform future cost allocation decisions and improve the transparency of the ECOSS. Staff Ex. 2.0 at 14. In Staff’s view, while ComEd’s proposal may not completely address the concerns about fully separating EV charging revenues in the ECOSS, it provides a foundation to assess the growth and impact of EV charging customers over time and is a reasonable compromise that allows for ongoing monitoring without requiring immediate and complex modifications to the ECOSS model. Staff further states that it reserves the right to propose alternative methods or improvements to cost allocation in future cases, should the annual load profile data reveal significant impacts from EV charging customers on peak demand or infrastructure needs, to ensure the Commission retains flexibility to adapt to new data and evolving market conditions as the state continues its transition to EVs and clean energy. Staff Ex. 5.0 at 25.

This issue is uncontested. The Commission approves ComEd’s alternative proposal to provide Staff and interested parties, on an informal basis, with annual load profiles for EV charging customers in the Watt-Hour Delivery Class and other customers in the Watt-Hour Delivery Class as part of its annual BE report.

### C. Other Miscellaneous Charges and Adjustments

ComEd proposes to update its Meter Rental Charges (“MRCs”) and Standard Meter Allowances (“SMAs”) that it uses to determine meter lease rental amounts associated with the provision of nonstandard meter installation under Rider ML – Meter Lease (“Rider ML”). ComEd Ex. 2.0 at 71-72. ComEd proposes to update its MRCs and SMAs with updated cost data using the same methodology approved by the Commission in the 2020 RDI. _Id._ at 72. Staff witness Sanders agrees that ComEd’s proposed updates to the MRCs and SMAs are consistent with the methodologies approved by the Commission in the 2020 RDI and no other party commented on this issue. Staff Ex. 2.0 at 14.

ComEd also proposes to update several other miscellaneous charges and adjustments contained in its Schedule of Rates. ComEd Ex. 2.0 at 73. These miscellaneous charges and adjustments were last updated in the 2020 RDI, and they are not standard delivery service charges designed to provide for the recovery of the Rate Year Net Revenue Requirement. _Id._ ComEd presented in direct testimony a table of currently effective and proposed rates, as well as exhibits showing how the updated values were computed. _Id._, Table 12. These proposed rates were all computed using the methodology most recently approved by the Commission in the 2020 RDI. _Id._ In responding to discovery from Staff, ComEd discovered that a value in calculating the Non-AMI Meter Reconnection fee was mistakenly inflated, and ComEd proposes a revised calculation for that fee in rebuttal testimony. _See_ ComEd Ex. 5.0 at 70.

This issue is uncontested. The Commission finds ComEd’s proposal to update the MRCs and SMAs to be reasonable and the updates are approved. The Commission also finds that ComEd’s proposed revised miscellaneous charges and adjustments are reasonable and ComEd’s proposal is approved.
