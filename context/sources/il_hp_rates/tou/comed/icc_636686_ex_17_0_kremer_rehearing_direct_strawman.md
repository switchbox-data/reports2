## II. WHAT COMED RESIDENTIAL CUSTOMERS PAY TODAY

**Q.** What charges appear today on a residential customer’s monthly ComEd bill?

**A.** There are three categories of charges on a residential customer’s ComEd bill: Supply, Delivery, and Taxes & Fees.

- **Supply-Related Charges** include charges for electric power and energy (“energy supply”), capacity, and transmission-related costs paid to PJM Interconnection, LLC (“PJM”), which is the regional transmission operator for the ComEd Zone. Supply is provided by either ComEd or a Retail Electric Supplier (“RES”) and is typically based on the volume of kilowatt-hours (“kWhs”) that a customer uses in a monthly billing period. If a customer elects to have their Supply provided by ComEd, ComEd provides that customer electric power and energy as a direct pass-through cost, without markup.
- **Delivery-Related Charges** are those charges that stem from costs incurred to deliver the electric power and energy (i.e., electric supply), specifically the costs to build and maintain a reliable distribution power grid, along with measuring and billing customers for the power and energy delivered. The charges related to the costs to deliver electric power and energy to customers appear on a customer’s bill in the “Delivery” category and reflect the portion of those costs for which the customer is responsible. [p. 4]
- **Taxes and Fees** are charges related to state requirements or programs that ComEd administers. For example, charges for Renewable Portfolio Standard, Energy Efficiency Programs, Energy Transition Assistance Charge, Taxes, and Franchise Costs can be found in this section of a customer’s ComEd bill.

These three categories are shown within the red boxes on the portion of an illustrative ComEd bill in Figure 1.

**Q.** What types of charges are included in the Supply-related charges category?

**A.** The Supply component for residential customers includes electricity supply charges, capacity charges, transmission-related charges, and PEA charges. I describe these in more detail here and how Rate BEST will apply these charges in the next section of my testimony.

**Q.** What are electricity supply charges?

**A.** Generally speaking, electricity supply charges are based upon the costs of purchasing (or procuring) the energy delivered to ComEd customers.

**Q.** How are the electricity supply charges assessed for a typical residential ComEd customer who takes service under Rate BES?

**A.** Under ComEd’s default fixed-price supply rate (fixed \$/kWh rate), Rate BES – Basic Electric Service (“Rate BES”), costs related to electric supply charges are incurred pursuant to the Commission-approved Illinois Power Agency (“IPA”) procurement plan, and the fixed \$/kWh rate includes the costs for the electric energy, capacity, and other miscellaneous services and procurement costs.

**Q.** How are these electricity supply charges currently assessed to customers under Rate BESH – Basic Electric Service Hourly Pricing (“Rate BESH”)?

**A.** Under ComEd’s hourly rate, Rate BESH, the electricity supply cost and resulting charge vary by hour based upon the ComEd Zone PJM real-time hourly prices, but those prices do not include capacity costs or miscellaneous services and procurement costs. Rather, capacity, miscellaneous services and procurement costs are broken out into separate line [p. 6] items in Rate BESH, as they will be in Rate BEST. Also, like Rate BESH, the electricity supply for Rate BEST will be procured in the PJM market and will be based upon the hourly prices.

<!-- EPON: goal,profile — Rate BEST converts hourly prices into FIXED prices in four daily periods, each with its own seasonal ¢/kWh rate (the aggregation step that creates the "profile" issue) -->

However, to provide more certainty and predictability to customers rather than continuously changing prices like under Rate BESH, the corresponding charges under Rate BEST will be converted into fixed prices in the four periods each day, each with its own seasonal \$/kWh rate.

**Q.** How does ComEd ensure that customers do not overpay for energy supply?

**A.** We use what is called the Purchased Electricity Adjustment, or PEA, which acts as the balancing mechanism to ensure that ComEd’s supply charges match supply costs over time. The PEA can be a charge or a credit that addresses the difference, if any, between the actual amount of money collected from ComEd’s customers for electricity supplied and the cost associated with purchasing the electricity. The supply costs are tracked (and the PEA is adjusted) separately for ComEd’s different supply rates. As a result, the resulting PEAs will differ between Rate BES, Rate BESH, and Rate BEST.

**Q.** What are capacity charges?

<!-- EPON: capacity-mdppec,midday-peak — capacity obligation (PLC) is described as a customer's expected hourly usage on a hot summer afternoon -->

**A.** Capacity charges are assessed on ComEd by PJM – they reflect PJM’s estimate of how much electric generation capacity needs to be in place in the future. PJM secures these resources for the future through the PJM capacity market, called the Reliability Pricing Model, by procuring the appropriate amount of power supply resources needed to meet predicted energy demand in the future by running annual auctions. PJM sets out what is called a capacity requirement for each zone in PJM, including for the “ComEd Zone,” and then PJM conducts an auction, which determines the price for that capacity. PJM then [p. 7] sends an invoice to ComEd and all “load serving entities,” that is, all the entities who serve customers, for their share of that overall capacity obligation. In practical terms, this means all electricity suppliers are required to pay for capacity to cover their customers’ total capacity obligation, also known as a Peak Load Contribution (“PLC”), which I discuss further in Section III.D under Capacity Charges. This charge monetarily represents a customer’s expected level of hourly usage in the afternoon on a hot summer day, when overall electricity usage across the grid is typically highest.

**Q.** What are transmission charges?

<!-- EPON: tmc — transmission charges allocated on Network Service Peak Load (NSPL) contributions -->

**A.** ComEd purchases transmission services from PJM, so these charges are related to the use of PJM’s transmission system, which allows for electricity to be transported across and throughout the PJM system. Most of these costs, similar to capacity costs, are based upon the requirements of the transmission system and allocated based upon the total of customers’ individual obligations as it relates to the entire obligation, known as a Network Service Peak Load (“NSPL”).

**Q.** What category of charges are addressed with ComEd’s Rate BEST proposal?

<!-- EPON: bundling — Rate BEST covers Supply only, but ComEd proposes it be paired with the residential DTOU rate -->

**A.** Rate BEST only includes the Supply-related charges, though I’ll note that ComEd proposes it should be paired with its recently approved residential DTOU rate.

## III. HOW RATE BEST WILL WORK

**Q.** At a high level, what is ComEd’s STOU proposal?

<!-- EPON: bundling,goal,capacity-mdppec,fixed-vs-volumetric — Rate BEST = four daily periods x summer/nonsummer usage prices PLUS customer-specific demand-based (kW) charges; bundled with DTOU for "consistent price signals" -->

**A.** For an STOU, ComEd is proposing what we call Rate BEST – Basic Electric Service Time of Use Pricing. Rate BEST is a TOU supply offering available to residential customers. [p. 8] Rate BEST has usage-based electricity supply prices that vary over four periods daily, and summer and nonsummer periods seasonally, and it has demand-based charges based upon customer-specific demand. When bundled with ComEd’s DTOU rate, enrolled customers will receive easy to understand, consistent price signals.

**Q.** What do you mean by “demand?”

<!-- EPON: capacity-mdppec,tmc,midday-peak — "demand" = customer load coincident with the five summer PJM peak hours (capacity) and five ComEd peak hours (transmission) -->

**A.** Demand is the amount of electricity used by a customer at any given time, like a snapshot. In this context, demand refers to the amount of electricity a customer used that coincides with the five hours of the summer when the overall PJM System demand was highest (PJM Coincident Demand, which is related to capacity charges) and the five hours of the summer when ComEd’s System demand was highest (ComEd Coincident Demand, which is related to transmission charges). These two sets of five coincident demands are used to determine a customer’s contribution to the system load.

**Q.** How will delivery charges work for ComEd customers who sign up for Rate BEST?

<!-- EPON: bundling,stacking — STOU is bundled with residential DTOU; Rate BEST periods deliberately align with DTOU periods; customers CANNOT keep the flat delivery rate and must take DTOU -->

**A.** Rate BEST will be bundled with ComEd’s residential DTOU rate. This means that it will be easy for customers to follow, because Rate BEST time periods have been designed to align with ComEd’s residential DTOU rate time periods. This will provide clear and consistent pricing signals to customers. However, this also means we are proposing that customers electing to take service under Rate BEST are not able to remain on the default, flat \$/kWh rate for the delivery category of their ComEd bill, and instead must take delivery on ComEd’s residential DTOU rate. [p. 9]

**Q.** Has ComEd provided draft Rate BEST tariff sheets?

**A.** Yes, ComEd Exs. 17.01 and 17.02 provide the draft Rate BEST tariff and informational sheets.

### A. Customer Eligibility

**Q.** Who would be eligible to take service under Rate BEST?

<!-- EPON: lockout — eligibility: AMI meter required; legacy full-retail net-metering (Rider POGNM) customers excluded -->

**A.** Any residential ComEd customer with an advanced metering infrastructure (“AMI”) meter who is not taking service under a legacy class of Rider POGNM – Parallel Operation of Retail Customer Generating Facilities with Net Metering (“Rider POGNM”) is eligible to take service under Rate BEST. Net metering (“NM”) customers taking service under one of ComEd’s non-legacy classes of Rider POGNM will be eligible to take service under Rate BEST.

**Q.** Why do customers need to have a smart meter to take service under Rate BEST?

**A.** Rate BEST is designed around four pricing periods. So just like with ComEd’s prior residential time of use pilot, Rate RTOUPP – Residential Time of Use Pricing Pilot (“Rate RTOUPP”), customers need to have an AMI meter installed that is capable of measuring interval usage. The AMI meter will measure the energy delivered to the customer in each of the four Rate BEST pricing periods to determine the customer’s electric supply pricing under Rate BEST, as well as delivery pricing under ComEd’s residential DTOU rate. Residential customers who have enrolled in Rider NAM – Non-AMI Metering (“Rider NAM”) do not have AMI equipment are ineligible for Rate BEST because they do not have the interval usage data necessary for a TOU rate. [p. 10]

**Q.** What are “legacy offerings” under Rider POGNM, and why are customers taking service under such a legacy offering ineligible to take service under Rate BEST?

**A.** Residential net metering customers who interconnected prior to January 1, 2025 are eligible for “full retail rate net metering”, meaning that for any excess generation they send to the grid, they receive 1:1 kWh (or monetary) compensation for all three component of their ComEd bill: Supply, Delivery, and Taxes & Fees. These full retail rate offerings are known as “legacy offerings” as they became unavailable to customers interconnecting on or after January 1, 2025; net metering customers interconnecting on or after January 1, 2025 are only eligible to receive credit for excess generation on the Supply portion of their ComEd bill. Service Classifications NM1, NM2, NM3, and NM4 of Rider POGNM are the classifications for ComEd’s legacy offerings.[^2] The Rate BEST net metering classification will require a new Service Classification. Customers taking service under one of these Rider POGNM legacy offerings are thus limited to the and [sic] Service Classifications that were in place on December 31, 2024.

**Q.** Are non-legacy NM customers eligible to take service under Rate BEST?

**A.** Yes, non-legacy NM customers are eligible to take service under Rate BEST. Non-legacy NM customers who choose to enroll in Rate BEST would receive monetary supply credits based on the amount of excess generation provided monthly within each period. For each period (Morning, Mid-Day Peak, Evening, and Overnight, as applicable) within a billing [p. 11] month, the customer will receive credit for the net amount of kWh provided to the grid at the supply rate applicable to that period.

For example, a non-legacy NM customer on Rate BEST who had total excess generation of 30 kWh in 20 Mid-Day Peak hours of a 720-hour month would see credit for 30 kWh multiplied by the Rate BEST Mid-Day Peak rate for the excess generation sent to the grid.

### B. Time Periods

**Q.** Does Rate BEST use differentiated time periods?

<!-- EPON: midday-peak,profile — four daily blocks x two seasons; costs are calculated and allocated separately to each period and season -->

**A.** Yes. Rate BEST will have both daily and seasonal time periods – that is, the same four blocks of time within each day, but two different prices for each block based on whether it’s summer or nonsummer. To send better price signals to customers, and better align the level of costs associated with the periods in which they occur, ComEd separately calculates, and allocates, over four daily periods (Morning, Mid-Day Peak, Evening, and Overnight), the summer and nonsummer costs.

**Q.** What are the daily periods for Rate BEST?

<!-- EPON: midday-peak,stacking,bundling — Mid-Day Peak = 1 p.m.–7 p.m. every day (weekends/holidays too); identical to the DTOU periods approved in the Jan. 16, 2025 Final Order -->

**A.** Rate BEST uses four daily time periods, shown below in Figure 2[^3]. The periods have consistent pricing for every day of the week, regardless of weekends or holidays. For ease of customer understanding and consistency, Rate BEST is using the same four time periods [p. 12] approved by the Commission for ComEd’s residential DTOU rate. Commonwealth Edison Co., Docket No. 24-0378, Final Order (Jan. 16, 2025) (“Final Order”) at 147-148.

**Figure 2: Daily Time Periods**

| Period       | Start Time | End Time |
| ------------ | ---------- | -------- |
| Morning      | 6:00 AM    | 1:00 PM  |
| Mid-Day Peak | 1:00 PM    | 7:00 PM  |
| Evening      | 7:00 PM    | 9:00 PM  |
| Overnight    | 9:00 PM    | 6:00 AM  |

**Q.** Why does Rate BEST use summer and nonsummer seasonal pricing?

<!-- EPON: midday-peak,profile — summer = June–September, nonsummer = October–May; supply prices seasonally higher in summer -->

**A.** Seasonal pricing provides residential customers with better market price signals. Electricity supply prices exhibit seasonal variations, with prices during the summer period (June - September) averaging higher than those in the nonsummer period (October - May).

**Q.** Does the DTOU rate use seasonal pricing?

<!-- EPON: stacking,bundling — DTOU has no seasonal factor, so bundled customers see higher supply charges in summer but flat-seasonal delivery; same blocks in both rates -->

**A.** No. The distribution charges are reflective of ComEd’s grid investments, which do not change seasonally. Therefore, as approved in the January Order, ComEd’s DTOU rate does not include a seasonal factor. Final Order at 147-148. As a result, customers enrolled in the bundled TOU offering will see slightly higher supply charges in the summer, but their delivery costs will not vary seasonally. But as I said, even though the prices for each block will change seasonally, the same blocks are in place for both seasons, and so customers are ultimately responding to only four sets of pricing signals (rather than eight or more if the periods were not aligned between the TOU offerings). [p. 13]

**Q.** Why not adopt separate weekend and holiday pricing for Rate BEST?

<!-- EPON: midday-peak,goal — no weekend/holiday carve-out: Mid-Day Peak prices apply 7 days a week, all holidays -->

**A.** Fewer, and more consistent, TOU periods will be more likely to drive customer behavior to shift usage to off peak periods. Customers only need to remember these four periods and need not adjust their behavior throughout the week or year. Therefore, ComEd is not proposing separate time periods for weekdays, weekends, and holidays. In addition, a uniform daily TOU rate structure simplifies billing and makes it easier for customers to understand their energy costs. This can also reduce confusion and make it easier for customers to manage their energy usage.

**Q.** Is Rate BEST designed to incentivize customers to change behavior during any of the four daily periods in particular?

### D. Rate BEST Design

**Q.** How did ComEd determine the Electricity Supply Charges applicable to each pricing period in Rate BEST?

**Q.** How does the “Reliability Requirement” relate to a ComEd customer’s capacity obligation?

<!-- EPON: capacity-mdppec,midday-peak — individual capacity obligation (PLC) = customer's share of the five highest PJM hourly loads in the prior June–September; fixed for June–May billing year -->

**A.** A customer’s capacity obligation, on the individual level, is a piece of ComEd’s total reliability requirement. The sum of the individual capacity obligations of all the customers in the ComEd Zone equals the total capacity obligation - the reliability requirement - determined by PJM and assigned to ComEd.

A customer’s individual capacity obligation is a historic look at the customer’s demand that is used to set their future charges. It is primarily based upon the customer’s share of the five highest hourly loads experienced by PJM in the previous June through September period. The customer’s capacity obligation is calculated annually and remains the same for the monthly billing periods starting with June through the following May. [p. 20]

**Q.** How is a customer’s individual capacity obligation determined?

<!-- EPON: capacity-mdppec,midday-peak — PLC set under PJM RPM methodology; peaks "have historically occurred around the same times of day in the summer months" (Fig. 9 = same 2024 PJM table as Fig. 3) -->

**A.** ComEd determines the capacity obligation for a retail customer based on the retail customer’s PLC and PJM's Reliability Pricing Model methodology. See ILL. C. C. No. 10, General Terms and Conditions Sheet No. 127. As shown in Figure 9 below, these peaks have historically occurred around the same times of day in the summer months.

**Q.** How does the customer’s capacity obligation relate to a “demand-based charge” or a “usage-based charge”?

<!-- EPON: capacity-mdppec,fixed-vs-volumetric — defines demand-based (kW snapshot, as in BESH today) vs usage-based (kWh averaged over all customers on the rate, as in BES); Rate BEST uses the demand-based (kW) form -->

**A.** Let me first explain the difference between the two charges: A demand-based charge takes into account the individual customer’s demand for electricity, represented in kW. These charges represent what ComEd must collect directly from that specific customer to pay its bill to PJM, based on the same demand “snapshots” PJM uses when it assesses the charges to ComEd. Customers on Rate BESH, the hourly rate, pay their capacity charge in this manner today.

In contrast, usage-based charges take into account the projected usage of every customer on a specific supply rate, which is measured in kWh. ComEd looks at the [p. 21] projected usage of electricity across all customers on that rate, which is how it is billed by PJM. Customers on Rate BES, the flat supply rate, are billed this way.

<!-- EPON: capacity-mdppec,fixed-vs-volumetric — Rate BEST customers get a kW-based capacity charge fixed for June–May -->

Customers on Rate BEST will receive a demand-based charge for their capacity obligation. The kW amount for a customer’s capacity obligation is calculated annually, as explained above, and remains the same for the monthly billing periods starting with June through the following May.

**Q.** How does this rate design compare to ComEd’s prior residential TOU pilot, Rate RTOUPP?

<!-- EPON: profile,capacity-mdppec,tmc — unlike RTOUPP (3 periods; capacity/transmission averaged into the kWh rate), BEST has 4 periods and breaks capacity and most transmission out as separate demand-based items billed individually rather than averaged across all Rate BEST customers -->

**A.** As was the case under Rate RTOUPP, and is the case for Rate BESH today, ComEd will procure electric energy, capacity, and other miscellaneous services for customers served under Rate BEST directly from PJM. Similar to Rate RTOUPP, Rate BEST will charge customers for monthly electricity usage at a kWh-based electricity supply charge rate that includes seasonal summer/nonsummer pricing and varies over several periods each day: under Rate RTOUPP customers saw three pricing periods and under Rate BEST customers will see four pricing periods. However, unlike Rate RTOUPP, the Rate BEST electricity supply charges will not include capacity or transmission costs in the usage-based \$/kWh rate; capacity and most transmission costs will be broken out as separate, demand-based, line items. These charges will be individually billed to customers based upon the way the charges are billed to ComEd from PJM, rather than averaged on a kWh-basis across all customers taking service under Rate BEST. [p. 22]

**Q.** Why does Rate BEST give customers a demand-based charge rather than a usage-based charge?

<!-- EPON: capacity-mdppec,goal,profile — rationale for kW capacity charge: reward individual load-shifters; under RTOUPP-style averaged kWh recovery, one customer's reduction is diluted across all customers on the rate -->

**A.** By using a demand-based charge, Rate BEST will provide direct incentives to customers who reliably respond to pricing signals to avoid the Mid-Day Peak period. Not only will these customers see reduced kWh electricity supply charges in the near term, but they will also see reduced kW-based demand charges in the long term.

Under the Rate RTOUPP structure, the kWh-based electricity supply charge included capacity, and this capacity component was based on the average capacity obligation for all customers on the rate. A customer who effectively shifted load and reduced their individual capacity obligation actually reduced the total capacity obligation for all customers on the rate, therefore all customers on the rate shared the benefit from a reduced average capacity obligation. In other words, the individual causing the reduction in costs received a diluted reduction in the cost that was passed along to them. Under such a rate structure, some customers will respond consistently, others will not, and the average response will ultimately determine any savings shared by all on the rate. Conversely, Rate BEST will directly reward those customers that work hard to reduce their capacity obligation by reflecting each customer’s individual obligation as a line item on their bills for the entire next delivery year, June through the following May.

**Q.** What can a customer do to reduce their PLC, and thus, their capacity charge?

<!-- EPON: midday-peak,capacity-mdppec — peaks "generally on the hottest days of the year, during the summer months, and between 3 p.m. to 5 p.m." — narrower than the 1–7 p.m. Mid-Day Peak window -->

**A.** A customer can reduce their PLC by reducing their electricity demand during peak hours. Though it is not known in advance which specific day or time will be the PJM or ComEd system peaks, we know that they are generally on the hottest days of the year, during the [p. 23] summer months, and between 3 p.m. to 5 p.m. If a customer is reliably reducing their Mid-day Peak Period load on Rate BEST, then they should see their individual Capacity Charge decrease the following year.

**Q.** How will Rate BEST customers be incentivized to shift or reduce load during the times when the system is most loaded?

<!-- EPON: midday-peak,stacking,capacity-mdppec — incentives: (1) higher summer Mid-Day Peak prices, (2) DTOU pairing gives "even stronger price signals" at the same hours, (3) Capacity Alerts texts/emails the day before expected peak days -->

**A.** There are multiple incentives within the Rate BEST rate design that are designed to encourage customers to shift or reduce their load during system peaks. First, the Mid-Day Peak Period prices will typically be higher during the summer, when the system is experiencing high load. Second, when paired with the DTOU rate, the Mid-Day Peak Period prices have even stronger price signals to “train” customers to change behavior during the hours when the system peaks have historically occurred. Third, customers will be able to sign up for “Capacity Alerts”, just like residential Rate BESH customers can, and Rate RTOUPP customers could. These Capacity Alerts will send a customer a text or email when the next day is expected to be a system peak day so the customer can plan accordingly.

**Q.** How will customers who respond well to Rate BEST’s price signals benefit from a demand-based capacity charge?

<!-- EPON: capacity-mdppec,goal — "fairest option" claim: kW capacity charge reflects the customer's individual impact on ComEd's PJM-assigned capacity cost; no dilution -->

**A.** As described in the comparison of capacity charges under Rate RTOUPP and Rate BEST, customers on Rate BEST who respond consistently when the system is most heavily loaded will see the direct benefit of their reduced capacity obligation as a standalone line item on their bills. Having the kW-based capacity charge is the fairest option to the customer because it is based upon that customer’s individual impact on the overall capacity costs [p. 24] assigned to ComEd by PJM. It is not diluted or averaged out in a usage-charge where the customer does not receive the direct reduction in costs for their performance in the prior summer.

#### 3. Transmission and Miscellaneous Procurement Charges

**Q.** What are Transmission Charges?

<!-- EPON: tmc — definition: NITS, transmission system enhancements and other PJM-associated FERC-tariff expenses -->

**A.** Transmission Charges reflect costs ComEd incurs in procuring certain services from PJM, including but not limited to, Network Integration Transmission Services (“NITS”), transmission system enhancements, and other PJM-associated expenses in accordance with applicable tariffs on file with the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (“FERC”). Essentially, these are costs that ComEd incurs to be part of the PJM transmission system.

**Q.** What are Miscellaneous Procurement Charges?

<!-- EPON: tmc — definition: reactive services, frequency response, black start; costs of buying energy rather than of participating in PJM transmission -->

**A.** Miscellaneous Procurement Charges reflect costs ComEd incurs related to the procurement of electric power and energy directly from PJM-administered markets for retail customers receiving electric service from ComEd including, but not limited to reactive services, frequency response and black start service. These are costs related to the purchasing of energy, rather than costs incurred as a result of ComEd’s participation in the PJM transmission system.

**Q.** How does ComEd propose to recover transmission and miscellaneous procurement costs under Rate BEST?

<!-- EPON: tmc,fixed-vs-volumetric — recovered as Rate BESH / Rider MSS: demand-based for NSPL-driven costs (NSPLC "transmission services obligation"), usage-based (kWh) for costs billed by PJM on usage -->

**A.** ComEd proposes to recover these costs in the manner recently approved by the Commission for Rate BESH and currently used under Rider MSS – Market Settlement Service.[^7] Docket No. 24-0378 [source says "14-0378"], Final Order at 190. Under this approach, the costs will be recovered based upon how they are incurred from PJM (just like Rate BEST’s approach to [p. 31] recovering capacity costs). Some costs will be demand-based and some will be usage-based. For costs billed to ComEd by PJM based on ComEd’s total Network Service Peak Load (“NSPL”), each customer’s relative portion of those costs will be billed based on the customer’s Network Service Peak Load Contribution (“NSPLC”). For simplicity, I will call this the customer’s “transmission services obligation.”

For costs billed by PJM based on each customer’s kWh usage, their related costs will be billed based upon the customer’s monthly kWh usage. The two illustrative charges are provided in Figure 14 below and are computed in ComEd Ex. 17.05.

<!-- EPON: tmc,fixed-vs-volumetric — illustrative TMC rates: \$3.723/kW demand-based and \$0.00067/kWh usage-based -->

| Usage-based Charge (\$/kWh) | \$0.00067/kWh |

<!-- EPON: tmc,midday-peak — transmission obligation = customer's share of ComEd's five highest hourly loads in the previous year; 2024 peaks (Fig. 15) set 2025 obligation -->

**Q.** How is ComEd proposing to charge for the transmission services obligation under Rate BEST?

<!-- EPON: tmc,bill-comparison — "Trans & Misc Component kW Charge": obligation fixed January–December; example 2.20 kW x \$3.723/kW = \$8.19 per month -->

**A.** ComEd Ex. 17.05 [source says "17.06"] provides an illustrative computation of the transmission and miscellaneous procurement charges under Rate BEST. As mentioned above, the demand-based transmission services obligation is based upon the customer’s NSPLC. Under Rate BEST, this obligation will be shown on a customer’s bill as “Trans & Misc Component kW Charge.” A customer’s transmission services obligation under Rate BEST is calculated on an annual basis and remains the same for the monthly billing periods starting with January through the following December. Referring back to the Rate BEST illustrative bill image provided in Figure 6, the exemplar customer’s transmission services obligation was 2.20 kW; when multiplied by the rate of \$3.723/kW, the resulting charge for this customer would be \$8.19 for this monthly billing period.

**Q.** Will customers’ behavior on Rate BEST affect these charges?

<!-- EPON: tmc,midday-peak — lowering demand during ComEd peaks lowers next-year transmission obligation -->

**A.** Yes. In the same manner in which lowering their demand during PJM’s peaks will lower their capacity obligation and reduce their capacity charge, if a customer lowers their demand during ComEd’s peaks, the participant will have a lower transmission services obligation and thus lower transmission charges for the following January through December monthly billing periods. [p. 33]

**Q.** What are the transmission and miscellaneous procurement costs incurred based upon the demand-based transmission obligation?

<!-- EPON: tmc — demand-based (\$/kW) cost items: NITS, Black Start, Reactive and Voltage Control; all incurred on total NSPL of the ComEd Zone -->

**A.** ComEd Ex. 17.05 [source says "17.06"] provides the specific cost items and calculations for the demand-based (\$/kW) transmission and miscellaneous procurement costs under Rate BEST. All these costs are incurred by ComEd based upon the total NSPL for the ComEd Zone. Some of those costs includes the NITS, Black Start Service and Reactive and Voltage Control Services, as described above.

**Q.** What are usage-based transmission and miscellaneous procurement costs incurred under Rate BEST?

<!-- EPON: tmc,fixed-vs-volumetric — usage-based (\$/kWh) items: scheduling, regulation, frequency response, synchronized reserve, admin/legal/working capital; allocated per kWh, possibly shared with BESH by energy -->

**A.** ComEd Ex. 17.05 [source says "17.06"] also provides the specific cost items and calculations for the usage-based (\$/kWh) costs related to transmission and miscellaneous procurement costs under Rate BEST. Note that none of the costs in this category are billed based upon NSPLs; most are PJM market-based services and are billed to ComEd based upon customer usage. Some of those costs includes Scheduling, Regulation, Frequency Response and Synchronized Reserve Services. There are also costs related to the administration, legal, mandated adjustments or other project costs along with cash working capital expense for the procurement of these services assigned on a per-kWh basis, which may be allocated with the corresponding charges in Rate BESH based upon the energy provided under each rate or other equitable methodology. All these costs will be recovered on a customer’s bill with a line item described as “Trans & Misc Component kWh Charge”. Referring back to the Rate BEST illustrative bill image provided in Figure 6, the customer’s transmission and [p. 34] miscellaneous procurement kWh charge was calculated based upon 750 kWhs of monthly usage times the corresponding rate.

**Q.** How do Rate BEST’s recovery methods for the transmission and miscellaneous procurement costs demonstrate alignment with the principles of cost causation?

<!-- EPON: tmc,goal,fixed-vs-volumetric — cost-causation argument: PJM bills NITS as \$/MW on total NSPLCs, so an equivalent \$/kW on each customer's obligation charges their "exact share" -->

**A.** In short, they align with the ways in which ComEd incurs the costs from PJM. For example, with regard to the demand-based component of the charge, when PJM determines the amount owed by ComEd, and therefore ComEd customers, related to Network Integration Transmission Services (NITS), it applies a \$/MW rate to the total of all NSPLCs within the ComEd zone. By applying an equivalent \$/kW rate to an individual customer based upon that specific customer’s transmission services obligation, the customer is charged under Rate BEST based upon their exact share of the total cost. If they successfully reduce their transmission services obligation by responding to Rate BEST price signals, they will directly benefit from their reduction. This is similar to how Rate BEST assesses capacity obligation on an individual basis rather than diluted across all customers on Rate BEST. Also, as is the case for Rate BEST’s treatment of capacity charges, the application of demand-based recovery structure to the demand-based transmission charges will provide customers greater predictability on their monthly bills.

**Q.** Does this approach also align with the goal of having customers respond during the hours when the response is most needed?

<!-- EPON: tmc,midday-peak,goal -->

**A.** Yes. Like capacity obligations, recovering these transmission services obligations based on each customer’s actual individual performance on the highest load peak days will [p. 35] encourage customers to respond and will reward the customers that respond when it is most needed.

**Q.** Did ComEd consider any other recovery methods for the transmission and miscellaneous procurement costs under Rate BEST?

<!-- EPON: tmc,fixed-vs-volumetric — alternative rejected: recover all TMC per kWh as in BESH today and RTOUPP; rejected as less aligned with the Commission's Final Order at 190 (BESH transmission on \$/kW basis) -->

**A.** Yes. We considered recovering all transmission and miscellaneous procurement costs in the same per kWh methodology used for rate BESH today, and which was used for Rate RTOUPP. However, the Rate BEST proposal is more aligned with the Commission’s direction regarding Rate BESH in this docket, and also aligned with the goal of having customers respond during the hours when the response is most needed. See Final Order at 190 (describing the Commission’s finding that transmission services charges for Rate BESH should be recovered on a cost per kW basis to encourage consistency in how costs are incurred and how they are recovered).

#### 4. Purchased Electricity Adjustment

**Q.** What is the purpose of a Purchased Electricity Adjustment?

<!-- EPON: tmc — PEA/TPEA also carries certain transmission expenses, cash working capital and supply administration costs (fn. 8) -->

**A.** At a high level, the purpose of a PEA is to ensure that the expenses that ComEd incurs purchasing electricity equal the revenues recovered from retail customers. Because ComEd has several supply offerings, ComEd has several PEAs, which are in turn part of several riders that ComEd uses to recover the costs of electricity supply from the customers it serves. The PEAs and HPEAs (Hourly PEAs) operate as part of Rider PE and Rate BESH, respectively, so that the expenses ComEd incurs for the direct procurement of full requirements of electric power and energy required by retail customers for which ComEd provides such supply, equal revenues from such retail customers for such supply based on [p. 36] changes in those customers’ actual usage and demands on ComEd’s system.[^8] ComEd has designed the Time of Use PEAs (“TPEAs”) to work the same for Rate BEST.

**Q.** Will ComEd true-up any over- or under-recovery of supply services costs for Rate BEST participants?

<!-- EPON: goal,fixed-vs-volumetric — supply is pass-through with no profit; a separate monthly TPEA factor per rate (BES, BESH, BEST) trues up supply costs vs revenue; uncapped -->

**A.** Yes. Unlike Delivery costs, where ComEd recovers a Commission-approved revenue requirement each year, supply costs incurred by ComEd to procure energy for its customers are passed directly through to customers without any additions or subtractions. In other words, ComEd sees no profit related to Supply costs. A separate TPEA factor will be determined monthly for participants using the same methodology used for the PEA and HPEA for Rate BES and Rate BESH, respectively.

**Q.** Will Rate BEST be part of ComEd’s annual Power Procurement reconciliation proceeding?

**A.** Yes. Like Rate RTOUPP and Rate BESH, ComEd’s overall costs related to its power procurement activities will be subject to reconciliation in ComEd’s annual Power Procurement reconciliation proceeding (e.g. ICC Docket No. 24-0079) to ensure that ComEd’s costs to procure electric power and energy match its collections from customers. ComEd expects to reconcile costs related to administrative activities for procuring power, Information Technology implementation costs, and the costs of the personnel to manage these activities for Rate BEST. [p. 37]

**Q.** Why would ComEd have to revise Rate GAP?

<!-- EPON: lockout,bundling — Rate BEST participants grouped with BESH customers and excluded from municipal-aggregation enrollment because they elected an alternative supply rate -->

**A.** Rate GAP governs the provision of retail customer data to a government authority in order for such government authority to aggregate retail customer electric power and energy requirements in accordance with Section 1-92 of the Illinois Power Act (“IPA Act”). The revisions to Rate GAP are necessary to allow ComEd to group the Rate BEST participants’ data in the same grouping as the residential Rate BESH customers for a municipality to determine which customers are eligible for enrollment with an alternative supplier under the provisions of Rate GAP. The Rate BESH and Rate BEST participants would not be included in the Rate GAP municipal aggregation enrollments since they have elected an alternative supply rate. [p. 38]

**Q.** Why would ComEd have to revise Rider POGNM?

**A.** The revisions to Rider POGNM are required to create the Service Classification under which net metering customers who elect to take service under Rate BEST will be served.
